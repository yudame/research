### Key Points
- Research suggests that sleep durations under 7 hours or over 8 hours, along with irregular patterns, may increase risks of cardiovascular mortality and events, with effects varying by age, sex, and conditions like anxiety or sleep apnea.
- Emerging evidence from 2025 points to potential negative cardiovascular effects from long-term melatonin use, while CPAP therapy shows promise for reducing risks in high-risk obstructive sleep apnea cases, though results are adherence-dependent and not universal.
- Sleep variability detected via wearables is associated with higher odds of sleep apnea and hypertension, highlighting the value of tracking for early intervention, but orthosomnia (obsession with data) can sometimes worsen sleep.
- Practitioners emphasize 7-9 hours of consistent, quality sleep as essential for heart health, advising routines like dark environments and stress management, while acknowledging barriers such as lifestyle demands.
- Social media discussions reveal enthusiasm for wearables in monitoring heart rate and sleep for cardiovascular benefits, but common barriers include late meals, screens, caffeine, and stress, with users sharing mixed experiences on supplements like melatonin.

### Recent Developments
Studies from 2025, such as one in *Scientific Reports*, indicate that prolonged sleep over 8 hours or short, irregular sleep under 7 hours correlates with higher all-cause mortality, with sex-specific differences—men more affected by short irregular sleep and women by long irregular patterns (https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-15828-6). A narrative review in *Current Cardiology Reports* reinforces U-shaped associations between sleep extremes and risks like obesity, diabetes, and hypertension (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12482946/). On sleep apnea, OHSU research suggests circadian disruptions exacerbate overnight vascular risks, implying timed interventions (https://news.ohsu.edu/2025/11/17/study-bodys-circadian-rhythm-may-increase-overnight-cardiovascular-risk-in-people-with-sleep-apnea). Scripps findings link one-hour sleep variability to doubled OSA risk and 71% higher hypertension odds, supporting wearables for detection (https://www.scripps.edu/news-and-events/press-room/2025/20251223-jaiswal-sleep.html). CPAP may reduce CV events in high-risk OSA per ACC analysis (https://www.acc.org/Latest-in-Cardiology/Journal-Scans/2025/08/19/13/42/CPAP-May-Improve-CV-Outcomes). However, long-term melatonin use is tied to 90% higher heart failure risk (https://newsroom.heart.org/news/long-term-use-of-melatonin-supplements-to-support-sleep-may-have-negative-health-effects).

In practice, they caution against over-relying on supplements like melatonin due to emerging risks, favoring behavioral changes (https://www.hopkinsmedicine.org/health/wellness-and-prevention/do-your-heart-a-favor-get-more-sleep).

The intricate relationship between sleep and cardiovascular health has garnered significant attention in recent medical literature, particularly through studies published between December 2024 and December 2025. This comprehensive overview synthesizes epidemiological evidence, mechanistic insights, intervention strategies, practitioner recommendations, and real-world user experiences, drawing from peer-reviewed journals, professional guidelines, and social media discussions. While sleep is a modifiable factor offering substantial preventive potential against heart disease, individual variability, adherence challenges, and emerging risks from common aids like melatonin underscore the need for personalized approaches.

#### Epidemiological Evidence on Sleep Patterns and Cardiovascular Risks
Abnormal sleep—characterized by durations shorter than 7 hours or longer than 8-9 hours, irregularity, poor quality, insomnia, obstructive sleep apnea (OSA), and circadian disruptions—consistently correlates with elevated cardiometabolic risks. A prospective cohort study of 9,641 Korean adults, published in *Scientific Reports* on August 16, 2025, followed participants for a median of 186 months and found that sleep longer than 8 hours increased all-cause mortality risk by 27% (adjusted HR 1.27), while short (<7 hours) and irregular sleep raised it by 28% in combined analysis. Sex differences were notable: men faced higher risks from short irregular sleep (HR 1.38), women from long irregular sleep (HR 1.78 for mortality and 1.82 for major adverse cardiovascular events [MACE]). Age-specific patterns showed short irregular sleep linked to MACE in younger adults (40-49 years) and long regular sleep to mortality in those over 60. No overall MACE association persisted after adjustments, but the findings advocate tailored sleep strategies.

A narrative review in *Current Cardiology Reports*, dated September 26, 2025, reinforces these U-shaped associations, linking short sleep to 48% higher coronary heart disease (CHD) risk and 15% higher stroke risk, and insomnia to 45% increased CVD odds. It highlights short sleep's ties to obesity (e.g., increased weight gain OR 1.32 in the Nurses’ Health Study), type 2 diabetes (30% risk increase for <6 hours), hypertension via sympathetic overactivity, metabolic syndrome (1.5-fold odds with insomnia), and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (OR 1.3 for ≤6 hours). Long naps in older adults may exacerbate these, though evidence is mixed. A 2025 systematic review within this paper confirms the U-shaped sleep-diabetes curve, with extremes elevating risks through insulin resistance and inflammation.

Further, a December 3, 2025, study from Scripps Research in the *Journal of Medical Internet Research* analyzed over 1,000 adults' wearable data, revealing that one-hour night-to-night sleep variability doubled OSA risk and raised hypertension odds by 71%. This underscores wearables' role in detecting patterns like chronotype influences (e.g., night owls at higher risk), potentially enabling early screening for conditions affecting 50 million Americans.

#### Advances in Sleep Apnea and Interventions
OSA, marked by airway obstructions, heightens CV risks through hypoxia and autonomic imbalances. An Oregon Health & Science University study, published November 17, 2025, in the *Journal of the American Heart Association*, found circadian disruptions in OSA impair blood vessel function most around 3 a.m., shifting peak CV event risks to overnight—unlike the morning peaks in the general population. This suggests timing interventions like pre-bed antioxidants.

On treatments, an August 19, 2025, ACC journal scan of a multi-trial analysis (ISAACC, RICCADSA, SAVE; n=3,549) showed CPAP reduced MACE by 3% in high-risk OSA (defined by pulse rate acceleration >9.4 beats/min or hypoxemia >87.1% min/hr), but increased risks in low-risk groups. Adherence is key; CPAP lowers blood pressure (2 mmHg systolic) and HbA1c (0.2-0.3% in T2DM+OSA), though trials like SAVE found no broad CV event reduction. An October 16, 2025, review in *Cureus* notes CPAP improves oxygen levels and intermediate CV markers like blood pressure.

Conversely, a November 3, 2025, AHA study of 130,828 insomnia patients linked long-term melatonin use (≥12 months) to 90% higher heart failure incidence, 3.5 times more hospitalizations, and nearly double all-cause mortality over 5 years. This challenges its "natural" safety, urging caution in sleep interventions.

#### Practitioner Insights and Advice
Sleep medicine doctors and cardiologists, per AHA guidelines, integrate sleep into CV prevention as part of "Life’s Essential 8," recommending 7-9 hours nightly to lower heart disease risks (https://www.heart.org/en/healthy-living/healthy-lifestyle/sleep). They stress optimization via hygiene: consistent schedules, dark/cool rooms (65-68°F), avoiding screens/caffeine late, and limiting alcohol/meals near bed (https://www.sleepfoundation.org/sleep-hygiene/healthy-sleep-tips). For heart patients, untreated OSA screening is urged, with CPAP or weight loss advised. Practitioners like those at Johns Hopkins note sleep as the body's "repair time," warning against supplements without behavioral changes (https://www.hopkinsmedicine.org/health/wellness-and-prevention/do-your-heart-a-favor-get-more-sleep). In discussions, they highlight empathy for barriers like shift work, advocating cognitive-behavioral therapy for insomnia over aids.

On X (formerly Twitter), users share practical insights on sleep tracking and CV health. Others link poor sleep to 48% higher heart disease risk, urging 7.5-9 hours (December 23, 2025, https://x.com/BlasMoros/status/2003496392761765936).

Barriers are prominent: screens/caffeine after noon, late meals/snacks, and stress hinder improvement (December 22, 2025, https://x.com/TXMCtrades/status/2003207359192044030). Orthosomnia from wearables causes harm via data obsession (October 6, 2025, https://x.com/DrAndyGalpin/status/1975175370996384194). Melatonin draws caution; one case tied chronic use to collapse from sleep deprivation, improving only via lifestyle shifts (December 26, 2025, https://x.com/IndianExpress/status/2004452435046420775). Weekend catch-up sleep may cut heart risks by 20% for weekday-deprived individuals (December 5, 2025, https://x.com/ShiningScience/status/1996806744949891204). These anecdotes align with research, showing enthusiasm for trackers but frustration with implementation.

In conclusion, 2025 research solidifies sleep's role in CV health, with calls for integrated interventions balancing technology, behavior, and caution on aids. Future studies should address causality and personalization to bridge gaps between evidence and practice.
