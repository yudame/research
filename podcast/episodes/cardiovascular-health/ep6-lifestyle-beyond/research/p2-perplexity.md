# Comprehensive Analysis of Non-Traditional Lifestyle Factors Affecting Cardiovascular Health in Middle-Aged Adults: A Research Synthesis

This report synthesizes current evidence on non-traditional lifestyle factors influencing cardiovascular health in middle-aged adults, excluding conventional interventions such as exercise, sleep duration, diet composition, and supplementation. Emerging research identifies multiple pathways through which psychological, social, environmental, and behavioral factors significantly impact cardiovascular outcomes independent of traditional risk factors. Key findings reveal that mind-body interventions including meditation and breathing exercises produce measurable reductions in blood pressure and heart rate variability[1][2][4], while social determinants such as loneliness and social isolation demonstrate cardiovascular risk magnitudes comparable to smoking and hypertension[3][6]. Environmental exposures through green space proximity and nature engagement show consistent inverse associations with cardiovascular disease incidence[9][12], alongside psychological constructs such as purpose in life and spiritual health demonstrating independent protective effects[16][61][64]. Occupational stress and circadian disruption from shift work confer elevated cardiovascular risk that warrants clinical attention comparable to traditional risk stratification[14][17][39]. This report evaluates these emerging factors through systematic examination of mechanistic pathways, population-specific effects, quality of evidence, and practical clinical implications for middle-aged adults.

## Mind-Body Interventions: Physiological Effects on Cardiovascular Parameters

### Meditation and Mindfulness-Based Interventions

Research on meditation and mindfulness practices has expanded considerably in recent years, with multiple mechanisms proposed to explain cardiovascular benefits. A comprehensive systematic review of mind-body therapies for cardiometabolic risk in U.S. middle-aged adults examined 14 studies incorporating seven distinct intervention types and found significant improvements across multiple cardiovascular risk domains[1]. Among these interventions, meditation emerged as the most frequently utilized modality, followed by yoga, breathing techniques, mind-body scanning, modified mindfulness-based stress reduction (MBSR), guided imagery, and qigong. The American Heart Association has formally acknowledged meditation as a "reasonable adjunct" to established cardiovascular risk reduction protocols[1]. Notably, systematic reviews have demonstrated the potential of these practices in improving blood glucose levels, reducing waist circumference, and lowering blood pressure while simultaneously alleviating psychosocial stress and improving emotional resilience[1].

Epidemiological trends reveal substantial uptake of these practices, with the National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health documenting a three-fold increase in meditation practices among American adults between 2012 and 2017 alone[1]. This surge reflects both growing consumer interest and accumulating scientific evidence supporting physiological benefits. A recent meta-analysis examining mindfulness-based interventions on blood pressure found significant but heterogeneous effects[4]. The pooled analysis demonstrated a standardized mean difference of -0.78 for systolic blood pressure (p=0.03) and -0.67 for diastolic blood pressure (p=0.03) comparing mindfulness interventions to control conditions[4]. While these effect sizes are modest by pharmacological standards, they approach the magnitude of blood pressure reductions achievable through dietary sodium restriction or weight loss interventions.

A notable randomized controlled trial involving transcendental meditation in high-risk populations revealed significant decreases in systolic blood pressure compared to health education control groups, with a reported 5.4-year hazard risk reduction in myocardial infarction, stroke, and mortality[1][4]. However, the same study noted that within-group reductions in both systolic and diastolic blood pressure among meditation practitioners showed no significant difference compared to control groups in alternative analysis[1], highlighting the importance of rigorous methodological design in interpreting effects.

Mechanistically, mindfulness interventions appear to exert cardiovascular effects through three primary pathways: attention control enabling selective focus on cardiovascular risk behaviors such as smoking and dietary choices; emotion regulation through improved stress response and self-efficacy; and enhanced self-awareness regarding physical sensations and behavioral patterns[4]. Cross-sectional studies have identified a 86% higher likelihood of achieving ideal cardiovascular health among participants with high versus low dispositional mindfulness levels[4]. However, the strength of evidence regarding stress reduction specifically as a mechanism remains limited, with meta-analyses of mindfulness meditation programs showing low-quality evidence for stress reduction compared to non-specific active control groups[4].

### Non-Sleep Deep Rest (NSDR) and Novel Relaxation Techniques

Emerging research has begun exploring non-sleep deep rest (NSDR), a technique distinct from both sleep and traditional meditation that employs guided verbal prompts while maintaining consciousness. A pilot randomized controlled trial in patients with coronary artery disease undergoing cardiac rehabilitation compared NSDR relaxation, virtual reality therapy, and standard Schultz Autogenic Training (SAT)[15][18]. Among 45 patients with established coronary artery disease enrolled during the second phase of rehabilitation, both NSDR relaxation and VR therapy proved effective in reducing Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) total scores, anxiety levels, and Perception of Stress Questionnaire (PSQ) general scores[15]. Notably, the magnitude of improvements in anxiety and emotional tension did not significantly differ between NSDR and VR approaches[15]. However, standard autogenic training proved insufficient for meaningfully improving mental health status in this cardiac population despite its historical use in rehabilitation programs[15]. This finding suggests that traditional relaxation techniques may require supplementation with more engaged cognitive approaches for optimal psychological outcomes in cardiovascular patients.

The study enrolled 45 coronary artery disease patients during the second phase of cardiac rehabilitation, representing a vulnerable population with established disease. Participants in both intervention groups received eight sessions of guided techniques alongside their standard rehabilitation protocol consisting of 20 minutes interval training and 20 minutes general fitness exercises[15]. The utilization of validated psychological outcome measures (HADS and PSQ) provides objective quantification of anxiety and stress reduction benefits. Long-term follow-up studies remain necessary to determine whether these short-term psychological improvements translate to reduced recurrent cardiovascular events or improved medication adherence[15].

## Breathing Exercises: Evidence for Blood Pressure and Autonomic Nervous System Modulation

Breathing exercises represent one of the most accessible and rapidly studied non-traditional cardiovascular interventions. A comprehensive meta-analysis of 15 studies examining breathing exercises and their effects on blood pressure and heart rate identified modest but statistically significant reductions in cardiovascular parameters[2]. The pooled analysis demonstrated a decrease in systolic blood pressure of 7.06 mmHg (95% CI: -10.20 to -3.92, p<0.01) and diastolic blood pressure reduction of 3.43 mmHg (95% CI: -4.89 to -1.97, p<0.01) compared to control conditions[2]. Heart rate also decreased by 2.41 beats per minute (95% CI: -4.53 to -0.30, p=0.03)[2]. While these reductions may appear modest in absolute terms, they carry substantial clinical significance given that a 5 mmHg decrease in blood pressure among hypertensive populations translates to approximately 25% reduction in cardiovascular complications[2].

The meta-analysis incorporated studies published from 2017 through September 2022, establishing contemporary evidence base. A recent meta-analysis by Wu and colleagues indicated that regular yoga and breathing exercises performed approximately three times weekly decreased systolic blood pressure by approximately 11 mmHg and diastolic pressure by 6 mmHg in hypertensive populations[2]. Importantly, the reduction in systolic blood pressure was more pronounced in patients performing breathing exercises (approximately 8 mmHg) compared to those performing no breathing exercise (approximately 2 mmHg), suggesting dose-response relationships[2]. The comparison with complementary findings from randomized controlled trials excluding physical postures demonstrated systolic blood pressure reductions of 6.56 mmHg and diastolic reductions of 3.42 mmHg, contrasted with reductions of 10.43 mmHg systolic and 8.20 mmHg diastolic in trials incorporating physical yoga postures[2].

The physiological mechanisms underlying breathing exercise benefits involve multiple pathways. Slow and deep breathing exercises decrease respiration rate, thereby increasing inhalation and exhalation volume and delivering increased oxygen into the bloodstream[2]. Multiple studies demonstrate that deep breathing exercises promote arteriolar dilatation through parasympathetic nervous system activation while inhibiting sympathetic nervous system activity[2]. This mechanism involves activation of cardiopulmonary mechanoreceptors that ultimately decrease both systolic and diastolic blood pressure in hypertensive populations[2]. Additionally, breathing exercises significantly increase heart rate variability in both pre-hypertensive and hypertensive populations through increased baroreflex sensitivity[2], a marker of improved autonomic nervous system balance and cardiovascular regulation.

A notable limitation in the breathing exercise literature involves heterogeneous intervention protocols and variable durations of practice, which may explain observed heterogeneity in study results. The available evidence base does not provide definitive guidance regarding optimal breathing exercise frequency, duration, or specific techniques for maximum cardiovascular benefit. Additionally, the quality assessment for included studies in systematic reviews has ranged from high to very low quality of evidence, suggesting caution in drawing firm conclusions about absolute effect magnitudes[1].

## Social Determinants of Cardiovascular Health: Loneliness, Social Isolation, and Connection

### Epidemiology and Magnitude of Social Isolation Effects

Research systematically examining loneliness and social isolation has revealed cardiovascular effects of surprising magnitude, comparable to or potentially exceeding conventional risk factors. A systematic review analyzing 13 studies across eight countries encompassing 63,978 participants identified that loneliness and social isolation are significantly associated with adverse cardiovascular disease outcomes including increased mortality, recurrent cardiovascular events, and higher hospitalization rates[3]. The mechanisms linking loneliness to poor cardiovascular outcomes include increased stress reactivity, autonomic dysregulation, and exaggerated inflammatory responses[3]. Lonely individuals demonstrate higher total peripheral resistance in response to psychological stressors, increased resting systolic blood pressure, low cardiac output, and lower heart rate variability in response to stress compared to non-lonely individuals[3].

Quantitatively, several meta-analyses have documented the magnitude of cardiovascular risk attributable to social isolation. One meta-analysis reported a 29% increased risk of coronary heart disease and 32% increased risk of stroke among socially isolated individuals[3]. These effect magnitudes approach those typically associated with smoking, obesity, and hypertension in prospective epidemiological studies[3]. The included studies identified specific cardiovascular outcomes associated with loneliness, encompassing increased risk of mortality, increased risk of stenosed vessels, elevated hostility, depression, major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), and increased hospitalization and readmission rates[3].

### Mechanistic Pathways Linking Social Isolation to Cardiovascular Dysfunction

The biological mechanisms underlying associations between social isolation and cardiovascular disease involve multiple interconnected pathways. Loneliness has been linked to increased hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis activity, leading to elevated cortisol levels, heightened systemic inflammation, and autonomic dysregulation[3]. These factors collectively contribute to endothelial dysfunction, increased arterial stiffness, and higher incidence of cardiovascular mortality[3]. Beyond direct physiological mechanisms, socially isolated individuals exhibit poor health-seeking behaviors including delayed presentation to emergency departments during acute cardiovascular events, suboptimal medication adherence, and reduced participation in cardiac rehabilitation programs[3].

A particularly well-designed population-based cohort study specifically examined social isolation among individuals with chronic kidney disease, a population already at substantially elevated cardiovascular risk[6]. This study of middle-aged and older adults found that both moderate and most severe isolation accelerated the onset of all-cause death and shortened survival time by 14% and 26% respectively[6]. Living alone and lack of social activities demonstrated particularly strong associations, with living alone showing approximately 49.1 and 71.6 days' restricted mean survival time differences within a 10-year window compared to individuals with highest social connection[6]. Notably, the association between social isolation and mortality differed by sex, with isolated men showing shortened survival of approximately 147 days over a 10-year window compared to least isolated men, whereas women showed no significant association[6].

The mediating pathways through which social isolation influences cardiovascular outcomes include both behavioral and physiological mechanisms. The study utilizing directed acyclic graph analysis found that cardiovascular disease and sarcopenia mediated 21.8% (95% CI: 7.1-43.0) of the total effect of social isolation on mortality[6]. This finding indicates that social isolation influences outcomes partly through accelerating cardiovascular disease development and partly through other mechanisms including muscle wasting and reduced functional capacity[6].

### Clinical Implications and Evidence for Interventions

The clinical implications of these findings suggest that social support assessment should become a routine component of cardiovascular risk evaluation[3]. Current guidelines emphasize that targeted interventions including social prescribing, peer support programs, and community-based initiatives should be prioritized in cardiovascular disease management[3]. Digital social connectivity programs and in-person support groups represent emerging intervention modalities warranting further evaluation in cardiovascular populations.

## Environmental and Exposure-Based Factors: Nature Access and Air Quality

### Green and Blue Space Proximity as Cardiovascular Protective Factors

Emerging epidemiological evidence indicates that proximity to urban blue spaces (rivers, shores) and green spaces (parks, forests) associates with favorable cardiovascular outcomes. A large prospective study examining associations between urban blue and green space exposure and coronary artery calcification enrolled 1,365 Black and 1,555 White middle-aged participants from the CARDIA study[9]. Among Black participants, shorter distance to a river (per interquartile range decrease of 1.45 km) associated with lower odds of coronary artery calcification (odds ratio 0.90, 95% CI: 0.84-0.96)[9]. Greater green space cover (per 10 percentage-point increase) similarly associated with lower CAC odds (OR 0.85, 95% CI: 0.75-0.95) in Black participants[9]. While associations were generally weaker among White participants, the protective effects of green space proximity were consistently observed.

A comprehensive systematic review and meta-analysis examining green spaces and all-cause mortality included nine cohort studies comprising more than 8 million individuals across seven countries[12]. The pooled hazard ratio for all-cause mortality per 0.1 incremental unit increase in Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) within a buffer of 500 meters of residence was 0.96 (95% CI: 0.94-0.97)[12]. The meta-analysis identified an exposure-response function demonstrating that protective effects of green space exposure increased progressively with greater surrounding greenness[12]. The mechanistic pathways linking green space exposure to mortality reduction likely involve stress reduction, improved relaxation and restoration, immune function enhancement, and reduced inflammation[12]. Some studies specifically examined psychosomatic stress reduction theory proposing that contact with natural settings shifts individuals with high stress levels toward more positive emotional states[12].

### Forest Bathing and Cardiovascular Physiology

Forest bathing, or Shinrin-yoku in Japanese, has emerged as a specific nature-based intervention with documented cardiovascular benefits. A study examining forest bathing effects on cardiovascular and metabolic parameters in 19 middle-aged male subjects compared day trips to forest parks with urban area walking on two separate occasions[58]. The forest bathing program significantly reduced pulse rate and substantially increased vigor scores while decreasing depression, fatigue, anxiety, and confusion as measured by Profile of Mood States testing[58]. Urinary dopamine levels after forest bathing were significantly lower than following urban area walking, suggesting relaxing effects[58]. Notably, serum adiponectin levels were significantly greater following forest bathing compared to urban area walking[58], indicating potential metabolic pathway involvement.

A more recent study examined effects of forest bathing on blood pressure and heart rate specifically[55]. Research conducted in Japan documented that spending time in forest environments significantly reduced blood pressure compared to urban settings[55]. One notable study by Dr. Qing Li found that individuals participating in forest bathing showed reductions in systolic blood pressure, with calming effects of natural environments helping mitigate stress-related physiological responses[55]. Importantly, the cardiovascular benefits of forest bathing appear to persist beyond the immediate exposure period, with participants demonstrating lower resting heart rates even after leaving forest environments[55].

The benefits appear mediated through activation of parasympathetic nervous system activity through nature exposure. Research by Park and colleagues demonstrated that forest environments significantly reduce heart rate compared to urban settings, contributing to improved autonomic balance[55]. However, these findings remain primarily derived from Japanese populations in specific forest settings, and generalizability to other ecological and cultural contexts requires further investigation.

### Adverse Environmental Exposures: Air Pollution and Environmental Noise

While positive environmental factors show cardiovascular benefits, adverse environmental exposures demonstrate clear cardiovascular harm. Air pollution represents an increasingly recognized independent cardiovascular risk factor. A review synthesizing epidemiological, biomedical, and clinical evidence indicates that ambient particulate matter in air pollution strongly associates with increased cardiovascular disease including myocardial infarction, cardiac arrhythmias, ischemic stroke, vascular dysfunction, hypertension, and atherosclerosis[37]. Meta-analyses examining long-term particulate matter exposure revealed that a 5 μg/m³ increase in estimated annual mean PM2.5 associates with 13% increased risk of coronary events (hazard ratio 1.13, 95% CI: 0.98-1.30)[37]. A 10 μg/m³ increase in annual mean PM10 associates with 12% increased risk of coronary events (HR 1.12, 95% CI: 1.01-1.25)[37]. These associations persisted even below current European limit values for particulate matter exposure[37].

Environmental noise exposure demonstrates similarly concerning cardiovascular associations. A comprehensive review examining noise and cardiovascular disease found that environmental noise contributes to higher prevalence of arterial hypertension, myocardial infarction, and stroke[38]. Meta-analysis of 24 cross-sectional studies on road traffic noise and hypertension reported an odds ratio of 1.07 (95% CI: 1.02-1.12, p<0.05) per 10 dB increase in daytime road traffic noise level[38]. For aircraft noise, a meta-analysis reported odds ratio of 1.13 (95% CI: 1.00-1.28) per 10 dB increase in day-night weighted noise level[38]. Notably, nocturnal noise demonstrates stronger associations with hypertension than daytime exposure, with 14% increased odds per 10 dB increase in nighttime traffic noise[38]. The mechanisms involve both autonomic nervous system dysregulation through sympathetic activation and direct vascular endothelial dysfunction[38].

## Occupational and Circadian Factors in Cardiovascular Risk

### Occupational Stress and Job Strain

Chronic occupational stress represents a substantial but often underappreciated cardiovascular risk factor. A meta-analysis of over 600,000 participants across 27 cohort studies from Europe, the USA, and Japan demonstrated that work stressors including job strain and long working hours associate with moderately elevated risk of incident coronary heart disease and stroke[14]. The excess risk for exposed individuals ranges from 10-40% compared with those free of such stressors[14]. The pooled relative risk of coronary heart disease across prospective studies is 1.34 (95% CI: 1.18-1.51) for employees reporting job strain compared to those free of job strain[14]. An earlier meta-analysis of prospective studies noted a relative risk of 1.39 (95% CI: 1.12-1.72) for individuals working long hours compared to those working standard hours[14].

A recent systematic review specifically examining workplace stressors among healthcare workers, a population experiencing particularly high occupational burden, identified 31 observational studies including 323,978 participants from 17 countries[17]. The key stressors identified were long working hours, night shifts, and high job strain[17]. Most studies reported significant associations between these stressors and increased risks of hypertension, ischemic heart disease, and cardiometabolic disorders[17]. The review concluded that growing evidence suggests strong correlation between workplace stressors and increased cardiovascular disease risk among healthcare workers, with negative consequences extending to patient care quality[17].

The mechanisms through which work stress promotes cardiovascular disease appear multifactorial. In the IPD-Work consortium study, people with job strain showed higher rates of diabetes (odds ratio 1.29; 95% CI: 1.11-1.51), smoking (1.14; 1.08-1.20), physical inactivity (1.34; 1.26-1.41), and elevated Framingham risk scores (1.13; 1.03-1.25), but no robust association with hypertension[14]. This pattern suggests that occupational stress promotes cardiovascular risk partly through behavioral mechanisms including reduced physical activity and increased smoking, alongside direct physiological effects through stress hormone activation.

### Night Shift Work and Circadian Disruption

Night shift work represents a specific occupational exposure with well-documented cardiovascular consequences. A comprehensive meta-analysis of 23 cohort studies encompassing data from over 3.3 million participants found that night shift work significantly increased risk of total cardiovascular disease events (relative risk 1.13, 95% CI: 1.10-1.16) and total cardiovascular disease mortality (RR 1.27, 95% CI: 1.18-1.36)[39]. Dose-response analysis revealed a linear relationship between shift work duration and cardiovascular risk, with each 5-year increment in shift work duration associated with 7% higher risk of cardiovascular disease incidence (RR 1.07, 95% CI: 1.04-1.09) and 4% increased risk of cardiovascular disease mortality (RR 1.05, 95% CI: 1.03-1.06)[39].

The mechanisms linking circadian disruption from shift work to cardiovascular disease involve multiple pathways. Night shift work disrupts synchronization between endogenous circadian clocks and environmental light-dark cycles[39][42]. This circadian misalignment increases sympathetic nervous system activity, elevates blood pressure, and disrupts normal 24-hour rhythmicity of heart rate and blood pressure regulation[42]. Circadian disruption associates with elevated nocturnal heart rate and blood pressure, which increase arrhythmia risk[42]. Sleep deprivation common in shift workers exacerbates cardiovascular pathophysiology through increased sympathetic activity and inflammation[42]. Additionally, insomnia, obstructive sleep apnea syndrome, and non-dipping hypertension patterns worsen outcomes by triggering neurohormonal pathways and abnormal blood pressure fluctuations[42].

A recent observational study examining light exposure at night and cardiovascular disease incidence in UK Biobank participants found that compared with individuals experiencing dark nights (0-50th percentiles of light exposure), those with brightest nights (91st-100th percentiles) demonstrated significantly higher risks of developing coronary artery disease (adjusted hazard ratio 1.32; 95% CI: 1.18-1.46), myocardial infarction (aHR 1.47; 95% CI: 1.26-1.71), heart failure (aHR 1.56; 95% CI: 1.34-1.81), atrial fibrillation (aHR 1.32; 95% CI: 1.18-1.46), and stroke (aHR 1.28; 95% CI: 1.06-1.55)[68]. These associations remained robust after adjusting for physical activity, smoking, alcohol consumption, diet, sleep duration, socioeconomic status, and polygenic risk[68]. Larger-magnitude associations of night light with heart failure risk were observed for females, and larger associations with atrial fibrillation risk for younger individuals[68].

## Temperature Exposure and Cardiovascular Physiology

### Sauna Bathing and Heat Therapy

Passive heating through sauna bathing and hot water immersion has been associated with reduced cardiovascular disease risk in observational studies from Japan and Finland. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials investigating passive heating interventions assessed effects on cardiometabolic risk and vascular health biomarkers[7]. Twenty randomized controlled trials testing interventions including hot water bathing, saunas, hot yoga, and local heating were included, with intervention durations ranging from 2 to 15 weeks[7]. Subgroup analyses indicated significant systolic blood pressure reduction with systemic (whole-body) heating interventions (−4.11 mmHg [−7.36, −0.86]) and in populations with underlying coronary risk or cardiovascular disease (−2.52 mmHg [−4.26, −0.79])[7].

However, the overall conclusion from this meta-analysis indicated that current randomized controlled trial evidence does not support improvements in most cardiometabolic or vascular health markers from passive heating interventions, likely due to moderate randomized trial quality and high heterogeneity between studies[7]. This finding contrasts with mechanistic hypotheses proposing that passive heating might improve cardiac and vascular function and reduce oxidative stress and inflammation[7].

A specific randomized controlled trial examined regular exercise combined with post-exercise sauna bathing compared to exercise alone in participants aged 49±9 years with low physical activity and at least one traditional cardiovascular disease risk factor[10]. Participants randomly assigned to exercise plus 15-minute post-exercise sauna (n=16) or exercise alone (n=15) were followed for eight weeks. The exercise-alone group demonstrated greater improvement in cardiorespiratory fitness (6.2 mL/kg/min; 95% CI: 4.2-8.3) but the combined exercise and sauna group displayed greater improvement in cardiorespiratory fitness than control (2.7 mL/kg/min; 95% CI: 0.2-5.3), lower systolic blood pressure (−8.0 mmHg; 95% CI: −14.6 to −1.4), and lower total cholesterol levels compared to exercise alone[10]. These findings suggest sauna bathing may offer supplementary cardiovascular benefits when combined with exercise, though the mechanisms and practical significance remain incompletely understood.

### Cold Exposure Effects on Cardiovascular Function

Research examining cold exposure effects on cardiovascular function has produced more cautionary findings than heat exposure research. A clinical study evaluating cardiovascular responses to cold exposure in hypertensive and normotensive men exposed participants to 15 minutes of −10°C temperature with 3 m/s wind speed while protected with winter clothing—simulating typical wintertime conditions in subarctic climates[8]. Hypertensive men demonstrated worse cardiovascular responses than normotensive controls, with increased blood pressure and cardiac workload during cold exposure[8]. The hypotheses examined were that cold exposure induces adverse changes in cardiovascular function including higher blood pressure and cardiac workload, and that hypertensive men demonstrate aggravated responses to cold[8].

Additional research on the Wim Hof Method, a multi-component approach combining cold water exposure, breathing exercises, and meditation, found that a 15-day intervention did not produce significant improvements in cardiovascular parameters at rest or during standardized stress testing[5]. In this study of 42 young participants (aged 26.7±5.7 years), no significant time by group interactions emerged for any outcome measures, including blood pressure, heart rate, or heart rate variability[5]. The intervention logs indicated participants performed 13±2 cold shower sessions, 12±3 meditation sessions, and 12±2 breathing exercise sessions throughout the 15-day period[5]. The lack of significant effects contrasts with claims by the method's proponent and suggests cold exposure alone or in combination with breathing and meditation may not produce meaningful cardiovascular benefits in young healthy individuals over short intervention periods.

## Psychological Factors and Personality Traits as Cardiovascular Risk and Protective Factors

### Purpose, Meaning, and Sense of Direction in Life

Emerging evidence identifies sense of purpose in life as an independent risk factor for cardiovascular disease. A meta-analysis of 10 prospective studies examining associations between sense of purpose and mortality observed that people with higher sense of purpose demonstrated reduced risk of all-cause mortality[16][61][64]. Among these studies, five specifically evaluated cardiovascular events (pooled n=124,948; mean follow-up duration 7.3 years; mean age 57-72 years), revealing that relative risk for cardiovascular events among people with higher sense of purpose was 0.83 (95% CI: 0.75-0.92) after adjusting for demographics, conventional cardiovascular risk factors, and psychological distress[16][61][64].

A prospective study following 1,054 middle-aged adults for 9-10 years found that consistently low levels of purpose in life over this extended period associated with higher all-cause mortality risk[16]. A prospective study in the English Longitudinal Study of Aging (ESLA) involving 7,304 older adults (mean age 84 years) found that higher sense of purpose associated with higher likelihood of engaging in moderate to vigorous physical activity more than once weekly and lower likelihood of sedentary behavior over four years after adjusting for sociodemographic covariates, self-rated health status, and depressive symptoms[16].

The mechanistic pathways through which purpose influences cardiovascular health involve three interconnected routes: enhancement of other psychological and social resources that buffer against stress; indirect effects through health behaviors; and direct effects on biological pathways[16][61]. Research examining associations between purpose in life and metabolic syndrome components found marginal associations between higher purpose and fewer metabolic syndrome components (p=0.06), including unhealthy waist circumference, elevated triglycerides, low high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, elevated blood pressure, and elevated fasting glucose[16]. Cross-sectional analysis in older adults revealed that higher purpose in life associated with reduced likelihood of having high-density lipoprotein cholesterol below recommended levels and marginally lower fibrinogen levels[16].

A gender-specific analysis found that in men, having a strong sense of purpose in life substantially reduced mortality risk from stroke (adjusted hazard ratio 0.28; 95% CI: 0.10-0.84), cardiovascular disease (aHR 0.56; 95% CI: 0.28-1.10), and all causes (aHR 0.62; 95% CI: 0.45-0.86) compared to men with low sense of purpose[64]. In women, no significant relationships emerged between sense of purpose and mortality outcomes[64]. This sex-specific finding suggests that purpose may operate through different biological or behavioral pathways in men versus women, though the mechanisms remain incompletely understood.

### Spirituality and Religious Engagement

Religious participation and spiritual engagement demonstrate associations with intermediate/ideal cardiovascular health across multiple indicators. A study examining religiosity and spirituality in African Americans found that higher levels of religiosity/spirituality associated with intermediate or ideal cardiovascular health across multiple Life's Simple 7 (LS7) components[22][50]. Specifically, higher religiosity/spirituality associated with higher likelihood of achieving intermediate or ideal smoking status (either quitting or never smoking) across all religiosity/spirituality domains[22]. This observation suggests religiosity/spirituality as practices may have stronger links to modifiable cardiovascular health behaviors than to biological cardiovascular risk factors[22].

A prospective study examining associations between spiritual health levels and 10-year ACC-AHA cardiovascular disease risk among middle-aged adults found gender-specific effects[19]. In men, compared to those with low spiritual health (first tertile), the logarithm of ACC-AHA risk score was reduced by 0.11 (p=0.004) and 0.18 (p<0.001) for those in second and third tertiles of spiritual health respectively[19]. Similar results were not observed in women[19]. The results may partially reflect higher cigarette smoking prevalence among those reporting lower spiritual health[19]. Spiritual health may improve cardiovascular functions by making parasympathetic activity overcome sympathetic autonomic nervous system dominance[19], and by positively affecting hormonal, immune, and nervous system mechanisms[19].

### Positive Psychological Well-Being, Optimism, and Resilience

Positive psychological well-being encompasses constructs including emotional vitality, optimism, life satisfaction, and sense of control. A prospective study from the Whitehall II cohort of 7,942 middle-aged men and women examined associations between well-being and coronary heart disease[26]. Positive psychological well-being showed threshold effects on cardiovascular outcomes, with relative hazard ratios of 0.74 (95% CI: 0.55-0.98) for emotional vitality and 0.73 (95% CI: 0.54-0.99) for optimism comparing highest to lowest well-being levels[26]. These associations appeared comparable for both genders and remained only weakly attenuated when accounting for depression and other psychological distress measures[26]. Notably, neither health-related behaviors nor biological cardiovascular risk factors fully explained these associations, suggesting direct physiological mechanisms[26].

A prospective examination of optimism and cardiovascular health in a multi-ethnic population using Life's Simple 7 metrics found that among MESA participants (n=3,520), the adjusted risk ratio for ideal or intermediate cardiovascular health comparing participants reporting high or medium optimism to those with lowest optimism was 1.10 (95% CI: 1.04-1.16) and 1.05 (95% CI: 0.99-1.11) respectively[54]. Some evidence emerged for effect modification by neighborhood deprivation and chronic stress[54], suggesting psychosocial risk context influences the magnitude of optimism's cardiovascular protective effects[54].

Meta-analytic evidence from 136,265 individuals found that higher sense of purpose in life associates with reduced risk of incident cardiovascular events[29]. The strongest evidence accumulated for optimism specifically[29], likely reflecting more extensive long-term cohort studies measuring optimism compared to other psychological well-being dimensions[29]. In cardiac patients with established disease, individuals with higher positive psychological well-being demonstrate lower risk of mortality and rehospitalization[29], suggesting psychological factors impact both disease development and disease prognosis[29].

## Interpersonal and Behavioral Factors Supporting Cardiovascular Health

### Gratitude Interventions and Emotional Processes

Emerging evidence suggests that gratitude practices produce measurable improvements in cardiovascular outcomes. A systematic review of 19 randomized controlled trials examining gratitude interventions in 2,951 participants found that gratitude not only promotes mental health and adherence to healthy behaviors but also improves cardiovascular outcomes specifically[27]. Gratitude may positively impact biomarkers of cardiovascular disease risk through multiple mechanisms[27]. In randomized controlled trials, gratitude interventions proved effective in reducing inflammatory markers including interleukin-6 (IL-6), C-reactive protein (CRP), tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), and other pro-inflammatory cytokines[27]. A randomized blinded trial found that circulating inflammatory markers decreased in patients with asymptomatic heart failure following an 8-week gratitude intervention[27].

In an observational cohort study of 1,775 adults, increases in gratitude correlated with improvements in hemoglobin A1c levels[27]. Following acute coronary syndromes, gratitude interventions associated with elevated adherence to health behaviors influencing cardiometabolic health including diet, physical activity, and medication adherence[27]. A pilot randomized study in Stage B heart failure patients (asymptomatic with cardiac structural abnormality) examined whether gratitude journaling improved biomarkers related to heart failure prognosis[30]. Seventy patients were randomized to eight weeks of gratitude journaling or treatment as usual. The gratitude intervention associated with improved trait gratitude scores (F=6.0, p=0.017), reduced inflammatory biomarker index score over time (F=9.7, p=0.004), and increased parasympathetic heart rate variability responses during gratitude journaling tasks (F=4.2, p=0.036)[30]. However, no resting pre- to post-intervention group differences emerged in heart rate variability[30]. These findings suggest gratitude may buffer against cardiovascular risk progression, particularly for patients with asymptomatic structural cardiac disease.

### Forgiveness as Cardiovascular Protective Factor

Limited research has examined forgiveness as a cardiovascular health determinant. A study of 99 normotensive participants (mean age 33.8 years) examined relationships between trait forgiveness and cardiovascular reactivity and recovery during controlled stressors[32]. Although forgiveness showed no association with cardiovascular reactivity during acute stress, higher levels of trait forgiveness predicted lower diastolic blood pressure at baseline (p<0.02) and faster diastolic blood pressure recovery following stress (p<0.003)[32]. Another study of 108 college students (44 males, 64 females) found that trait forgiveness associated with lower blood pressure levels, while state forgiveness showed associations with lower blood pressure, heart rate, and rate pressure product[35]. State forgiveness further associated with diastolic and mean arterial pressure reactivity during acute stress interviews[35]. Increased blood pressure recovery after stress also linked to trait forgiveness[35], suggesting forgiveness may produce beneficial effects by reducing allostatic load associated with betrayal and conflict[35].

### Pet Ownership and Social-Behavioral Mechanisms

Pet ownership, particularly dog ownership, demonstrates consistent inverse associations with cardiovascular mortality. A meta-analysis involving over 3 million participants demonstrated that dog ownership associated with 24% risk reduction for all-cause mortality compared to non-dog ownership (RR 0.76; 95% CI: 0.67-0.86)[44]. When restricted to cardiovascular mortality specifically, dog ownership conferred 31% risk reduction (RR 0.69; 95% CI: 0.67-0.71)[44]. In individuals with prior coronary events, living with a dog associated with even more pronounced risk reduction for all-cause mortality (RR 0.35; 95% CI: 0.17-0.69)[44]. A large prospective nationwide cohort study of 3,432,153 individuals with up to 12 years follow-up found that dog ownership associated with lower risk of death (HR 0.67; 95% CI: 0.65-0.69) and cardiovascular disease death (HR 0.64; 95% CI: 0.59-0.70) in single-person households, and all-person households showed lower mortality (HR 0.89; 95% CI: 0.87-0.91) and cardiovascular death (HR 0.85; 95% CI: 0.81-0.90)[47].

The protective mechanisms likely operate through multiple pathways. Pet ownership alleviates social isolation and depression—both recognized cardiovascular risk factors[47]. Dog ownership motivates physical activity and time spent outdoors, particularly in single-person households where dog owners walked more frequently than individuals in multiple-person households[47]. Pet ownership associates with elevated parasympathetic and diminished sympathetic nervous system activity[47], lower cardiovascular reactivity to stress, and faster recovery of blood pressure following stressful activities[47]. However, observational evidence cannot establish causality, and pets may be adopted preferentially by individuals with particular personality traits or health profiles.

### Volunteering and Prosocial Engagement

Volunteering demonstrates associations with improved cardiovascular biomarkers. An outcome-wide analysis examining volunteering and changes in cardiovascular biomarkers in adults aged 50 and older found that compared with non-volunteers, volunteering more than 200 hours annually associated with lower risk for clinically high diastolic blood pressure[56]. Increased volunteering effort (change from 1-99 hours to greater than 100 hours annually) associated with lower likelihood of clinically high systolic and diastolic blood pressure levels[56]. Sustained high volunteering (>100 hours at both baseline and follow-up) associated with lower diastolic blood pressure[56].

Mechanistically, volunteer activity demonstrates "calming" effects on the cardiovascular system and associates with better cardiovascular outcomes including lower risk of hypertension, lipid dysregulation, chronic inflammation, heart failure, and cardiovascular diseases[56]. Prosocial behaviors in experimental and observational settings show empirical links to reduced systolic and diastolic blood pressure, lower inflammation levels, and reduced cholesterol[56].

Critically, the motives for volunteering appear to substantially influence health outcomes. A prospective study examining mortality risk in older adults found that respondents who volunteered had significantly reduced mortality risk 4 years later (odds ratio 0.52; 95% CI: 0.38-0.71)[59]. However, volunteering for self-oriented reasons produced mortality risk similar to non-volunteers[59]. Only those volunteering for other-oriented reasons—motivated by social connection and altruistic values—demonstrated decreased mortality risk[59]. This finding highlights that the psychological orientation accompanying prosocial behavior substantially influences health consequences[59].

## Cultural and Artistic Engagement as Cardiovascular Protective Factors

### Attendance at Cultural Events and Performance

Longitudinal research spanning 36 years (1982-2017) in a Swedish adult population examined whether cultural participation protects against coronary heart disease[69]. Among 3,296 participants assessed at baseline and three eight-year interval measurement periods, those with highest level of cultural exposure (visiting theatres, museums, cinemas, concerts, sermons) demonstrated 34% risk reduction for coronary heart disease compared to those with lowest cultural exposure[69]. The hazard ratio for highest versus lowest cultural participation was 0.66 (95% CI: 0.50-0.86) using marginal structural Cox models with inverse probability weighting[69].

Another prospective cohort study of 25,420 Swedish adults examined associations between visiting theatre/cinema and attending arts exhibitions with all-cause, cardiovascular, cancer, and other-cause mortality over 8.3 years of follow-up[72]. Not visiting theatre/cinema during the past year associated with higher all-cause and cardiovascular mortality[72]. Not visiting arts exhibitions associated with higher all-cause and other-cause mortality[72]. The combination of having visited neither theatre/cinema nor arts exhibitions associated with higher all-cause, cardiovascular, and other-cause mortality[72]. These associations persisted after adjusting for relevant covariates[72].

The mechanisms linking cultural engagement to cardiovascular protection remain incompletely understood. Proposed pathways include stress reduction, improved resilience, enhanced social connection, increased life satisfaction, reduced anxiety and depression, and improved cognitive function[69][72]. The associations could not be explained by confounding from depression or other psychological distress, and adjustment for health behaviors only marginally altered estimates[69].

### Music, Laughter, and Awe Experiences

Specific musical compositions demonstrate differential effects on cardiovascular parameters. A randomized study of 60 subjects assigned to listen to compositions by Mozart, Strauss, or ABBA for 25 minutes measured serum cortisol, heart rate, and blood pressure before and after listening[45]. Music by Mozart and Strauss markedly lowered systolic blood pressure (−4.7 mmHg and −3.7 mmHg respectively) and diastolic blood pressure (−2.1 mmHg) compared to ABBA music and silence[45]. Mozart's music had the strongest effect on heart rate reduction (mean fall of 5.6±9.8 bpm) compared to Strauss (4.7±9.3 bpm) and ABBA (3.0±7.4 bpm)[45]. Listening to music resulted in notable lowering of serum cortisol concentrations compared to rest alone[45], with more pronounced effects in men particularly for Mozart and Strauss compositions[45].

Laughter demonstrates consistent inverse associations with cardiovascular disease. A cross-sectional study of 20,934 community-dwelling older Japanese adults aged 65 years or older found that the prevalence of heart disease among those who never or almost never laughed was 1.21 times higher (95% CI: 1.03-1.41) than among those who laughed every day[81]. The adjusted prevalence ratio for stroke was 1.60 (95% CI: 1.24-2.06)[81]. The association could not be explained by depressive symptoms, suggesting laughter operates through independent mechanisms[81]. Laughter buffers effects of psychological stress, improves vascular endothelial function, and may function as a form of physical activity[81].

Experiences of awe—defined as the overwhelming feeling of wonder inspired by encounters with vast, complex phenomena in nature, art, spirituality, or human achievement—appear to confer cardiovascular and metabolic benefits[82]. Research demonstrates that awe experiences produce elevated vagal tone, reduced sympathetic activation, increased oxytocin, and reduced inflammation[82]. These physiological changes associate with enhanced mental health including increased optimism, sense of connection, and improved resilience[82]. Studies indicate that awe in nature reduces rumination and stress while elevating well-being[82]. In adolescents from underresourced schools and combat veterans, awe experienced outdoors during recreational activities promoted stress and post-traumatic stress symptom reductions with improvements in well-being[82].

## Additional Behavioral Factors with Cardiovascular Implications

### Anger Frequency and Hostility

Frequent episodes of strong anger demonstrate consistent associations with increased cardiovascular disease risk. In a large cohort of 47,077 middle-aged and elderly individuals, frequent episodes of strong anger associated with increased risk of heart failure (hazard ratio 1.19; 95% CI: 1.04-1.37), atrial fibrillation (HR 1.16; 95% CI: 1.06-1.28), and cardiovascular disease mortality (HR 1.23; 95% CI: 1.09-1.40)[43]. The link between anger frequency and heart failure appeared more pronounced in men and participants with diabetes history[43]. Anger and hostility have linked to elevated cortisol levels, which associates with metabolic syndrome components and adverse cardiovascular outcomes[43].

Hostility in patients with established coronary heart disease demonstrates similar cardiovascular consequences. Among 1,022 outpatients with stable coronary heart disease, those with hostility scores in the highest quartile demonstrated 58% greater risk of secondary cardiovascular events than those in the lowest quartile (HR 1.58; 95% CI: 1.19-2.09; p=0.001)[46]. Much of this association appeared mediated by poor health behaviors including physical inactivity and smoking rather than physiological mechanisms[46].

### Sexual Frequency and Cardiovascular Outcomes

A recent study examining sexual frequency and cardiovascular disease risk revealed non-linear relationships with a "U-shaped" association pattern[63]. Compared to sexual frequency less than 12 times per year, sexual frequencies of 12-51 times per year and 52-103 times per year associated with significantly lower cardiovascular disease incidence and all-cause mortality risk in unadjusted and adjusted models[63]. However, sexual frequency exceeding 103 times per year showed attenuated protective effects[63]. The group with best prognosis demonstrated sexual frequency of 52-103 times per year, corresponding roughly to approximately once weekly[63]. Those with lowest sexual frequency (0 per year, sexual frequency <12 times/year) showed highest cardiovascular disease risk and mortality[63].

### Acupuncture and Traditional Medicine Approaches

A systematic review and meta-analysis of 12 randomized controlled trials examining acupuncture for hypertension in elderly patients with 1,466 total subjects found that both acupuncture alone and acupuncture combined with antihypertensive drugs significantly improved blood pressure reduction efficiency compared with drug-only treatment[67]. Acupuncture alone treatment achieved relative risk of 1.11 (95% CI: 1.03-1.20, p<0.01) compared to drugs-only treatment, while combined acupuncture and drug treatment achieved relative risk of 1.18 (95% CI: 1.06-1.31, p<0.01)[67]. Subgroup analysis indicated that effects persisted across varying treatment frequencies and durations, with more pronounced effects in treatment lasting more than four weeks[67].

### Tai Chi as Alternative Movement Practice

A randomized clinical trial examining Tai Chi versus aerobic exercise for blood pressure reduction in 342 prehypertensive adults found that 12 months of Tai Chi proved more effective than aerobic exercise for reducing systolic blood pressure[80][83]. The mean decrease in office systolic blood pressure was significantly greater in the Tai Chi group compared with aerobic exercise group by 2.40 mmHg (95% CI: −4.39 to −0.41)[80][83]. Additionally, 24-hour ambulatory systolic blood pressure (−2.16 mmHg; 95% CI: −3.84 to −0.47) and nighttime ambulatory systolic blood pressure (−4.08 mmHg; 95% CI: −6.59 to −1.57) showed significantly greater reductions in Tai Chi compared to aerobic exercise[80][83]. Tai Chi may reduce sympathetic excitability by producing relaxing effects, enhancing vagal modulation, and decreasing sympathetic modulation[80][83].

## Depression, Anxiety, and Cardiovascular Outcomes

A meta-analysis and Mendelian randomization analysis of 39 studies encompassing 63,444 cardiovascular disease patients found that 20.8% of cardiovascular disease patients demonstrated depression diagnosis[31][34]. Depressive symptoms linked to increased unadjusted all-cause mortality compared with non-depressed patients (HR 2.095; 95% CI: 1.723-2.546)[31]. Prevalence of depression in coronary artery disease patients was 19.8% (95% CI: 16.0-24.6%) and 24.7% (95% CI: 17.9-34.3%) in heart failure patients[31][34]. Anxiety prevalence in cardiovascular disease patients reached 23.2% (95% CI: 17.3-29.2%)[31][34]. The Mendelian randomization analysis revealed strong evidence of causal relationships for depression and coronary artery disease (OR 1.183; 95% CI: 1.057-1.325, p=0.004), depression and myocardial infarction (OR 1.214; 95% CI: 1.091)[31][34].

## Sex Differences in Cardiovascular Health and Risk Factor Impact

A large prospective cohort study in Ontario, Canada examining sex differences in cardiovascular health status found that with declining cardiovascular health (assessed by eight health behaviors and factors including diet, sleep, physical activity, smoking, body habitus, blood glucose, cholesterol, and blood pressure), absolute cardiovascular disease rates were higher in men[66]. However, the relative impact of poor cardiovascular health on future risk was substantially higher for women than men[66]. Poor cardiovascular health associated with 5.08-fold higher cardiovascular disease rate in women (95% CI: 3.78-6.84) versus 2.54-fold in men (95% CI: 1.89-3.41; p<0.01 for interaction)[66]. This sex-specific effect modification suggests that cardiovascular health promotion strategies may require gender-specific approaches[66].

## Inflammatory Biomarkers as Mechanisms and Outcomes

Multiple studies have established inflammatory biomarkers as predictive markers and mechanistic mediators of cardiovascular outcomes. A meta-analysis examining associations between fibrinogen (FIB), interleukin-6 (IL-6), C-reactive protein (CRP), and galectin-3 (Gal-3) and cardiovascular disease risk found that high levels of each marker predicted cardiovascular disease development[60]. IL-6 demonstrated the strongest association (HR 1.16; 95% CI: 1.10-1.22, p<0.001), followed by CRP (OR 1.25; 95% CI: 1.15-1.35, p<0.001)[60]. The IL-6 cascade response generating CRP may explain similar predictive ability of both markers[60]. Midlife levels of inflammatory markers including intracellular adhesion molecule-1 (ICAM-1) proved particularly prognostic, with ICAM-1 identified as a dual marker associated with both atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease and cognitive decline[57].

## Synthesis and Implications

The convergence of evidence from diverse research domains identifies multiple non-traditional lifestyle factors that significantly influence cardiovascular outcomes in middle-aged adults independent of exercise, sleep duration, diet composition, and supplementation. Mind-body interventions including meditation, mindfulness, breathing exercises, and novel techniques such as non-sleep deep rest produce measurable but modest reductions in blood pressure and heart rate through parasympathetic nervous system activation and stress reduction mechanisms[1][2][4][15]. Social determinants—particularly loneliness and social isolation—demonstrate cardiovascular risk magnitudes comparable to or exceeding conventional factors[3][6], with biological mechanisms involving HPA axis dysregulation and inflammation[3]. Environmental factors including green space proximity and air quality represent modifiable social determinants with documented cardiovascular consequences[9][12][37][38]. Psychological constructs including purpose in life, spiritual engagement, and positive well-being show independent protective effects not fully explained by absence of depression[16][26][54][61][64]. Occupational stress and circadian disruption from shift work and nighttime light exposure confer substantial cardiovascular risk requiring clinical attention[14][17][39][68]. Behavioral factors including gratitude practice, pet ownership, volunteering with other-oriented motivation, and cultural engagement demonstrate cardiovascular benefits, while anger frequency and hostility show harmful associations[27][43][44][56][69].

## Conclusion

Research systematically examining non-traditional cardiovascular health factors has identified multiple modifiable determinants affecting middle-aged adults that warrant clinical integration alongside conventional risk factor management. The evidence quality ranges from well-replicated findings across multiple large prospective cohorts (social isolation, occupational stress, depression, environmental exposures) to preliminary research based on small intervention trials (NSDR, gratitude journaling, specific meditation practices). Healthcare providers should incorporate assessment of social connectedness, occupational stress, psychological well-being, access to natural environments, and psychological factors into comprehensive cardiovascular risk evaluation. Future research should prioritize large-scale randomized controlled trials with long-term follow-up examining causality and cost-effectiveness of targeted interventions addressing these emerging risk and protective factors. Sex-specific and population-specific investigation is warranted given evidence that effect magnitudes vary by demographic factors. Given the substantial and growing body of evidence documenting cardiovascular effects of non-traditional factors, clinical guidelines appear outdated in limiting cardiovascular prevention discussion to conventional exercise, diet, smoking cessation, and medication approaches. Comprehensive cardiovascular health promotion requires addressing the psychosocial, occupational, environmental, and behavioral domains documented in this synthesis.
