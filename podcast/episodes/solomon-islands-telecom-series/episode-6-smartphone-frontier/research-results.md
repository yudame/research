## Comprehensive Research Report: Smartphone Adoption and Device Provisioning Strategies in Emerging Markets

This extensive research report synthesizes 86 sources comprising peer-reviewed studies, meta-analyses, randomized controlled trials, government reports, market research, and development organization publications to provide an evidence-based analysis of smartphone adoption and device provisioning in emerging markets with specific focus on Pacific island nations like Solomon Islands.

**Key Findings:**

### Multidimensional Barriers Beyond Cost

Device affordability, while significant, represents only one layer of adoption barriers. The research identifies five co-equal constraints limiting smartphone penetration:

**Affordability barriers** manifest differently across populations. For the poorest segments in developing economies, entry-level smartphones consume 30% of monthly income and represent 24% of women's monthly income versus 12% for men. In island archipelagos like the Philippines, device delivery costs reach five times those of coastal nations like Nigeria. However, affordability as standalone explanation fails to account for feature phone persistence in markets where smartphones are technically available and increasingly affordable.[1][2][3][4]

**Infrastructure gaps** create paradoxical "coverage-usage" divides. The Pacific region achieves 86% mobile broadband population coverage yet only 27% actual usage, generating 59% usage gap—highest globally except Sub-Saharan Africa. Solomon Islands specifically shows only 25% 4G coverage (2020), with data costs reaching US$170/month (38% average monthly income), while internet access extends to only 15-20% of population despite 60-70% mobile ownership.[5][6][7][8]

**Digital literacy deficits** operate independently of infrastructure. UNESCO reports over 40% of developing country populations lack basic digital skills. Solomon Islands' 42% digital literacy score reflects this gap. Roessler et al.'s Malawi RCT found smartphone uptake "sensitive to participant literacy levels," indicating basic literacy as prerequisite for smartphone utility distinct from device access.[9][10]

**Gender-based inequalities** create systematic exclusion. Sub-Saharan Africa's smartphone gender gap narrowed to 25% (2024) from 28% (2023), yet 265 million fewer women than men use mobile internet across low- and middle-income countries. Entry-level handset costs consume double the percentage of women's versus men's income. Cultural attitudes in South Asia restrict women's ownership through beliefs that phones reduce chastity or family focus.[11][3][12]

**Content and language barriers** reduce perceived smartphone utility. Locally relevant digital content in indigenous languages remains scarce across Pacific island nations, limiting practical application for non-English speakers. In rural Pacific communities, government services, educational resources, and market information availability in local languages remains minimal.[6][13]

### Device Provisioning Model Evidence

**Pay-As-You-Go (PAYGO) financing** demonstrates superiority for unbanked populations. World Bank analysis identifies PAYGO as "generally most suitable for low-income individuals" because it operates without credit checks (relying on device-locking technology), addresses three of five credit supply determinants, and serves populations with irregular income streams—characteristic of Solomon Islands' subsistence-based rural economy (75% rural population).[14][6]

**Buy-Now-Pay-Later (BNPL) models** require established banking infrastructure. Safaricom's Lipa Mdogo Mdogo (Kenya) leverages M-Pesa's network for repayment processing, while Vodacom's Easy2Own (South Africa) mandates bank account deductions—only feasible where banking penetration reaches 89%. Applicability to Solomon Islands (75% unbanked population) remains limited.[15]

**Government subsidy programs** show temporary demand generation with sustainability limitations. China's 2025 trade-in program provided 15% subsidies (capped CNY500/~USD71) on devices below CNY6,000 (~USD850). Initial results (January 20, 2025) showed 3-5x sales surge, with 48 million participants purchasing 51.48 million devices generating USD$19.9 billion by May. However, critical finding: by week 11 post-launch, cumulative smartphone sales declined 5% year-over-year despite ongoing subsidies. Subsidy failure mechanisms included consumer hesitation regarding new model assessments and macroeconomic uncertainty suppressing demand independent of financial incentives.[16][17][18]

### Mobile Money-Smartphone Adoption Nexus

Khan et al.'s 2025 meta-analysis demonstrates mobile money's direct effect on financial inclusion through informal enterprise profitability and trade credit channels. This creates virtuous cycle: smartphone access enables mobile money adoption, improving business viability and strengthening ability to sustain device payments. Solomon Islands' early 2023 mobile money launch via Our Telekom addresses critical financial inclusion gap (75% unbanked), though 42% digital literacy creates implementation challenges.[19][6]

### Critical Research Gaps

Virtually no peer-reviewed RCTs examine smartphone adoption specifically in Pacific island contexts. Existing evidence derives from Blumenstock (Uganda 2020), Roessler et al. (Malawi 2023), and India-focused analyses, potentially missing island-specific infrastructure, cultural, and economic dynamics. Roessler et al.'s Malawi finding of substantial phone attrition between distribution and follow-up, combined with mixed income effects (null monthly income despite weekly gains), raises questions regarding long-term sustainability in island contexts with extreme economic vulnerability.[10][20][21][22]

### Key Points on Smartphone Adoption and Device Provisioning in the Solomon Islands and Pacific Islands
- Research suggests smartphone penetration in the Solomon Islands has reached around 42% as of early 2025, with gradual growth driven by improving connectivity, though barriers like affordability and rural infrastructure persist.
- Mobile money services like IumiCash show low adoption (10-15% active users as of mid-2023), and while they may encourage smartphone use for advanced features, evidence of direct demand boost is limited.
- Infrastructure deployments such as Starlink (licensed in 2024) appear likely to enhance smartphone viability by improving internet access, but quantifiable impacts on adoption remain emerging.
- Device financing programs are scarce in the Solomon Islands, with broader Pacific examples focusing on general financial inclusion rather than operator-specific subsidies; perspectives highlight affordability as a key barrier.
- Entry-level smartphones in Solomon Islands retail for approximately SBD 750–1,500 (about $90–180 USD), with Chinese brands like Tecno and Infinix gaining popularity for budget options.

### Recent Developments
Evidence leans toward modest progress in smartphone adoption, supported by infrastructure improvements but hindered by economic factors. For instance, Starlink's rollout since 2024 has begun transforming rural connectivity, potentially making smartphones more practical for daily use. Mobile money like IumiCash, while innovative, has not yet significantly driven device demand due to low activity rates.

### Practitioner Perspectives
Stakeholders, including telcos and development organizations, emphasize affordability and literacy as major hurdles, recommending financing models and partnerships to expand access. GSMA reports highlight investment gaps, suggesting reforms to improve returns and reduce costs. Regional telcos like Digicel focus on coverage expansion, which could inform strategies in the Solomon Islands.

### Market Reality
Affordable brands dominate, with refurbished and Chinese models like Tecno Spark series offering value under SBD 1,500. Local retail listings on platforms like Facebook indicate strong demand for budget devices amid economic constraints.

The Solomon Islands and broader Pacific island nations, including Papua New Guinea (PNG), Fiji, and Vanuatu, face unique challenges in smartphone adoption and device provisioning due to geographic isolation, economic disparities, and infrastructure limitations. As of early 2025, smartphone penetration in the Solomon Islands stands at approximately 42.5% of the population, with around 352,000 internet users out of roughly 800,000 people, reflecting a slight increase from 336,000 in early 2024. This growth aligns with broader Pacific trends, where mobile connectivity is expanding but remains uneven, with 5G adoption under 1% regionally as of late 2024. Key drivers include satellite internet advancements like Starlink and fiber deployments, while barriers such as high costs, low digital literacy, and cash-dominant economies persist. Mobile money services, intended to boost financial inclusion, show mixed results in spurring device demand. Practitioner insights from telcos, providers, and international organizations underscore the need for targeted financing and policy reforms. Market data reveals a preference for affordable Chinese brands, with entry-level prices ranging from SBD 750 to 1,500 in local retail.

#### Adoption and Usage of Our Telekom's IumiCash Mobile Money Service
IumiCash, a mobile wallet service in the Solomon Islands, was launched in January 2021 as a foreign-owned provider, separate from Our Telekom's M-Selen (launched June 2023). As of July 2023, there were about 13,400 mobile wallet accounts across providers like IumiCash, Ezi Pei, and M-Selen, but only 10-15% were active, indicating low adoption. Usage is limited by rural infrastructure gaps, lack of interoperability between services, and heavy reliance on cash, which accounted for 80% of transactions in 2020. Services include cash-in/out, P2P transfers, top-ups, and QR payments (IumiCash-specific, requiring smartphones).

Efforts to boost adoption include training programs through the OASIS Data Garden Project, a collaboration between SATSOL and IumiCash Solomon Islands Ltd. In 2023, workshops targeted school staff, canteen operators, and communities, with role-playing for transactions. By July 2024's commercial launch, around 100 SIM cards were registered, with top-ups monitored via IumiCash. Surveys showed high smartphone ownership but slow digital money uptake due to cash preferences and trust issues. Women comprised at least 30% of sign-ups, highlighting some inclusivity, but overall penetration remains low amid 34% formal financial access.

Has it driven smartphone demand? Evidence is limited and indirect. Full IumiCash features like QR payments require smartphones (42% of mobile users have them), potentially encouraging upgrades, but low activity suggests minimal impact. GSMA notes mobile money's potential for inclusion but warns of barriers like connectivity and ID issues. No 2024-2025 statistics show a clear causal link to increased smartphone sales.

#### Impact of SATSOL Fiber and Starlink Infrastructure (2023-2024)
SATSOL, a local ISP, has partnered on fiber and satellite projects to address connectivity gaps. Starlink Solomon Islands Limited received a class license in September 2024 from the Telecommunications Commission Solomon Islands (TCSI), following a two-year process and MoU with SpaceX. This reactivated services for existing users and positioned Starlink as an alternative to costly local options from Our Telekom and Bmobile. SATSOL resells Starlink for corporate and public sectors, with deployments in remote areas like Aleang village in the Shortland Islands, enabling first-time access to online services.

Impacts include improved emergency communications, telemedicine, and economic activities. In rural Fiji and Solomon Islands, Starlink has enhanced school connectivity for online resources. On smartphone viability, better internet (high-speed, reliable) likely boosts adoption by making data-intensive apps feasible, countering previous slow/unreliable services. However, Starlink's performance varies (e.g., not always meeting 100 Mbps goals), and affordability remains a concern. Regional reports note competition from Starlink has reduced revenues for incumbents like Vodafone in the Cook Islands by 12% in 2024. No direct 2024-2025 data ties this to smartphone uptake spikes, but GSMA forecasts gradual growth in mobile internet to bridge gaps by 2030.

#### Device Provisioning and Financing Programs in Pacific Nations
Programs are limited and focus on broader inclusion. In Solomon Islands, PNG, Fiji, and Vanuatu, no widespread operator-led device subsidies exist as of 2024-2025. The 2025 Money Pacific Goals emphasize digitalization for recovery, but not specific financing. USAID's Digital Ecosystem Assessment covers 12 countries, noting low smartphone access in rural areas. Government initiatives include ADB/World Bank co-financing for Pacific projects, but these target infrastructure, not devices.

In Vanuatu, Digicel committed to 99% mobile coverage under Universal Access Policy in 2025, indirectly supporting device use. GSMA advocates smartphone financing as a mobile money catalyst, but no Solomon-specific programs.

Our Telekom offers no explicit device financing or subsidies in 2024-2025; focus is on M-Selen and data plans. Bmobile/Vodafone: Partnership ended in 2019; no recent subsidies, though Bmobile explored satellite deals.

#### Perspectives from Telcos, Providers, and Retailers
In Solomon Islands and Pacific markets, barriers include affordability (e.g., lipa polepole schemes for 60% of sales indicate low cash flow), infrastructure deficits, and digital literacy. Retailers note slow adoption due to cash economies and trust issues. Telcos like Our Telekom emphasize digitizing engagement.

Regional telcos: Digicel invests in LTE (80%+ coverage in PNG), Vodafone relaunches top-ups.  These could inform Solomon by prioritizing mass-market prepaid models.

Development organizations: GSMA recommends reforms to unlock investment and affordability. World Bank/ADB fund digital programs (e.g., $374M to Vanuatu/Fiji/Samoa 2018-2022). Pacific Islands Forum aids trade/digital strategies.

#### Entry-Level Smartphone Costs in Solomon Islands
Local retail (e.g., Facebook groups) shows entry-level prices: Tecno Spark 20c SBD 1,500, Samsung A03 Core SBD 900, Samsung A20s SBD 1,200.  Brand new budget models like Realme C21Y around SBD 2,100. Prices fluctuate; Samsung Galaxy A06 5G (128GB) ~SBD 2,000+.

#### Popular Brands and Models at Affordable Points
Chinese brands like Tecno and Infinix dominate budget segments in Pacific markets, praised for value (e.g., Infinix Smart 10 Plus SBD ~4,800, Tecno Spark Go 2 SBD 742).  Refurbished options (e.g., iPhone 8 Plus SBD 1,500) are common. Videos highlight Tecno Spark series and Infinix models as top 2024 budgets.

# Comprehensive Analysis of Smartphone Access Policy and Regulatory Frameworks in the Solomon Islands and Pacific Region

## Executive Summary

The proliferation of mobile telephony in the Pacific Islands has been a catalyst for economic development, yet the transition from basic connectivity to meaningful digital inclusion—defined by the adoption of internet-enabled smartphones—remains hampered by structural, regulatory, and economic barriers. This report provides an exhaustive analysis of the policy and regulatory frameworks governing smartphone access in the Solomon Islands, juxtaposed against comparative case studies from Papua New Guinea (PNG), Fiji, Vanuatu, and Samoa.

The evidence suggests that while the Solomon Islands has established foundational frameworks through its *National ICT Policy 2017* and *National Financial Inclusion Strategy 3 (NFIS3)*, the specific regulatory mechanisms required to democratize device ownership—such as targeted Universal Service Obligation (USO) disbursements for hardware, reduced import duties, and robust consumer credit protections—are either nascent or underutilized compared to regional peers like Vanuatu and Fiji.

Key findings indicate that the Solomon Islands lacks Mobile Number Portability (MNP), a regulatory gap that, when combined with device financing, risks entrenching platform lock-in and reducing market competition. Furthermore, while the *Secured Transactions Act 2008* provides a legal basis for using movable assets as collateral, the practical application of this for consumer device financing is limited by a lack of specific consumer credit protection regarding repossession of essential digital tools. The gender digital divide remains a critical policy concern, with a 25% gap in smartphone ownership often cited, necessitating intersectional policy responses that align financial inclusion targets with ICT infrastructure rollout.

## 1. Regulatory & Policy Frameworks in the Solomon Islands

### 1.1 National Digital Inclusion Strategies and ICT Policies

The Solomon Islands Government (SIG) has articulated its digital ambitions through two primary documents: the *National ICT Policy* and the *National Financial Inclusion Strategy*. These documents shift the focus from pure infrastructure deployment (towers and cables) toward "meaningful connectivity," where device access is a prerequisite for participation in the digital economy.

#### 1.1.1 National ICT Policy 2017
The *Solomon Islands National ICT Policy*, adopted in 2017, serves as the overarching framework. Its vision is "a peaceful, united and progressive Solomon Islands communicating and informed by technologies open to all" [cite: 1]. The policy explicitly identifies "ICT for Equity" as a core principle, mandating the promotion of access to further gender equality and inclusive development [cite: 2].

While the policy sets high-level goals for availability and affordability, it lacks specific, quantified targets for smartphone penetration rates. Instead, it focuses on the enabling environment:
*   **Infrastructure:** Leveraging the submarine cable (completed in 2019) to lower wholesale costs.
*   **Liberalization:** Maintaining a competitive market between operators (Our Telekom and bmobile/Vodafone).
*   **Objectives:** The policy outlines nine objectives, including "Accessible ICT" and "ICT for Business," but stops short of mandating state-funded device subsidies, relying instead on market forces to drive down costs [cite: 1].

#### 1.1.2 National Financial Inclusion Strategy 3 (NFIS3) 2021–2025
More concrete targets regarding device access are found in the financial sector's strategic planning. The *National Financial Inclusion Strategy 3 (NFIS3)*, launched by the Central Bank of Solomon Islands (CBSI), explicitly links smartphone access to financial resilience.

*   **Official Targets:** The strategy sets a goal to increase active users of financial accounts from 283,954 to **400,000** by 2025, with a specific mandate that **50% of these must be women** [cite: 3, 4].
*   **Strategic Context:** The strategy recognizes that the country's low population density and archipelagic geography make brick-and-mortar banking unviable. Therefore, Digital Financial Services (DFS) are the primary vehicle for inclusion. Since DFS requires a mobile interface, smartphone accessibility is implicitly elevated to a national strategic priority [cite: 5].
*   **Implementation:** The strategy is supported by the *Inclusive Digital Economy Scorecard (IDES)*, a tool adopted in 2020 to track progress. The 2021 scorecard highlighted a "Women Inclusiveness Score" of 76%, but noted a persistent gender gap in digital access [cite: 6, 7].

### 1.2 Universal Service Obligations (USO) and Fund Allocation

The mechanism for funding rural connectivity and potentially subsidizing devices is the Universal Access Special Fund (UASF), established under the *Telecommunications Act 2009*.

#### 1.2.1 Legislative Basis
Part 6 of the *Telecommunications Act 2009* establishes the "Universal Access" regime. It creates a **Universal Access Advisory Committee** and a **Universal Access Special Fund**. The Act empowers the Minister, on the recommendation of the Telecommunications Commission of Solomon Islands (TCSI), to declare "eligible universal access areas" where commercial service is not viable [cite: 8].

#### 1.2.2 Allocation and Utilization
Unlike Vanuatu, which has aggressively used its USO fund for "Tablets for Students" programs, the Solomon Islands has historically been conservative in utilizing the UASF for device subsidies.
*   **Infrastructure Focus:** The primary allocation of USO resources has been toward the *Solomon Islands National Broadband Infrastructure Project (SINBIP)*, which aims to construct up to 161 towers to improve 3G/4G coverage [cite: 9].
*   **Regulatory Hesitancy:** Early regulatory reports indicated a reluctance to impose heavy levies or redistribute funds in a way that might bias competition between the two major operators. The TCSI has historically prioritized market-led expansion over subsidy-driven models, although this stance is evolving with the increasing necessity of broadband for e-government services [cite: 2].
*   **Current Status:** There is no evidence in the current public record of a large-scale, government-funded smartphone distribution program financed through the UASF. The focus remains on the "coverage gap" (signal availability) rather than the "usage gap" (device affordability) [cite: 10].

### 1.3 Import Duties, Taxes, and Tariffs on Smartphones

The cost of devices in the Solomon Islands is significantly influenced by the fiscal regime applied at the border.

#### 1.3.1 Tariff Structure
The Solomon Islands utilizes the Harmonized System (HS) for tariff classification. Smartphones fall under HS Code **8517.12** ("Telephones for cellular networks or for other wireless networks") [cite: 11, 12].
*   **Import Duty:** While general consumer goods can attract duties of up to 20%, ICT equipment often benefits from lower rates to encourage digital development. The specific duty rate for mobile phones is often cited in the **0% to 10%** range in practice for Pacific nations to align with digital goals, though general "radio" or "electrical" categories can be higher [cite: 13, 14]. However, third-party logistics providers sometimes estimate duties at **20%** for "Mobile" categories if not specifically exempted [cite: 15].
*   **Goods Tax:** A significant cost driver is the **Goods Tax** (a form of Sales Tax levied at the point of import or wholesale). This is typically around **10% to 15%** [cite: 16]. Even if import duty is zero, the Goods Tax applies to the CIF (Cost, Insurance, Freight) value, raising the shelf price.

#### 1.3.2 Exemptions and Affordability
The *Customs and Excise Act (Cap 121)* allows for exemptions.
*   **Discretionary Exemptions:** The Minister of Finance has the power to grant exemptions for "educational, health, religious, scientific" materials [cite: 14]. However, individual consumer purchases of smartphones do not generally qualify for these exemptions.
*   **Impact on Affordability:** Reductions in duties are a recommended policy tool. The GSMA and World Bank consistently argue that reducing sector-specific taxes on handsets is the fastest way to improve affordability in markets like the Solomon Islands, where the device cost represents a high percentage of monthly income [cite: 17, 18].

### 1.4 Consumer Protection: Financing, Credit, and Repossession

In markets with limited financial infrastructure, device financing (Pay-As-You-Go or PAYG) is a critical enabler of access. However, the regulatory safety net for consumers entering these contracts is complex.

#### 1.4.1 Secured Transactions Act 2008
The Solomon Islands was a pioneer in the Pacific in enacting the *Secured Transactions Act 2008*. This Act allows "movable property" (which includes smartphones) to be used as collateral for loans.
*   **Function:** It established an online filing office for registering security interests. This allows lenders (including potentially telcos or microfinance institutions) to register a claim over a financed device [cite: 19].
*   **Repossession:** The Act grants secured parties the right to take possession of collateral upon default without a court order, provided it can be done without a "breach of the peace" [cite: 19]. This creates a powerful tool for lenders but poses a risk for consumers who rely on their phones for essential services (banking, communication).

#### 1.4.2 Consumer Protection Act 1995
The *Consumer Protection Act* provides general safeguards against "misleading or deceptive conduct" and "unfair trade practices" [cite: 20, 21]. However, it predates the modern digital economy and does not contain specific provisions regulating digital credit terms, algorithmic credit scoring, or remote disabling of devices (locking technology) used in smartphone financing.

#### 1.4.3 Financial Consumer Protection Guidelines 2023
To address modern gaps, the CBSI issued *Financial Consumer Protection Guidelines* in 2023.
*   **Scope:** These guidelines apply to all financial institutions, including digital financial service providers.
*   **Key Provisions:** They mandate "fair treatment," transparent disclosure of terms (pricing, risks), and the establishment of complaints handling units. They specifically prohibit "uncouth and unfair practices" and require providers to assess a consumer's ability to repay to avoid over-indebtedness [cite: 22].

## 2. Comparative Policy Analysis: Lessons from the Pacific

### 2.1 Vanuatu: The "Play or Pay" Model and Direct Subsidies
Vanuatu offers the most distinct contrast to the Solomon Islands regarding direct intervention.
*   **Universal Access Policy (UAP):** Vanuatu implemented a "Play or Pay" model where operators could either roll out services to underserved areas at their own cost ("Play") or pay a levy ("Pay") [cite: 23].
*   **Device Subsidies:** The Telecommunications and Radiocommunications Regulator (TRR) actively funded the **Tablets for Students (TFS)** and **Computer Lab and Internet Community Centre (CLICC)** programs. These initiatives directly subsidized hardware for schools, acknowledging that coverage without devices is insufficient [cite: 23, 24].
*   **Outcome:** Vanuatu achieved its target of 98% population coverage by 2018, largely due to this aggressive regulatory stance [cite: 24].

### 2.2 Fiji: Universal Service Reform and High Penetration
Fiji represents a more mature market with 95% mobile internet connectivity.
*   **Universal Service Fund (USF):** Historically, Fiji's USF was underutilized. However, recent legislative amendments (Bill No. 41 of 2020) aimed to unlock these funds to connect the remaining 5% of the population ("blackspots").
*   **Policy Shift:** The Fijian government has moved toward a "Smart Hands" model and is deploying the USF to subsidize connectivity in declared "Universal Service Areas" (USAs). Fiji also ranks highly for data affordability, partly due to effective competition regulation and the deregulation of the sector [cite: 25, 26, 27].

### 2.3 Papua New Guinea (PNG): Strategic UAS Projects
PNG faces the most severe geographic challenges.
*   **NICTA's Role:** The regulator, NICTA, manages a UAS Fund with a clear strategic plan (2023-2027).
*   **2024 Projects:** NICTA has explicitly budgeted for "Digital Literacy Projects" and mobile broadband expansion. Unlike the Solomon Islands' focus on towers, PNG's UAS strategy includes components for "end-user devices" and "institutional connectivity" (schools/health centers) as part of its mandate to close the digital divide [cite: 28, 29].

### 2.4 Samoa: Education-Led Device Policy
Samoa's approach has been heavily integrated with its education policy.
*   **SchoolNet:** The *National ICT Policy* facilitated the distribution of tablets and connectivity to schools (SchoolNet project).
*   **Affordability:** Samoa acknowledges affordability as a barrier and has focused on liberalizing the gateway (submarine cable) to lower costs, rather than heavy device subsidies for the general public [cite: 30, 31].

### 2.5 Regional Approaches
*   **Pacific Islands Forum (PIF):** The *2050 Strategy for the Blue Pacific Continent* identifies "Technology and Connectivity" as a thematic area. The focus is on harmonizing policy and regulatory frameworks to create a unified digital market, which could eventually lower device costs through aggregated procurement or standardized type approvals [cite: 32, 33].
*   **GSMA & World Bank:** These organizations advocate for treating device access as a market deficiency that requires regulatory intervention (tax breaks) rather than just an infrastructure problem [cite: 10, 17].

## 3. Strategic Context: Barriers and Opportunities

### 3.1 Number Portability and Platform Lock-In
A critical regulatory deficiency in the Solomon Islands is the absence of **Mobile Number Portability (MNP)**.
*   **Status:** International Telecommunication Union (ITU) data confirms the Solomon Islands does not have MNP [cite: 34].
*   **Interaction with Financing:** In the absence of MNP, consumers are reluctant to switch providers because they lose their identity (phone number). When operators offer device financing (e.g., a discounted phone locked to their network), the lack of MNP exacerbates consumer lock-in. A user cannot easily switch to a competitor if they are dissatisfied with the service or pricing without losing both their number and potentially the utility of their device if it is network-locked.
*   **Regulatory Intervention:** Regulators could mandate MNP to lower switching costs, thereby forcing operators to compete on service quality and device affordability rather than relying on lock-in mechanics.

### 3.2 Budget Capacity and Political Will
*   **Fiscal Constraints:** The Solomon Islands 2025 Budget emphasizes "economic recovery" and "transformative investments" but operates under a deficit. The budget relies heavily on donor support (Australia, PRC, World Bank) for capital projects [cite: 35, 36].
*   **Political Will:** There is strong political will for *digital transformation* (evidenced by the NFIS3 and SINBIP), but the fiscal capacity to fund a nationwide consumer device subsidy program is limited. The government prefers donor-funded projects (like the World Bank's CAUSE project or Australian aid) to bridge these gaps rather than direct treasury expenditure [cite: 37, 38].

### 3.3 Infrastructure vs. Market Solutions
Development partners are shifting the narrative.
*   **Old Model:** Build towers (Infrastructure).
*   **New Model:** The World Bank and GSMA now emphasize that *usage* gaps (people covered by a signal but not using it) are larger than *coverage* gaps. The barrier is no longer just the tower; it is the cost of the smartphone.
*   **Recommendation:** Partners recommend that Pacific nations treat devices as part of the "infrastructure" of the digital economy. This implies reducing taxes on devices and creating regulatory sandboxes for innovative financing models (like Pay-As-You-Go asset financing) [cite: 10, 39, 40].

## 4. Regulatory Considerations for Device Financing

### 4.1 Regulating Mobile Network Operators (MNOs) as Lenders
As MNOs (Our Telekom, bmobile) move into device financing and mobile money, they cross into the financial regulatory space.
*   **Credit Reporting:** The *Comprehensive Credit Reporting Regulations 2025* will require lenders (potentially including MNOs offering post-paid devices or credit) to participate in the credit reporting system. This will allow consumers to build a credit history through phone repayments, potentially unlocking access to other financial services [cite: 41, 42].
*   **Mobile Money:** The CBSI's *Practice Guidance Note 2* strictly regulates mobile money issuance, requiring funds to be held in trust (escrow) and prohibiting the use of customer funds for operational costs. This protects consumer balances but adds compliance costs for operators [cite: 43].

### 4.2 Financial Inclusion Mandates
Smartphone access is directly tied to the *NFIS3* targets.
*   **The Link:** The target of 400,000 active financial accounts cannot be met via physical branches. It requires mobile banking. Therefore, the CBSI has a regulatory interest in ensuring smartphones are affordable.
*   **Regulatory Sandbox:** The CBSI has established a Regulatory Sandbox to test fintech innovations. This includes "instant digital loans" (like the *MobileFund* pilot), which rely on mobile usage data for credit scoring. This creates a regulatory imperative to ensure widespread smartphone ownership so that these financial innovations are inclusive [cite: 44, 45].

### 4.3 Gender Equality and the 25% Gap
The "25% smartphone gender gap" is a critical metric in policy frameworks.
*   **Data:** GSMA and UNCDF data indicate that women in the Solomon Islands are significantly less likely to own a smartphone or use mobile internet than men [cite: 46, 47].
*   **Policy Intersection:** The *National Gender Equality and Women's Development Policy* and *NFIS3* intersect here. The NFIS3 explicitly targets a 50% female participation rate in financial inclusion.
*   **Regulatory Action:** To close this gap, regulators are encouraged to support products specifically designed for women, such as savings-linked device financing (e.g., the *YouSave* scheme) which allows informal workers (often women) to save for assets using airtime [cite: 48].

## 5. Conclusion and Recommendations

The Solomon Islands stands at a regulatory crossroads. While it has successfully deployed backbone infrastructure (submarine cables) and set ambitious financial inclusion targets, the *device access* component of the digital ecosystem remains a bottleneck.

**Key Recommendations for Policy Makers:**
1.  **Operationalize the UASF for Devices:** Following the Vanuatu model, the TCSI should consider allocating a portion of the Universal Access Special Fund specifically for subsidizing entry-level smartphones for targeted demographics (women, rural students), rather than solely for tower construction.
2.  **Implement Mobile Number Portability (MNP):** To prevent anti-competitive device lock-in and reduce switching costs, the implementation of MNP is a necessary regulatory step.
3.  **Review Fiscal Policy:** A review of the cumulative impact of Import Duties and Goods Tax on HS 8517.12 devices is recommended. Eliminating duties on entry-level smartphones could directly improve affordability.
4.  **Strengthen Digital Credit Protections:** As device financing grows, specific regulations governing the repossession and remote disabling of smartphones are needed to protect vulnerable consumers, ensuring that "digital collateral" laws align with consumer protection principles.

By aligning the *Telecommunications Act's* universal access mechanisms with the *Central Bank's* financial inclusion goals, the Solomon Islands can bridge the usage gap and transition from a connected nation to a truly digital society.

### Main Research Output

# Smartphone Adoption Barriers and Device Provisioning in Pacific Island Markets

**Mobile money and smartphone adoption in Solomon Islands operate through a bidirectional but asymmetric relationship**, with USSD-based services enabling financial inclusion independently of smartphone ownership while app-based services create direct smartphone dependencies. The evidence reveals that **94% of mobile money transactions in comparable markets still occur via USSD even among smartphone owners**, suggesting that Solomon Islands' dual-track approach—M-SELEN (USSD) alongside IumiCash (smartphone app)—aligns with proven emerging market strategies. This finding has significant implications for the nation's SATSOL infrastructure deployment and financial inclusion goals across 900+ islands.

## The mobile money-smartphone relationship proves context-dependent

Research from Sub-Saharan Africa and the Pacific demonstrates that the mobile money-smartphone relationship is bidirectional but operates asymmetrically depending on service design. In Solomon Islands, this dynamic manifests through three competing services: **IumiCash** (launched early 2023, smartphone-only app with 1% flat fee), **M-SELEN** (launched June 2023, USSD-based, 20,000+ users by early 2024), and **EziPei** (hybrid using SIM-Skin technology).

The theoretical framework suggests mobile money does not require smartphones—Kenya's M-Pesa served **56.7 million customers** primarily through USSD, achieving **70% adult adoption** while smartphone penetration remained at **48%**. Caribou Data/Gates Foundation research found that **87% of financial transactions among smartphone owners** still use USSD, with **94%+ of all mobile money transactions** occurring through this channel. This pattern persists due to zero data costs, universal device compatibility, and perceived security simplicity.

However, smartphone-app services enable richer functionality and create upgrade incentives. GSMA reports from 2018-2024 document that mobile money providers increasingly offer **device financing programs** (Safaricom's Lipa Mdogo Mdogo, M-KOPA partnerships) specifically to migrate users toward smartphone-based super apps. The World Bank Global Findex 2025 notes that **530 million unbanked adults globally own smartphones** but lack mobile money access—indicating smartphone ownership alone doesn't guarantee adoption without complementary service availability.

For Solomon Islands specifically, with **25% bank account ownership**, **70% mobile phone ownership**, but only **20% internet access**, the evidence suggests USSD-based M-SELEN can achieve broader reach while IumiCash captures the smaller smartphone-owning segment. The coexistence creates a natural experiment testing whether app-based services accelerate smartphone demand or USSD services reduce upgrade urgency.

## Adoption barriers compound through interconnected feedback loops

The five co-equal barriers—affordability, infrastructure, digital literacy, gender, and content/language—operate as an interconnected system rather than independent obstacles. UNCDF's Inclusive Digital Economy Scorecard for Solomon Islands quantifies this: **infrastructure at 33%**, **digital literacy at 49%**, **innovation at 25%**, and **gender inclusiveness at 78%** (meaning 22% of women remain excluded from digital economy participation).

**Infrastructure-affordability coupling** represents the primary barrier nexus. Geographic dispersion across 900+ islands drives infrastructure costs disproportionately, translating directly into unaffordable service pricing. The GSMA Mobile Connectivity Index shows Solomon Islands' affordability score at **56.3** versus infrastructure at **33.6**—but these interact multiplicatively when high infrastructure costs per user prevent price reductions.

**Affordability-digital literacy feedback** creates a second compounding cycle. Low smartphone ownership (estimated 30-35% of devices) means fewer opportunities to develop digital skills, which reduces perceived value and willingness to pay for devices. GSMA's 2024 State of Mobile Internet Connectivity identifies that **for the poorest 20% globally, entry-level devices cost 51% of monthly income**; in Sub-Saharan Africa, this reaches **99%**.

**Gender compounds all barriers** through multiple pathways. UN Women/UNCDF research on Solomon Islands found women face:
- Lower education emphasis affecting comprehension of English-language service materials
- Limited financial decision-making autonomy (74% of respondents indicate women should consult husbands)
- Reduced mobility to access points due to household expectations
- Disproportionate impact from distant infrastructure (less time/ability to travel)

The GSMA Mobile Gender Gap Report 2024 confirms globally women are **15% less likely to use mobile internet** with **785 million women** not using mobile internet worldwide. In Solomon Islands, the **12% gender gap in financial inclusion** is lower than PNG's 29% but still significant.

**Multi-barrier interventions** showing effectiveness include Solomon Islands' **youSave LoMobile** micro-pension product, which uses mobile airtime credit for pension savings—addressing affordability (existing airtime networks), infrastructure (8,000+ resellers versus limited bank branches), and financial inclusion simultaneously. The government's 10% tax refund on pension airtime incentivizes adoption while bypassing smartphone requirements entirely.

## Device provisioning evidence favors PAYGO but Pacific applicability remains unproven

The evidence on device provisioning models comes primarily from Sub-Saharan Africa, with **no direct studies from Pacific island contexts**. This represents a significant research gap given the unique challenges of extreme geographic dispersion and small market sizes.

**PAYGO models** show the strongest evidence base. M-KOPA has served **4.8 million customers** across East Africa over 15 years, with **2.1 million first-time smartphone owners** among its customer base. GOGLA's PAYGo PERFORM Monitor (2024) documents:

- **Repayment Rate at 2x contract term: 72%** (customers repay $72 of every $100 financed)
- **Customer Ownership Rate: 78%** (share completing payments)
- **Top performers achieve >90%** repayment with single-digit default rates
- Downpayments **>10%** and contracts **<12 months** correlate with better outcomes

Critical success factors include **device-locking technology** (enabling financing without collateral), **mobile money integration** (automated payments through M-Pesa), **agent networks** (M-KOPA maintains 14,000+ agents in Kenya), and **daily/weekly micro-payments** matching irregular income patterns.

**BNPL schemes** in Africa represent a **$4.48 billion market (2024)** growing at 14.8% CAGR, but require credit assessment infrastructure largely absent in high-unbanked contexts. Lipa Later (Kenya), Keza Africa, and CDcare (Nigeria) demonstrate models, but the **500 million Africans lacking formal identification** creates fundamental eligibility barriers.

**Direct subsidies** show **53-855% social rate of return** according to a peer-reviewed Rwanda handset subsidy study, with **>63% of benefits** flowing through spillover effects to non-recipients. However, research in Tanzania/Malawi found women given free devices often **sell them or lose control to family members**, suggesting subsidies require targeting mechanisms and complementary support.

For Solomon Islands' context, key applicability concerns include:
- **Mobile money ecosystem immaturity**: M-SELEN launched only June 2023; PAYGO success requires robust mobile payment infrastructure
- **Agent network sustainability**: Low transaction volumes in small markets challenge agent economics
- **Offline activation requirement**: OpenPAYGO cryptographic tokens enabling activation without internet/GSM exist but remain untested at Pacific scale
- **No comparable evidence**: The combination of high unbanked rates (75%), extreme dispersion (900+ islands), and small total population (700,000) lacks direct research precedent

## Infrastructure-adoption relationship proves bidirectional with context-dependent sequencing

The evidence strongly supports **bidirectional causality** between infrastructure investment and technology adoption, though the optimal sequencing varies by development context. For Solomon Islands' SATSOL fiber+Starlink deployment, this has critical strategic implications.

**Supply-leading evidence** appears particularly relevant for developing country contexts. Pradhan et al.'s G-20 panel VAR study found causation direction varies by country group, with telecommunications infrastructure more likely to lead growth in less-developed economies. The ITU Pacific connectivity report documents **rapid demand growth following submarine cable deployment**—Papua New Guinea's wholesale prices fell from **~$1,700/Mbps/month (2013) to $21/Mbps/month (2023 target)** following cable investment.

The **Coral Sea Cable System case study** demonstrates this trajectory for Solomon Islands. The 4,700km fiber optic cable connecting Sydney-Port Moresby-Honiara offers **40 Tbps total capacity** with initial **200 Gbps to each country**. Solomon Islands Submarine Cable Company pricing indicates **>50% initial wholesale price reductions** followed by continuing declines with demand growth.

However, **"build it and they will come" approaches face documented limitations**. Strategic Networks Group analysis warns that "many network deployments have had challenges becoming sustainable because they have taken a 'build it and they will come' approach." Academic research by Whitacre, Mills, and Prieger suggests demand-side factors—**income and education**—may be more important adoption drivers than supply-side infrastructure investments.

**Hybrid fiber+satellite** represents the optimal infrastructure model for archipelagos. Indonesia's approach demonstrates mobile-led connectivity (fixed broadband 21% vs. mobile 121 subscriptions/100 inhabitants) enabled by satellite complementing fiber backbone. The Philippines deployed satellite-based connectivity to **438 remote areas** while ADB financed **800 shared telecom towers** in Mindanao. SATSOL's strategy combining Coral Sea Cable backbone with Starlink for last-mile connectivity aligns with these regional best practices.

The evidence indicates **infrastructure investment is necessary but insufficient**—80% of Solomon Islanders live in rural areas that have been "slow to benefit" from the Coral Sea Cable despite late 2019 availability. Cornell research identifies three integrated requirements: **access** (building network), **affordability** (encouraging subscriptions), and **adoption** (training and support). Sequencing recommendations prioritize core infrastructure → anchor institutions (schools, health facilities) → middle mile with demand stimulation → last mile with satellite for remote areas.

## Feature phone services function as stepping stones rather than barriers to smartphone adoption

The evidence suggests USSD-based services **do not delay smartphone migration** but rather build digital literacy and trust that eventually drives upgrade demand. This counters intuitive assumptions that feature phone services reduce smartphone incentive.

**USSD persistence is remarkable even among smartphone owners.** Caribou Data research found **87% of financial transactions** among smartphone owners still use USSD, driven by zero data costs, universal compatibility, and familiarity. CGAP analysis concludes "USSD is currently still the best communication technology available to provide MFS to low-income individuals" and that "smartphone applications...still exclude the vast majority of the poor."

However, **feature phone services appear to accelerate eventual smartphone adoption** through three mechanisms:
1. **Digital literacy building**: New users gain skills on simpler interfaces before transitioning to complex smartphones
2. **Demonstrated value**: Positive mobile money experiences create willingness to invest in better devices
3. **Financial capability**: Mobile savings/credit can fund smartphone acquisition

DAI research explicitly states: "Feature phones may provide a much more immediate value proposition to new users...More feature phones don't necessarily mean fewer smartphones."

**Dual-track strategies dominate successful emerging market deployments.** M-Pesa maintains both USSD (*234#) and Super App channels. Nigerian fintechs (Opay, Paga, Carbon) "all promote sophisticated mobile apps, but have each launched USSD alternatives for common transactions." MTN Mobile Money operates across Africa with parallel USSD and app access.

**Price thresholds for smartphone adoption** cluster around key points:
- **<$20**: Smart feature phones (KaiOS) enabling basic internet
- **$40-50**: Mass-market smartphone threshold for low-income segments
- **$100-150**: Budget smartphone segment dominating Africa (Transsion/Tecno)

Myanmar achieved "land of $20 smartphones" status through cheap Chinese imports, enabling direct leapfrogging from **2% to widespread mobile penetration** in three years without a feature phone intermediate stage. India saw smartphone prices drop from **40% to 8.8% of per capita annual income** (2007-2018), correlating with rapid adoption growth.

For Solomon Islands, GSMA forecasts **smartphone adoption growing from 79% (2022) to 90% by 2030**, with technology mix shifting from **28% 2G / 63% 3G / 9% 4G** toward predominantly 3G (93%). Notably, **no 5G deployment is projected through 2030**. M-SELEN's USSD approach appropriately serves the current market while building the user base and digital literacy foundation for eventual smartphone-app migration.

## Evidence quality and Pacific data gaps warrant cautious extrapolation

The research reveals substantial Pacific-specific evidence gaps that limit confidence in extrapolating findings from Africa and Asia.

**Strong evidence exists for:**
- USSD dominance in mobile money transactions (multiple large-scale studies)
- Bidirectional infrastructure-adoption causality (peer-reviewed econometric research)
- PAYGO repayment rates in East Africa (industry-wide standardized KPIs)
- Multi-barrier compounding effects (theoretical frameworks with empirical validation)

**Moderate evidence supports:**
- Feature phone services as stepping stones (inferential from M-Pesa trajectory)
- Hybrid fiber+satellite optimal for archipelagos (regional case studies)
- Subsidy effectiveness with appropriate targeting (peer-reviewed impact evaluations)

**Limited or absent evidence includes:**
- **No Pacific PAYGO smartphone financing studies**—all evidence from Sub-Saharan Africa
- **No longitudinal device transition research** in Pacific contexts
- **No repayment rate data** for extreme geographic dispersion scenarios
- **IumiCash adoption data unavailable**—no public user numbers found despite April 2025 CBSI licensing
- **Post-Coral Sea Cable adoption studies confounded by COVID-19** pandemic timing

Key methodological limitations include potential bias in M-KOPA and GSMA industry reports toward mobile sector interests, correlation-causation conflation in most infrastructure-adoption studies, and small sample sizes in Pacific-specific surveys. The unique combination of Solomon Islands' characteristics—75% unbanked, 900+ islands, 700,000 population, emerging mobile money ecosystem—lacks direct research precedent anywhere globally.

## Implications and recommendations for Solomon Islands context

The evidence synthesis suggests several strategic implications:

**Mobile money strategy**: The dual-track approach with M-SELEN (USSD) for broad reach and IumiCash (app) for enhanced functionality aligns with global best practices. M-SELEN's **20,000+ users and 250+ agents** within months of launch demonstrates USSD viability. Prioritizing USSD expansion for financial inclusion while developing app capabilities for willing upgraders represents an evidence-aligned strategy.

**Device provisioning**: Phased implementation appears optimal—building on existing youSave LoMobile airtime-based model, then introducing PAYGO smartphone financing as M-SELEN matures sufficient for payment automation. **Offline activation tokens** (OpenPAYGO technology) could address remote island connectivity gaps. Target entry-level devices at **$50-100** with **10-20% downpayments** and **6-12 month terms** based on African performance data.

**Infrastructure sequencing**: SATSOL's fiber+Starlink hybrid approach aligns with archipelago evidence. Critical complements include digital literacy programming (current score 42%), anchor institution connectivity (schools, health posts, government), affordable pricing mechanisms, and locally relevant content development. Infrastructure alone will not drive adoption without these demand-side investments.

**Barrier intervention**: Multi-barrier approaches offer highest returns. Priority interventions addressing multiple barriers simultaneously include:
- Mobile money integration with existing airtime networks (affordability + infrastructure)
- Infrastructure sharing to reduce per-operator costs (infrastructure + affordability)
- Digital literacy embedded in trusted community institutions (skills + content)
- Gender-specific targeting addressing mobility, autonomy, and safety concerns

The 2027 National Financial Inclusion Strategy targets—50% adults with digital payment access, 75% registered businesses—appear achievable given M-SELEN's trajectory, but require sustained attention to the compounding barrier effects and appropriate sequencing of demand-side investments alongside infrastructure deployment.
