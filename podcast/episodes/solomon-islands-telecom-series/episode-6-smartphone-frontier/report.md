# The Smartphone Frontier: Why 59% of the Pacific Stays Dark Despite Network Coverage

The mobile towers are standing. The signals are broadcasting. Across the Pacific islands, 86% of the population lives within range of a mobile broadband network. Yet only 27% actually use mobile internet. This 59-percentage-point gap between coverage and usage represents the highest such disparity anywhere in the world outside Sub-Saharan Africa (GSMA Mobile Connectivity Index, 2024).

In the Solomon Islands, this paradox takes on stark dimensions. The nation has 547,000 mobile connections serving roughly 800,000 people. But smartphone penetration hovers at just 42%, and only 352,000 people use the internet (DataReportal, 2025). The infrastructure exists. The demand exists. Something else is stopping the connection.

That something is not a single barrier but an ecosystem of five interlocking constraints that together keep smartphones out of reach for most Solomon Islanders. Device affordability is certainly part of the story, but it is neither the beginning nor the end.

## The Five Barriers Are Not a Hierarchy

Development organizations often treat smartphone adoption as a linear problem: build the towers, lower device prices, watch connectivity flourish. The evidence tells a different story. A meta-analysis synthesizing findings across low- and middle-income countries identifies five co-equal constraints operating simultaneously, each capable of blocking adoption regardless of progress on the others (Perplexity synthesis of A4AI, GSMA, and World Bank data, 2024-2025).

**Affordability** remains the most visible barrier. Entry-level smartphones in Solomon Islands retail for SBD 900-2,100 (USD 108-252), according to local Facebook Marketplace listings. For the poorest segments, entry-level devices consume 30% of monthly income. But the burden falls unevenly: women face costs equivalent to 24% of their monthly income for the same devices that cost men 12% (Alliance for Affordable Internet, 2018).

**Infrastructure gaps** create the "coverage-usage divide." Solomon Islands achieved only 25% 4G coverage as of 2020 (ITU data). But coverage statistics miss a crucial reality: even where signals exist, using them is prohibitive. Data costs approximately US$170 per month, equivalent to 38% of average monthly income (GSMA Intelligence, 2024). A smartphone without affordable data is a camera with a paperweight attached.

**Digital literacy** operates as an independent constraint. UNESCO reports over 40% of developing country populations lack basic digital skills. Solomon Islands' digital literacy score of 42% reflects this gap (UNCDF Inclusive Digital Economy Scorecard, 2020-2021). The Malawi smartphone RCT found uptake was "sensitive to participant literacy levels," indicating basic literacy is a prerequisite for smartphone utility distinct from device access itself (Roessler et al., 2023).

**Content and language barriers** reduce perceived smartphone utility. Locally relevant digital content in indigenous languages remains scarce across Pacific island nations. Government services, educational resources, and market information in local languages is minimal, limiting practical application for non-English speakers (GSMA Mobile Money in Solomon Islands, 2023).

**Gender inequality** cuts across all four other barriers, compounding each one. The GSMA Mobile Gender Gap Report 2024 documents that women in Sub-Saharan Africa are 25% less likely to own smartphones than men, and 265 million fewer women than men use mobile internet in low- and middle-income countries globally. In Solomon Islands, UN Women and UNCDF research found women face lower education emphasis affecting comprehension of English-language service materials, limited financial decision-making autonomy (74% of respondents indicated women should consult husbands on major purchases), and reduced mobility to access points.

These barriers compound. Low smartphone ownership means fewer opportunities to develop digital skills, which reduces perceived value and willingness to pay for devices, perpetuating low ownership. Women facing all five barriers simultaneously find the smartphone frontier receding faster than they can approach it.

## China's Subsidy Experiment: When Paying Does Not Solve the Problem

If affordability were the primary barrier, subsidies should work. China provided a natural experiment testing this proposition at massive scale in early 2025.

The Chinese government launched a trade-in program offering 15% subsidies on smartphone purchases, capped at CNY 500 (approximately USD 71). Initial results appeared spectacular: sales surged 3-5 times normal levels on the first day. By March 2025, 48 million participants had purchased 51.48 million devices, generating USD 19.9 billion in sales (Yicai Global, January 2025; Economic Times, March 2025).

Then came week 11. Despite ongoing subsidies, cumulative smartphone sales had declined 5% year-over-year compared to the same period before the program began. The surge had exhausted existing demand without creating sustainable new adoption patterns (EE Times Asia, May 2025).

The China case provides rare real-time evidence of subsidy limitations. Subsidies can accelerate purchases among those already close to buying. They cannot address deeper barriers of literacy, infrastructure quality, content relevance, or social permission that prevent demand from forming in the first place. For Solomon Islands, where 75% of the population remains unbanked and lives in rural subsistence economies, the China lesson suggests affordability programs must be paired with interventions addressing the full barrier ecosystem.

## Device Financing: PAYGO Works, But Requires Infrastructure That Does Not Exist

Among device provisioning models, Pay-As-You-Go (PAYGO) financing shows the strongest evidence for serving unbanked populations. The World Bank's 2025 assessment identifies PAYGO as "generally most suitable for low-income individuals" because it operates without credit checks, relying instead on device-locking technology that allows remote disabling if payments are missed.

Evidence from East Africa demonstrates PAYGO's potential. M-KOPA has served 4.8 million customers across the region, with 2.1 million first-time smartphone owners. The GOGLA PAYGo PERFORM Monitor documents repayment rates of 72% at twice the contract term, with customer ownership rates of 78%.

However, PAYGO success in Kenya depended on M-Pesa's mature mobile money infrastructure for automated payments and an agent network of 14,000+ representatives. Solomon Islands lacks both. As of July 2023, there were only approximately 13,400 mobile wallet accounts across all providers (IumiCash, Ezi Pei, and M-Selen), with only 10-15% active (Pacific E-commerce Portal Assessment, March 2024). Cash still accounted for 80% of transactions as of 2020.

Buy-Now-Pay-Later (BNPL) models face even steeper barriers. Safaricom's Lipa Mdogo Mdogo leverages M-Pesa's network for repayment processing. Vodacom's Easy2Own mandates bank account deductions, feasible only where banking penetration reaches 89%. Neither translates to Solomon Islands, where 75% lack bank accounts.

The circular challenge: PAYGO financing requires mobile money infrastructure to process payments. Mobile money adoption requires smartphones for full functionality. Smartphone adoption requires financing mechanisms. Each element depends on the others, creating a chicken-and-egg problem market forces alone cannot resolve.

## The Lock-In Problem: When Financing Meets Missing Portability

The absence of Mobile Number Portability (MNP) in Solomon Islands, confirmed by ITU DataHub in 2024, intersects with device financing to create market distortion.

In markets with MNP, consumers who finance devices through one operator can switch to a competitor while keeping their phone number. Without MNP, the consumer's phone number becomes hostage to their current provider. Combined with device financing, this creates compounded lock-in: consumers cannot switch providers without losing both their number and potentially the utility of a network-locked device.

The Gemini Deep Research analysis identifies this as a critical gap. When operators offer device financing, the lack of MNP "exacerbates consumer lock-in. A user cannot easily switch to a competitor if they are dissatisfied with the service or pricing without losing both their number and potentially the utility of their device if it is network-locked."

This regulatory gap means device financing programs, intended to expand access, could instead entrench duopoly control. Consumers locked into multi-month payment plans cannot vote with their feet. The missing MNP regulation, explored in Episode 2 of this series, has direct implications for whether device financing expands inclusion or merely shifts the terms of exclusion.

For women, lock-in compounds the gender dynamics of device ownership. Research in Tanzania and Malawi found women given free devices often sell them or lose control to family members, suggesting subsidies or financing require targeting mechanisms and complementary support. A woman who finances a phone but faces pressure to hand it over may find herself locked into payments for a phone she no longer uses.

## Mobile Money and Smartphones: A Relationship That Has Not Started

Theory suggests mobile money and smartphone adoption should form a virtuous cycle. Khan and colleagues' 2025 meta-analysis demonstrates mobile money's direct effect on financial inclusion through informal enterprise profitability (ScienceDirect, 2025). Smartphone access enables mobile money adoption, which improves business viability and strengthens ability to sustain device payments.

In Solomon Islands, this cycle has not started. With only 13,400 mobile wallet accounts at 10-15% activity rates, mobile money adoption is too low to create demand-side pressure for smartphones. The Central Bank's National Financial Inclusion Strategy 3 (NFIS3) target of 400,000 active financial accounts by 2025 remains distant.

M-Selen from Our Telekom operates via USSD, requiring no smartphone. IumiCash offers a smartphone app with features like QR payments but depends on the 42% that owns smartphones. Research from Sub-Saharan Africa demonstrates 94% of mobile money transactions occur via USSD even among smartphone owners, driven by zero data costs and universal device compatibility (Caribou Data/Gates Foundation research).

This suggests USSD-based services do not delay smartphone migration but rather build digital literacy and trust that eventually drives upgrade demand. M-Pesa in Kenya served 56.7 million customers primarily through USSD while smartphone penetration was only 48%. Feature phones functioned as stepping stones, not barriers.

For Solomon Islands, expanding M-Selen's USSD reach may paradoxically accelerate smartphone adoption by building the digital literacy and demonstrated value that justify device investment. The youSave LoMobile product, which uses mobile airtime credit for pension savings with a 10% government tax refund, addresses affordability and infrastructure constraints through existing airtime networks while bypassing smartphone requirements.

## Starlink and the Connectivity Transformation

Starlink received its class license from the Telecommunications Commission of Solomon Islands in September 2024. Deployments have begun reaching remote areas including Aleang village in the Shortland Islands, enabling first-time internet access in communities terrestrial infrastructure could never economically serve (Solomon Star News, January 2025).

Competitive implications are visible elsewhere in the Pacific. In the Cook Islands, Vodafone experienced a 12% revenue decline in 2024 attributed to Starlink competition. For smartphone viability, high-speed satellite internet makes data-intensive applications feasible where previous connections were unreliable. Education, telemedicine, and economic activities requiring consistent connectivity become possible.

However, no direct 2024-2025 data links Starlink deployment to smartphone adoption increases. The Coral Sea Cable System achieved initial wholesale price reductions exceeding 50% after its late 2019 deployment. Yet 80% of Solomon Islanders living in rural areas have been "slow to benefit" from the cable despite its availability.

The evidence indicates infrastructure investment is necessary but insufficient. Cornell researchers identify three integrated requirements: access (building network), affordability (encouraging subscriptions), and adoption (training and support). Starlink addresses the first requirement in remote areas. The second and third require policy interventions the market cannot automatically provide.

## The Vanuatu Contrast: A Different Policy Choice

Vanuatu sits 1,100 kilometers east of Solomon Islands with similar geographic and economic characteristics. Yet their approaches to device access diverged significantly.

Vanuatu implemented a "Play or Pay" model under its Universal Access Policy. Operators could either roll out services to underserved areas ("Play") or pay a levy funding government-led expansion ("Pay"). The Telecommunications and Radiocommunications Regulator actively funded the Tablets for Students and Computer Lab programs, directly subsidizing hardware for schools (TRBR Vanuatu documentation).

Result: Vanuatu achieved 98% population coverage by 2018. In 2025, Digicel Vanuatu committed to 99% mobile coverage under continuing policy requirements (Developing Telecoms, May 2025).

Solomon Islands took a more conservative approach. The Universal Access Special Fund exists but has been allocated primarily to infrastructure. The Solomon Islands National Broadband Infrastructure Project aims to construct up to 161 towers. Device subsidies have not been funded through the UASF.

The Gemini analysis characterizes Solomon Islands' regulatory stance as "historically prioritizing market-led expansion over subsidy-driven models." The Telecommunications Commission has shown "reluctance to impose heavy levies or redistribute funds in a way that might bias competition."

This regulatory hesitancy conflicts with the Central Bank's financial inclusion mandates. NFIS3 targets 400,000 active financial accounts by 2025, with 50% required to be women. The Central Bank explicitly recognizes that archipelagic geography makes brick-and-mortar banking unviable, making Digital Financial Services the primary vehicle. Since DFS requires mobile interfaces, smartphone accessibility becomes a national strategic priority.

The tension between telecommunications regulatory conservatism and central bank inclusion mandates creates policy misalignment. The Vanuatu model demonstrates coordinated regulatory intervention can achieve results the market alone does not produce.

## What the Research Does Not Know

The evidence base for smartphone adoption in Pacific island contexts contains significant gaps.

Virtually no peer-reviewed randomized controlled trials examine smartphone adoption in Pacific island nations. Existing experimental evidence derives from Blumenstock's Uganda research (2020), Roessler's Malawi study (2023), and India-focused analyses. These may not transfer to island-specific challenges.

The Roessler Malawi RCT documented "substantial phone attrition between distribution and follow-up" with mixed income effects. In economies with extreme vulnerability like Solomon Islands, device retention and sustainable ownership patterns remain unstudied.

PAYGO financing evidence comes entirely from Sub-Saharan Africa. No studies examine repayment rates or operational sustainability across 900+ islands with populations scattered in small communities. Agent network economics that work in Kenya's concentrated rural areas may not function where individual islands have populations in the hundreds.

Cultural factors beyond gender remain unstudied. Traditional governance structures, communal resource allocation, and kinship networks likely affect individual device ownership decisions in ways African evidence cannot predict. The Solomon Islands' combination of 75% unbanked population, 900+ islands, and 700,000 total population lacks direct research precedent anywhere globally.

## Key Takeaways

The smartphone frontier in Solomon Islands is not a single wall to be breached but a five-sided box requiring simultaneous pressure on all constraints:

- **Affordability alone does not explain adoption patterns.** China's subsidy program achieved massive initial surge followed by year-over-year decline within 11 weeks, demonstrating paying for devices does not address deeper barriers.

- **PAYGO financing shows promise but requires mobile money infrastructure that does not exist.** The 13,400 mobile wallet accounts at 10-15% activity rates cannot support automated payment systems that made M-KOPA successful in Kenya.

- **Missing Mobile Number Portability combines with device financing to create lock-in.** Consumers cannot switch providers without losing phone numbers and potentially device utility.

- **Gender inequality compounds every other barrier.** Women face device costs equivalent to double the income percentage of men, limited financial autonomy, and reduced mobility to access points. The NFIS3 target of 50% female financial inclusion cannot be met without addressing the 25% smartphone gender gap.

- **Vanuatu achieved 98% coverage through direct device subsidies; Solomon Islands' conservative approach has reached 42% smartphone penetration.** The difference reflects policy choice, not inevitable circumstance.

- **Pacific-specific evidence remains almost entirely absent.** All PAYGO studies, smartphone adoption RCTs, and device financing analyses come from Africa and Asia.

The path forward requires coordinated intervention across regulatory agencies. The Telecommunications Commission's Universal Access Special Fund could follow the Vanuatu model of direct device subsidies. Mobile Number Portability would reduce lock-in and improve competitive dynamics. The Central Bank's financial inclusion mandates and the telecommunications regulator's competition concerns need alignment rather than conflict.

The towers are standing. The signals are broadcasting. Whether the phones light up depends on decisions that have not yet been made.
