## Operational Requirements for Launching a Third Mobile Network Operator in Solomon Islands

### Regulatory Licensing Framework and Process

**Licensing Authority and Legislative Basis**

The Telecommunications Commission of Solomon Islands (TCSI) is the independent statutory authority responsible for regulating the telecommunications sector under the Telecommunications Act 2009. The regulatory framework requires that no person or entity may provide telecommunications services or use radio frequencies in Solomon Islands without appropriate authorization from TCSI. The Commission operates under Part 5 of the Act, which establishes two primary licensing categories: individual licenses (for customized operations) and class licenses (for standard applications via prescribed forms).[1]

**Individual License Application Requirements**

A third mobile operator must apply for an individual service license, which differs from the class license regime. The application process requires submission of comprehensive documentation through TCSI's prescribed standard form. Key application components must include:[2][3]

- **Organizational details**: Company registration, trading name, business license confirmation
- **Management composition**: Directors' background, appointments, residency status, previous business experience, and any history of bankruptcy or liquidation
- **Technical capabilities**: Network design specifications, technology platforms, service deployment methodology
- **Financial qualifications**: Detailed five-year business plan including sources of funding (debt/equity structure), independent financial confirmation, pro-forma financial statements (cash flow and income projections), profitability timeline
- **Marketing strategy**: Customer acquisition plans, revenue projections, service offerings
- **Quality of service commitments**: Service level targets with supporting documentation
- **Network coverage plans**: Geographic rollout phases, coverage targets, timeline milestones

The Telecommunications Act 2009 does not specify minimum capital thresholds; rather, financial qualification is assessed through the competitive evaluation process based on the applicant's demonstrated financial resources and operational capability.[4]

**Timeline and Approval Process**

The regulatory timeline operates as follows:

- **Initial response**: TCSI must respond to license applications within 45 days with approval, denial, or information requests[4]
- **Individual license determination**: Per published evaluation processes; for competitive selection, TCSI uses objective criteria including financial bids, technical experience, service quality commitments, and other relevant factors[4]
- **License issuance**: Follows completion of spectrum allocation coordination (simultaneously handled), with license publication in the Gazette and on the TCSI website[4]
- **Effective date**: Individual licenses are effective upon issuance; no waiting period applies[2]

An applicant must allow adequate time for spectrum application processing, which can be submitted concurrently with the service license application.[4]

**Financial Obligations and Regulatory Fees**

Operating telecommunications services in Solomon Islands requires payment of multiple categories of regulatory fees:

- **Annual license fees**: Up to 2% of gross revenues paid annually to TCSI[4]
- **Supplementary fees**: Additional fees if the Telecommunications Commission Special Fund operates at a deficit[4]
- **Universal Access Fund levy**: Contribution of up to 2% of gross revenues to support universal access obligations in rural areas[4]
- **Application/administrative fees**: Non-refundable charges for license application processing, with specific fee schedules published by TCSI

"Gross revenues" are defined as revenues from telecommunications service provision and access after deducting amounts paid on an arms' length basis to other service providers, but before deduction of operational costs. These obligations apply from the commencement of service delivery.[4]

### Spectrum Allocation Framework

**Available Frequency Bands and Current Allocation Status**

Solomon Islands has allocated the following commercial mobile spectrum bands according to its National Radio Spectrum Plan (consultation draft released December 2015):[5]

| **Frequency Band** | **Technology** | **Current Allocation Status** | **Capacity Status** |
|---|---|---|---|
| 700 MHz | 4G LTE | Allocated (3 × 15 MHz) | Not currently utilized |
| 850 MHz (Band 5) | UMTS/4G LTE | Allocated | Assigned; requires review |
| 900 MHz (E-GSM) | GSM/UMTS/4G LTE | Fully allocated and utilized | High utilization |
| 1800 MHz (DCS) | GSM/UMTS/4G LTE | Assigned and utilized | Capacity available |
| 2100 MHz (Band 1) | UMTS/4G LTE | Assigned | Not currently utilized; capacity available |
| 2300 MHz | 4G LTE | Assigned | Not currently utilized; capacity available |
| 2500 MHz | 4G LTE | Assigned | Not currently utilized; capacity available |

Current operators use predominantly 900 MHz and 1800 MHz GSM with 2100 MHz UMTS for 3G services. Our Telekom (formerly Solomon Telekom) operates on GSM 900 MHz, 1800 MHz, UMTS Band 1 (2100 MHz), and UMTS Band 5 (850 MHz). This suggests available capacity in the 700 MHz, 2300 MHz, or 2500 MHz bands, or additional allocation in underutilized bands.[6]

**Spectrum Allocation Process**

TCSI allocates and assigns frequency bands according to its spectrum management authority under Part 7 of the Telecommunications Act 2009. The allocation process requires:[7]

- **Coordination**: Spectrum license applications coordinated with service license applications[4]
- **National Spectrum Plan compliance**: All allocations follow the published national spectrum plan or amendments thereto[7]
- **Non-discriminatory allocation**: Allocations made on an open, non-discriminatory basis per regulatory standards[7]
- **Frequency assignment**: Specific frequencies assigned via regulatory determination with published conditions[7]

**Spectrum Fees and Usage Conditions**

TCSI may set annual fees for spectrum use through regulation, with revenue directed to the Universal Access Special Fund to subsidize rural service provision. Where spectrum is bundled within a service license, no separate spectrum fee applies. The Commission has authority to impose conditions including:[7]

- **Vacation obligations**: Non-exclusive declarations for particular bands[4]
- **Non-use remedies**: Frequency vacation without compensation if not used within specified periods or if non-compliance occurs[4]
- **Compensation mechanisms**: Spectrum vacation with compensation available only under specified circumstances[4]

### Network Infrastructure and Rollout Sequencing

**Infrastructure Inventory and Coverage Benchmarks**

As of 2014, Solomon Islands' mobile network infrastructure comprised:[8]

- 125 GSM base stations (2G)
- 18 UMTS nodes (3G, 2100 MHz)
- 19 UMTS nodes (3G, 850 MHz)
- Connectivity via microwave links and satellite backhaul
- International gateway and earth stations based in Honiara

Current coverage: 83% of the population has mobile network access (2014 data), though this concentrated in urban centers and provincial capitals. Rural coverage remains limited, with approximately 80% of the population residing in rural villages.[8]

**Capital Infrastructure Costs and Benchmarks**

Mobile network deployment in geographically dispersed island environments presents significant capital cost challenges:

- **Per-site capital expenditure (tower + equipment)**: AUD $1-3 million for fully designed, built, and commissioned towers in regional areas, with power and backhaul potentially accounting for 50% of costs, RAN equipment ~30%, and tower structure 20-25%[9]
- **Backhaul costs**: Can constitute 30-50% of total infrastructure investment; in Solomon Islands' case, satellite backhaul for remote sites is predominant[10]
- **Annual maintenance**: Estimated at 5-7% of capital cost per annum for tower maintenance alone[9]

For a small island archipelago, total network investment to achieve 50%+ population coverage with 3G/4G typically requires significant capital mobilization.

**Rollout Sequencing and Phases**

Network deployment typically follows a five-phase framework:[11]

1. **Planning Phase**: Market analysis, business case development, spectrum coordination, regulatory approval (typically 3-6 months)
2. **Design Phase**: RF planning, site survey, infrastructure design, backhaul routing (2-4 months)
3. **Build Phase**: Site acquisition, civil works, equipment installation, commissioning (6-12 months for initial urban rollout)
4. **Acceptance Phase**: Network testing, performance validation, live network cutover
5. **Operations Phase**: Ongoing maintenance, capacity management, technology upgrades

For Solomon Islands' market, a third operator would likely prioritize:

- **Phase 1 (Months 0-6)**: Honiara and key provincial centers (Auki, Gizo) with existing infrastructure sites
- **Phase 2 (Months 6-12)**: Additional provincial centers and secondary urban areas
- **Phase 3 (Months 12-24)**: Rural rollout via satellite or microwave backhaul to achieve 50%+ population coverage

The incumbent operators achieved the current 83% coverage through 10-15 years of gradual expansion, suggesting that a new entrant targeting rapid market share would need to focus initially on dense urban areas.

**Performance Obligations and Network Milestones**

Individual licenses typically include network rollout conditions with specified coverage milestones, performance bonds, and financial penalties for non-compliance. Based on Bemobile's historical license conditions:

- **Performance bond**: Cash bond (typically USD $5-10 million) securing network rollout obligations[12]
- **Coverage milestones**: Phased population or geographic coverage targets at specified intervals (e.g., Year 1: 20%, Year 2: 40%, Year 3: 60%)[12]
- **Frequency of review**: Annual compliance audits; drawdown of performance bond funds for milestone failures[12]
- **Remedies**: Extended timelines for completion, bond drawdown, or license amendment based on operator circumstances[12]

### Spectrum Management and Technical Requirements

**Interconnection and Access Obligations**

Part 9 of the Telecommunications Act 2009 establishes mandatory interconnection requirements. All licensed operators must:[4]

- **Provide interconnection**: Upon reasonable request from other licensed operators, at technically and economically feasible points on the network[4]
- **Negotiate in good faith**: Within 30 days of written request; if agreement not reached, the Commission may intervene and issue binding determinations[4]
- **Reference offers**: Dominant operators must publish reference offers specifying interconnection terms (call handover points, technical standards, pricing)[4]
- **Call termination**: The physical linking of networks to allow calls originated on one operator's network to terminate on another's[4]

For a new entrant, this means automatic right to negotiate interconnection agreements with existing operators (Solomon Telekom and Bmobile-Vodafone) for:
- Mobile termination rates (handover costs)
- Call origination/transit
- Access to number portability infrastructure (when implemented)
- Roaming arrangements

**Dominant Service Provider Status and Obligations**

Solomon Telekom is deemed a dominant service provider in all markets except mobile telecommunications, though both existing operators have substantial market power in the mobile segment. A third operator entering the market would not initially be classified as dominant, but would be subject to non-discriminatory access requirements if it later achieves significant market share.[4]

### Distribution Channel Buildout

**SIM Card Registration and Distribution Framework**

The Solomon Islands operates a mandatory SIM registration regime under TCSI directives to manage subscriber identification. All service providers must:

**SIM Registration Requirements**:[13][14][15]

- Register all new SIM cards within 24 hours of activation in the service provider's database
- Upload validated subscriber data to the National Subscriber Registry (NSR) within 1-5 working days
- Maintain compliant subscriber information including identity verification (passport, voter ID, driver's license, birth certificate, or NPF membership ID)[16]
- Conduct monthly registration audits to ensure compliance[14]
- Suspend unregistered or non-compliant SIM cards per regulatory directives[14]

**Authorized Agent/Dealer Networks**:[14]

Distribution of SIM cards requires a structured agent network. Service providers must:

- Authorize agents, dealers, and distributors in writing with documented business licensing (registered business name or limited company registration)[14]
- Verify agent business locations (Honiara area, provincial area, or village designation)[14]
- Ensure agents have valid contact information and employee consent forms for SIM card personnel[14]
- Conduct monthly compliance inspections to verify SIM registration procedures[14]
- Submit quarterly reports to TCSI on agent network compliance[14]

**Distribution Channel Strategy for Market Entry**

A third operator should establish a multi-channel distribution approach:

1. **Primary channels** (high-volume locations):
   - Operator-owned retail stores in Honiara, provincial capitals (Auki, Gizo, Noro, Munda)
   - Major shopping centers and transport hubs

2. **Secondary channels** (coverage extension):
   - Authorized mobile phone retailers and electronics shops
   - Petrol stations and convenience stores
   - Community post offices and trading stores in secondary towns

3. **Digital channels**:
   - Online SIM activation (where feasible via digital identity verification)
   - Self-service kiosks in urban centers

**Market Benchmarks**: In Pacific markets, digital channel penetration remains limited due to low internet penetration (20% in Solomon Islands) and high rates of cash-based transactions. Physical distribution remains dominant, with agents typically earning commission on SIM sales (typically 5-10% of retail price) and activation bonuses.[17]

### Customer Acquisition and Pricing Strategy

**Market Characteristics and Prepaid Dominance**

Solomon Islands' mobile market is overwhelmingly prepaid-based, consistent with Pacific and developing Asia-Pacific norms. Market characteristics include:[18]

- **Prepaid penetration**: 82% of connections in developing Asia-Pacific markets
- **Churn rates**: 3.1-6.5% per month (37-80% annually) in comparable developing markets; operators must replace entire customer base every 2-3 years
- **ARPU (Average Revenue Per User)**: Low to moderate; 2011 data showed affordability challenges with 17.1% of monthly income spent on mobile services in Solomon Islands vs. 7% in Fiji or Samoa[19]
- **Customer value focus**: High churn-prone prepaid market requires retention and value-added services to achieve profitability

**Pricing Strategy Framework**

Without number portability currently implemented, a new entrant must compete primarily on:

1. **Service affordability**: Lower per-minute voice rates and data bundle pricing
2. **Coverage and quality**: Superior signal quality and reliability in populated areas
3. **Value-added services**: Mobile money, international roaming, data packages
4. **Promotional bundling**: Combined voice/SMS/data packages

**Competitive Pricing Benchmarks** (based on Pacific comparables):[20][19]

Voice services in Pacific SIDS range from VUV 1.64-2.01 per minute (postpaid vs. prepaid), with bundled packages increasingly common. Data pricing typically ranges USD $0.50-2.00 per MB in developing markets, with 1GB monthly bundles priced USD $5-15.

**Customer Acquisition Without Number Portability**

Number portability has not been implemented in Solomon Islands; TCSI is evaluating implementation frameworks with no announced timeline. Therefore, a third operator's customer acquisition must address:[1]

- **SIM switching friction**: Customers must retain old SIM for portability; encourage dual-SIM ownership via promotions
- **Targeted acquisition**: Focus on price-sensitive segments and coverage-deprived areas
- **Retention focus**: Emphasize loyalty programs, automatic top-up discounts, preferential pricing for long-tenure customers
- **Financial identity building**: Link mobile usage to value accumulation (mobile money, credit scoring) to increase switching costs[18]

**Agent/Retailer Channel Incentives**:

Typical dealer compensation structures in Pacific markets include:
- Retail margin: 10-15% on SIM card sales
- Activation bonus: USD $0.50-2.00 per new subscriber
- Volume discounts for high-volume dealers
- Training and marketing support for prominent locations

### Operational Systems Requirements

**Business Support Systems (BSS) and Operations Support Systems (OSS)**

A fully operational mobile network requires integrated BSS/OSS platforms to manage:

**BSS Functions**:[21][22]
- Customer management and ordering systems
- Billing and invoicing (prepaid top-ups, postpaid invoicing)
- Revenue assurance and financial reporting
- Customer care and service delivery tracking
- Provisioning and activation workflows
- Rating and charging engines for calls/SMS/data

**OSS Functions**:[22][21]
- Network configuration and management
- Resource inventory management (spectrum, sites, equipment)
- Fault management and incident response
- Service provisioning automation
- Service assurance and quality monitoring
- SIM provisioning and remote lifecycle management
- Network performance analytics

**Integration Requirements**:

Modern OSS/BSS systems must support:

- **Cloud-native architecture**: Scalability for growing subscriber base, modular design enabling component updates[21]
- **Open APIs**: TM Forum Open Digital Architecture (ODA) standards enabling third-party integration for payment systems, financial services, and partner platforms[21]
- **Real-time integration**: Batch daily data consolidation between central database and service provider systems with push/pull data aggregation capabilities[15]
- **SIM management automation**: Bulk SIM activation, remote provisioning for eSIMs, automated device suspension for non-payment[22]
- **Interconnection systems**: Automated call routing, rating, and settlement with other operators
- **Regulatory reporting**: Automated subscriber data export to National Subscriber Registry (NSR), frequency usage reporting, compliance monitoring

**Vendor Options**:

OSS/BSS platforms suitable for small island operators include:
- Established telecom software vendors: Openet (AMDOCS), Comtech, Netcracker, CSG Systems
- Cloud-based solutions: Openminded (Amdocs cloud), Mavenir's cloud RAN management
- Regional vendors with developing market experience

Typical implementation timeline: 6-12 months from vendor selection to live deployment, requiring dedicated IT team and partner systems integrators.

### Regulatory Compliance and Universal Access Obligations

**Universal Access Commitments**

Part 6 of the Telecommunications Act 2009 establishes universal access obligations requiring licensed operators to contribute to telecommunications services in underserved areas. Specific obligations are determined per license but typically include:[23][4]

- **Universal Access Fund contribution**: 0.5-2% of gross revenues directed to rural service subsidies[4]
- **Specified coverage targets**: Population or geographic coverage percentages in rural provinces as stipulated in license conditions
- **Service quality standards**: Minimum quality of service levels for universal access provision

**Compliance Reporting and Audit**

Operators must submit:
- **Annual reports**: Service provider operating statistics including subscriber counts, revenue, network coverage, service quality metrics[24]
- **Quarterly financial reports**: Revenue and operational expense documentation
- **Compliance certifications**: Verification of universal access obligations, interconnection compliance, number usage compliance
- **Network data**: Coverage maps, base station locations, technology deployment status

The Commission conducts regulatory audits to verify compliance with license conditions and may:
- Investigate alleged violations of interconnection, pricing, or service quality obligations
- Impose administrative penalties (up to specified amounts per violation)
- Require license amendments or operational changes
- Revoke licenses for material non-compliance

### Implementation Timeline and Phased Launch

**Critical Path to Commercial Service Launch**

A realistic timeline for third operator entry, assuming regulatory pre-approval and spectrum availability:

| **Phase** | **Duration** | **Key Milestones** |
|---|---|---|
| **Pre-licensing** | 2-3 months | Initial business planning, financial structure, technical team recruitment, Honiara office establishment |
| **License application** | 3-6 months | Formal application submission, TCSI evaluation, competitive selection (if applicable), license issuance |
| **Spectrum assignment** | 1-2 months | Coordinate with service license, publish spectrum allocation, fee payment |
| **Network planning** | 3-4 months | RF planning, site surveys, backhaul design, equipment procurement (GSM core, BTS, antennas, backhaul terminals) |
| **Build Phase 1** | 6-9 months | Build 30-50 sites in Honiara and provincial capitals, establish network operations center, TMSC, and main data center |
| **OSS/BSS deployment** | 3-6 months (concurrent with build) | System design, configuration, integration testing, staff training |
| **Interconnection agreement** | 2-3 months (concurrent with build) | Good-faith negotiation with existing operators, TCSI arbitration if needed, implementation of handover points |
| **Launch preparation** | 2-3 months | Final network acceptance testing, commercial partner activation (agents/dealers), marketing campaign |
| ****Commercial launch** | **Month 18-24** | **Begin service provision in Honiara and select provincial centers** |
| **Phase 2 expansion** | 12-24 months | Expand to secondary cities and rural areas per license milestones |

**Total timeline to meaningful market presence: 18-24 months from license award to commercial launch, with profitability likely 3-5 years out depending on capital structure and market response.**

### Market Entry Risks and Success Factors

**Identified Challenges Specific to Solomon Islands**:[25][26][19]

1. **Geographic dispersion**: Nearly 1,000 islands with 80% rural population requiring expensive satellite or microwave backhaul
2. **Limited purchasing power**: GDP per capita USD $1,517 (2013 data) creates affordability pressure; high churn requires aggressive cost structure
3. **Infrastructure duplication**: Costly for new entrant to build from zero; shared tower arrangements and backhaul partnerships critical
4. **Small absolute market**: ~700,000 population (2023); total addressable market may support only 2-3 operators; market saturation risk
5. **Incumbent infrastructure advantage**: Existing operators own established sites, backhaul, and skilled workforce

**Success factors based on Pacific operator case studies**:[27][25]

- **Aggressive urban rollout**: Achieve rapid market share in Honiara and capitals through superior service quality and lower pricing
- **Financial partnerships**: Secure equity/debt financing from development finance institutions (ADB, IFC) as accessed by Digicel PNG and Bemobile
- **Network efficiency**: Leverage efficient equipment (small cells for densification rather than full towers), shared backhaul agreements
- **Adjacent services**: Bundle mobile money, international roaming, broadband to offset high churn and build customer lifetime value
- **Local partnerships**: Joint ventures or licensing agreements with established local businesses for distribution and operations

## **1. Regulatory and Licensing Requirements**

### **1.1 Sector Regulator**

The **Telecommunications Commission of Solomon Islands (TCSI)** is the independent statutory authority responsible for regulation of the telecommunications sector under the **Telecommunications Act 2009**. The Commission oversees the licensing, spectrum management, compliance, and enforcement of the Act. ([TCSI][1])

### **1.2 Licensing Framework**

To legally operate a telecommunications network in the Solomon Islands:

* **Service Licence:** No person or entity may provide telecommunications services without a **service licence** (class or individual) issued by TCSI. ([TCSI][2])

  * A **class licence** authorizes standard telecommunications service provision and is obtained by submitting the prescribed registration form. If qualifying criteria are met, registration typically takes effect 45 days after filing for the licence. ([TCSI][3])
  * An **individual licence** is issued when unique conditions or exclusive rights must be specified, and is in place for specific operators. ([TCSI][4])

* **Radio Spectrum Licence:** A separate application is required for use of radio frequencies to operate wireless services. Service licences do **not** confer rights to use spectrum; applicants must apply for a spectrum licence under the Telecommunications Act. ([TCSI][3])

TCSI can prescribe general and special conditions, and has authority to suspend or revoke licences for non-compliance. ([TCSI][4])

### **1.3 SIM Card Registration**

The **Telecommunications (Subscriber Registration) Directives 2023** establish requirements for providers to register SIM subscribers and maintain a **National Subscriber Registry (NSR)** controlled by TCSI. Licenced service providers must collect subscriber identification (name, date of birth, address and valid ID) and maintain electronic records. ([Solomon Islands Government][5])

### **1.4 Interconnection**

Interconnection arrangements between networks are required under Solomon Islands regulatory policy to allow subscribers on one network to communicate with subscribers on another. The TCSI enforces interconnection obligations and timelines between operators. ([SIBC Online][6])

### **1.5 Telephone Numbering**

Mobile numbers and geographic allocations are administered by TCSI. Numbers are allocated to licensed operators in blocks (for example, blocks of 10,000 mobile numbers). ([Sent Unified Messaging API][7])

The spectrum award process in the Solomon Islands is thus not solely a financial transaction but a key policy tool for universal service delivery.B. Spectrum Allocation Framework and StrategyTCSI is tasked with developing the National Spectrum Plan, allocating frequency bands, and producing frequency licenses suitable for use in the Solomon Islands, ensuring compliance with international standards set by the International Telecommunication Union (ITU).3 In an archipelago setting, securing access to low-band frequencies, such as 700 MHz or 800 MHz, is essential for cost-effective, long-reach coverage necessary to achieve the ambitious SINBIP goals.4An effective strategy for the third entrant must incorporate advocacy for a regulatory mechanism that guarantees access to this crucial low-band spectrum. Without access to low-band spectrum, a third operator's ability to challenge the incumbents outside Honiara will be severely constrained.C. ### Main Research Output

# Launching a Third Mobile Operator in Solomon Islands: Operational Realities and Strategic Analysis

A third mobile network operator in Solomon Islands faces a challenging but not impossible path to market. The regulatory framework permits entry, spectrum capacity exists in higher bands, and infrastructure improvements like the Coral Sea Cable have reduced backhaul costs. However, the economics are severe: a market of **~700,000 people already served by two operators** likely cannot support three profitable networks without significant consolidation or cost innovation. Realistically, a greenfield entrant would require **US$40-70 million in capital**, **30-42 months to launch**, and would need to capture **40-50% market share** to break even—a target that took Digicel years to achieve even in markets where it was first to challenge monopolies.

The strategic play for any new entrant lies not in traditional MNO economics but in leveraging existing infrastructure partnerships (particularly SATSOL's connectivity assets and the government-funded 161-tower SINBIP project) alongside mobile money integration through platforms like IumiCash to differentiate on financial inclusion rather than pure connectivity.

---

## The regulatory pathway exists but requires proactive engagement

The Telecommunications Commission Solomon Islands (TCSI), established under the **Telecommunications Act 2009**, operates a liberalized framework with no current exclusivity protections for incumbents. Our Telekom's controversial 15-year monopoly license—which famously blocked Digicel's 2006-2007 entry attempt via High Court injunction—expired around 2018.

Two licensing pathways exist for market entry. A **Class Licence** offers the faster route at just **45 days** from application to registration, suitable for ISPs or potentially MVNO arrangements. However, a full Mobile Network Operator license requires an **Individual Licence** through a competitive tender process that TCSI must initiate—there is no standing application process. The 2010 tender that awarded Bmobile its license demonstrates this is achievable, but prospective entrants must engage directly with TCSI and the Ministry of Communication and Aviation to gauge government appetite for a third operator.

Spectrum availability is more favorable than commonly assumed. While prime low-band frequencies (700, 850, 900 MHz) are already assigned to incumbents, **significant capacity remains available** in 1800, 2100, 2300, and 2500 MHz bands according to TCSI's 2018 ITU presentation. These higher bands are adequate for urban 4G/5G deployment but require denser tower networks for equivalent rural coverage—a critical consideration given Solomon Islands' dispersed geography.

---

## Network architecture must embrace hybrid connectivity models

Deploying a network across Solomon Islands' **1,000+ island archipelago** demands abandoning conventional terrestrial-only architecture. The geographic reality is stark: 80% of the population lives in rural areas scattered across islands separated by significant maritime distances, with limited grid power and challenging logistics for equipment transport and maintenance.

The **Coral Sea Cable System**, operational since January 2020, transformed the international connectivity equation. This 4,700km submarine cable connecting Honiara to Sydney via PNG delivers **40 Tbps total capacity** at dramatically lower per-GB costs than satellite alone. The **Solomon Islands Domestic Network** further extends fiber to Auki, Noro, and Taro, providing backbone connectivity to secondary population centers.

For inter-island backhaul beyond fiber landing points, operators must choose between traditional VSAT (550-650ms latency, $500-2,000 monthly per site) and the game-changing option of **Starlink LEO satellite** (20-40ms latency, licensed in Solomon Islands since August 2024). SATSOL holds exclusive authorized reseller rights for Starlink nationally and has pioneered the "Data Garden" model—combining Starlink backhaul with solar power and community WiFi hotspots for remote village connectivity. This infrastructure could support a new entrant's outer-island coverage strategy without massive capital deployment.

The government-funded **Solomon Islands National Broadband Infrastructure Project (SINBIP)** adds another dimension. This Chinese-funded initiative is deploying **161 mobile tower sites** across all provinces, with 14 sites operational by September 2024 and completion expected by 2026. While currently partnered with Our Telekom, tower-sharing arrangements could potentially enable a third operator to leverage this infrastructure rather than duplicating it.

Power infrastructure remains the persistent challenge. Off-grid sites require **hybrid solar-battery-diesel systems** costing $80,000-150,000 per remote tower site, with ongoing fuel logistics adding significant operational complexity. Equipment must be cyclone-rated (Category 5) and elevated for flood protection, while salt air accelerates corrosion, reducing equipment lifespan compared to continental deployments.

---

## Competitive dynamics favor a technology differentiation strategy

The current duopoly reveals exploitable gaps. **Our Telekom** dominates with approximately **65% market share** and coverage across all nine provinces, but its 4G network remains largely Honiara-centric. The operator benefits from 97% ownership by the Solomon Islands National Provident Fund, creating strong government relationships and investment backing, plus a vertically integrated position through its M-SELEN mobile money service (20,000+ users since June 2023 launch).

**Bmobile-Vodafone** occupies the challenger position with roughly **35% share** but operates in only **4 of 9 provinces** and critically lacks any 4G network—a significant technology gap. The operator's PNG state-owned parent company has reportedly struggled to invest adequately, with persistent rumors of potential sale. Bmobile's innovative November 2023 partnership with **Lynk Global** for satellite-to-phone SMS service represents an attempt to differentiate through coverage extension, but hasn't reversed its competitive decline.

Customer pain points consistently cited include: rural coverage gaps ("You can't even get phone reception past Visale"), service unreliability in provinces, high data costs relative to income levels, and limited 4G availability outside the capital. **60% of Solomon Islanders are under 25**, creating strong latent demand for data services that current operators haven't fully captured.

The most compelling differentiation opportunity lies in rapid 4G/5G deployment across provincial capitals where Bmobile offers only 2G/3G and Our Telekom's 4G footprint hasn't extended. A new entrant deploying modern network technology across secondary cities could position as the "technology leader" rather than competing purely on price—a strategy that proved effective for Digicel in markets where it could leapfrog incumbent technology.

---

## Customer acquisition without number portability requires dual-SIM exploitation

Solomon Islands lacks mobile number portability, meaning customers cannot transfer their existing numbers to a new operator. This creates a substantial barrier to switching but not an insurmountable one given regional market dynamics.

**Dual-SIM phone penetration** across developing markets averages **85%** according to GeoPoll surveys, with Asia-Pacific leading global adoption. Customers maintain multiple SIMs for coverage hedging (different operators cover different areas), price arbitrage (voice vs. data optimization), and reliability backup. This means a new entrant competes for the "second SIM slot" rather than requiring customers to abandon existing numbers entirely.

Digicel's Pacific expansion provides the acquisition playbook: within 8 months of PNG entry, **SIM prices fell 80%** and call rates dropped 60%. Aggressive price disruption combined with superior coverage drove rapid adoption. Fiji's experience adds nuance—Digicel captured only 32% market share against entrenched Vodafone, demonstrating that first-mover advantage in established markets proves difficult to overcome.

Effective acquisition tactics from comparable markets include:
- **Free or near-free SIM distribution** at launch
- **On-network calling incentives** that create network effects within social groups
- **Mobile money sign-up bonuses** that demonstrate immediate utility
- **Sports and community sponsorship** building brand recognition
- **Enterprise prioritization** targeting lower-churn corporate accounts before mass consumer marketing

The **IumiCash partnership opportunity** deserves particular attention. Unlike M-SELEN (tied to Our Telekom SIMs), IumiCash operates as a **telco-agnostic mobile money platform** with CBSI Money Transfer Service licensing. A new operator could integrate IumiCash as its mobile money solution, immediately offering customers a proven payment platform without years of development—while simultaneously creating a differentiation point against Our Telekom's closed ecosystem.

---

## SATSOL and IumiCash represent strategic partnership leverage

**SATSOL** (Satellite Solutions Solomon Islands) offers the most compelling infrastructure partnership opportunity. This locally-owned company holds the highest teleport in Guadalcanal, operates a modern underground fiber network in Honiara, maintains ISO 9001:2015 and ISO/IEC 27001:2013 certifications, and serves as the exclusive Starlink reseller nationally. SATSOL already provides approximately **20% of Solomon Islands' internet bandwidth** and maintains relationships with government agencies, international NGOs, and major businesses.

A new MNO could leverage SATSOL's infrastructure for: backhaul connectivity via Starlink for outer-island sites, data center colocation in Honiara and Sydney (Equinix), enterprise customer relationships, and potentially the "Data Garden" community connectivity model that combines solar power, Starlink, and WiFi hotspots for remote village coverage at **$15,000-30,000 per site** versus $80,000-150,000 for traditional tower builds.

**IumiCash Limited** (Company No. 20200170) operates independently from any bank or telco—importantly, it is **not a BSP subsidiary** despite some market confusion. The company holds CBSI Money Transfer Service licensing obtained through the regulatory sandbox process, operates across Solomon Islands and Samoa, and charges a flat **1% transaction fee** on transfers. IumiCash's telco-agnostic positioning means a new operator could offer mobile money services immediately rather than building competing infrastructure from scratch.

The **CBSI regulatory environment** actively supports such integration. The Regulatory Sandbox Framework launched April 2022 provides controlled testing for fintech innovations, while the recently launched **SOLATS** (Solomon Islands Automated Transfer System) real-time gross settlement system enables electronic and mobile payment integration at the national infrastructure level.

## Financial realities suggest alternative entry models merit consideration

The capital and timeline analysis reveals challenging economics for traditional greenfield MNO entry. Comparable Pacific market investments ranged from **US$35 million (Vanuatu)** to **US$150 million initial + $340 million planned (PNG)**, suggesting Solomon Islands would require **US$40-70 million** for comprehensive coverage competing with incumbents.

Realistic timeline from license application to commercial launch: **30-42 months**, including 6-12 months for regulatory approval, 6-9 months for equipment procurement, 6-12 months for infrastructure deployment, and 2-3 months for testing and soft launch.

Break-even analysis reveals the core challenge. With estimated monthly ARPU of **US$4-8** and fixed costs of approximately $13-17 million annually (combining amortized CAPEX and OPEX), a new entrant would need **270,000-350,000 subscribers** to reach break-even—equivalent to **48-63% of the total mobile market** at current 80% penetration. Path to profitability: **5-10 years** under moderate assumptions, longer if price wars erode margins.

Acquiring existing infrastructure, spectrum licenses, and ~119,000 subscribers could provide faster market entry at potentially lower total cost than greenfield development.

## Lessons from Pacific third-operator launches reveal success patterns

Digicel's Pacific expansion from 2006-2009 provides the most relevant case studies, though with important methodological caveats about small-market generalizability and survivor bias.

**PNG success (91% market share achieved):** Digicel entered a market with just 2% mobile penetration, 100,000 subscribers, and K250 ($80) SIM cards. Within 8 months, SIM prices dropped to K5-10, subscriptions grew to 2.4 million by 2011 (2,400% increase), and mobile penetration reached 34%. The World Bank credited liberalization with contributing nearly 1% to PNG's GDP.

**Fiji limitation (32% market share, #2 position):** Digicel's only Pacific market where it failed to achieve dominance. Vodafone Fiji's 14-year head start and entrenched customer relationships proved impossible to overcome despite aggressive pricing and coverage expansion.

**Vanuatu playbook ($35M investment, 65% share):** Investment in state-of-art network covering all communities for the first time, combined with market-first innovations (per-second billing, rollover minutes, international roaming), achieved market leadership against TVL.

**Solomon Islands blocked entry (2006-2007):** Digicel applied for a license in December 2006 and began transporting tower equipment, but Our Telekom obtained a High Court injunction citing its 15-year exclusivity license. This legal barrier—now expired—prevented what would likely have been a similar market transformation.

The consistent success pattern across markets: **dramatic price disruption at entry** (40-80% reductions), **aggressive coverage build prioritizing population over geography**, **community integration through employment and philanthropy**, and **mobile money integration** addressing unbanked populations. However, the Fiji case demonstrates that these tactics cannot guarantee success against entrenched incumbents with established customer relationships.

---

## Conclusion: strategic viability depends on non-traditional approaches

A third mobile operator in Solomon Islands faces severe but not impossible economics. The regulatory pathway exists, spectrum is available, infrastructure has improved dramatically with the Coral Sea Cable, and customer pain points with incumbents create market opportunity. However, traditional greenfield MNO economics require market share levels that proved difficult to achieve even for Digicel's well-funded, operationally excellent Pacific expansion.

The most viable strategic approaches prioritize:

1. **Acquisition over greenfield**: Bmobile's apparent strategic challenges and limited geographic footprint make it a potential acquisition target, providing existing infrastructure, spectrum, and subscribers at potentially lower total investment than building from scratch.

2. **Infrastructure leverage over capital deployment**: SATSOL's connectivity assets, the SINBIP tower project, and Starlink availability reduce the need for massive independent infrastructure investment.

3. **Mobile money differentiation over pure connectivity competition**: Partnership with IumiCash enables immediate financial services differentiation against Our Telekom's closed M-SELEN ecosystem.

4. **Technology leadership over coverage parity**: Rapid 4G/5G deployment in provincial capitals where Bmobile offers only 2G/3G creates differentiation without requiring island-by-island coverage matching Our Telekom.

5. **Strategic patience over rapid profitability**: Any entrant must accept 5-10+ year horizon to profitability, suggesting strategic rationale (geopolitical positioning, regional platform building, development impact) may matter as much as pure financial returns—as Australia's $1.33 billion contribution to Telstra's Digicel Pacific acquisition demonstrated.

The window for entry is open, but the path to sustainable profitability requires creative business models that transcend conventional MNO economics.
