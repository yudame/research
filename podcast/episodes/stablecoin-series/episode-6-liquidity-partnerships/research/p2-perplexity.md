# Stablecoin Liquidity Infrastructure: Market Makers, Exchanges, and Multi-Chain Strategies in 2025

The stablecoin ecosystem has evolved into a complex, multi-layered financial infrastructure that depends critically on sophisticated liquidity provisioning across centralized exchanges, decentralized protocols, and blockchain networks. As of late 2025, the stablecoin market has reached $305 billion in circulation, processing over $15.6 trillion in quarterly on-chain transfers—exceeding Visa's settlement volumes[1][84]. This expansion has been undergirded by institutional market makers, exchange partnerships, and cross-chain liquidity strategies that together maintain price stability and ensure deep order books across trading venues. This report provides a comprehensive analysis of how stablecoin liquidity infrastructure functions, the role of key institutional participants, regulatory drivers shaping the landscape, and empirical evidence on market structure and concentration risks.

The rapid ascent of stablecoins from specialized cryptocurrency trading vehicles to mainstream payment infrastructure has required fundamental innovations in how liquidity is provisioned and managed. Rather than relying solely on centralized intermediaries, modern stablecoin systems employ a layered architecture combining institutional market makers, multiple exchange venues, arbitrage mechanisms, and permissionless decentralized protocols. Understanding this infrastructure is essential for regulators, financial institutions, and market participants seeking to navigate an increasingly complex ecosystem where technological capability and regulatory compliance are becoming inseparable.

## The Role and Functions of Market Makers in Stablecoin Ecosystems

Market makers represent the foundational layer of stablecoin liquidity infrastructure, providing continuous bid and ask quotes that enable rapid execution, reduce price slippage, and maintain peg stability across venues. Unlike speculative trading, market making for stablecoins involves sophisticated operations that balance inventory management, cross-exchange arbitrage, and the coordination of positions across multiple blockchain networks and trading pairs. The function is particularly critical for stablecoins because their entire value proposition depends on maintaining a tight peg to their reference asset, whether that asset is the U.S. dollar, euro, or other backing collateral.

Market makers in the stablecoin space employ advanced trading algorithms that analyze market conditions, order book depths, and real-time price discrepancies across numerous venues. They profit from the bid-ask spread—the difference between the highest price buyers are willing to pay and the lowest price sellers will accept—while simultaneously providing the essential service of creating liquidity that allows traders to execute transactions quickly and with minimal price impact[7]. For institutional-grade stablecoins like USDC and USDT, this process ensures that price deviations remain minimal, typically within a few basis points of the intended peg. The sophistication of this operation cannot be understated; market makers must continuously monitor Ethereum transaction costs, Solana fees, TRON capacity, and the relative liquidity across dozens of trading pairs on hundreds of exchanges, adjusting their positions in real time to optimize returns while maintaining the infrastructure upon which the entire ecosystem depends[7].

The firm supports over 350 spot and CFD trading pairs, enhancing trading efficiency across centralized and decentralized venues. Wintermute's business model reflects the capital-intensive nature of modern market making; the firm must maintain sufficient inventory across multiple chains, manage counterparty relationships with exchanges and issuers, and execute trades with sub-millisecond precision to capture the spreads that define profitability.

**Jump Trading**, operating through its Jump Crypto division, brings extensive experience from traditional finance into the cryptocurrency market, leveraging high-frequency algorithmic trading technologies and advanced research capabilities to provide deep liquidity for both spot and derivative markets[7][10]. The firm's ability to seamlessly move between traditional markets and decentralized protocols has established it as a critical bridge between the old and new financial worlds. ## Centralized Exchange Listing: Requirements, Timelines, and Strategic Importance

Centralized exchanges (CEXs) remain the primary venue where retail and institutional traders interact with stablecoins, and listing on major exchanges like Binance and Coinbase represents a critical milestone for any stablecoin seeking widespread adoption and liquidity. The listing process is not merely a technical exercise; it reflects the exercise of gatekeeping power by exchanges over which assets receive access to their user base, and it incorporates assessments of legal compliance, financial stability, and market readiness. Understanding the listing requirements and process is therefore essential to understanding how stablecoins achieve market penetration and liquidity at scale.

Coinbase, one of the most rigorous exchanges in its listing standards, operates a formal application process that captures the complexity and rigor now expected in the institutional ecosystem. Project teams submit an online questionnaire capturing key information including whitepapers, team backgrounds, tokenomics, links to source code, block explorers, and third-party security audits[6]. The application then undergoes business evaluation assessing market demand, community traction, and technical requirements for integration. Most critically, the asset is evaluated by a core review process encompassing three dimensions: legal assessment of whether trading the token would be classified as a securities transaction in relevant jurisdictions, compliance and risk mitigation review of token distribution and on-chain activity for financial crime risks, and technical security evaluation of contract code, design, and operational risks[6].

The timeline for Coinbase listings reflects both the complexity of the review process and the importance of moving quickly. On average, due diligence takes approximately one week, and trading can be enabled within two weeks of approval[6]. However, the total timeline can vary dramatically based on factors such as token complexity, whether Coinbase already supports the network, project team responsiveness, and the technical work required for custody and trading. Tokens on supported networks—which as of late 2025 include Ethereum, Base, Solana, Arbitrum, Optimism, Polygon, and Avalanche—can be supported faster than new or unsupported chains, since new blockchain support requires dedicated engineering teams to build custom node infrastructure, validate consensus models, and develop new APIs and compliance controls from the ground up[6].

The evaluation framework reveals the institutional gatekeeping that now characterizes CEX participation. Beyond core legal, compliance, and technical reviews, Coinbase also assesses market factors including trading demand, market capitalization, liquidity, traction measured through holder numbers and active wallets, and qualitative signals such as community sentiment and team track record[6]. These evaluations are not conducted solely by compliance teams; they involve product, legal, and technical specialists who collectively assess whether a token represents a sound addition to the exchange's offering. The strategic importance of CEX listings cannot be overstated for stablecoins. ## Decentralized Exchange Integration and Automated Market Maker Mechanics

While centralized exchanges remain the largest venue for stablecoin trading, decentralized exchanges (DEXs) have emerged as critical infrastructure for permissionless trading, yield provision, and composability with decentralized finance (DeFi) applications. DEX trading represents a fundamentally different model from CEX trading; rather than relying on centralized order matching, DEXs use Automated Market Makers (AMMs) that enable trading against algorithmic pricing mechanisms embedded in smart contracts. Understanding DEX infrastructure is essential to understanding modern stablecoin liquidity because DEXs now collectively process substantial trading volumes and serve as the execution layer for a growing array of sophisticated financial applications.

**Uniswap** dominates the DEX landscape, accounting for approximately 55 percent of all DEX transactions as of 2024[8]. The protocol's dominance reflects both network effects—more liquidity attracts more traders, which attracts more liquidity providers—and continuous technical innovation. Uniswap V3, launched in 2021, introduced concentrated liquidity mechanisms that allow liquidity providers to specify custom price ranges for their deposits, potentially increasing capital efficiency by up to 4,000 times compared to earlier versions[8]. This innovation transformed the economics of stablecoin liquidity provision on DEXs. Rather than having capital locked across the entire price spectrum even when trading occurs only in a narrow band, concentrated liquidity allows providers to concentrate their capital in the range where trading actually occurs, earning more fees with less capital while reducing slippage for traders.

The mechanics of concentrated liquidity are particularly important for stablecoin pairs where price ranges are predictable and narrow. A liquidity provider might deposit USDC and USDT into a Uniswap V3 pool but specify that their capital will only be active in the price range of $0.9999 to $1.0001, capturing fees from that narrow but heavily-traded band. When price movements exceed the provider's specified range, the position effectively exits (all USDC, no USDT if price rises, or all USDT, no USDC if price falls), requiring active management by the provider to maintain exposure[8]. This active management requirement represents a meaningful friction compared to passive liquidity provision, but for professional market makers and sophisticated liquidity providers, the enhanced fee capture justifies the operational complexity.

Uniswap's multi-chain expansion has been critical for stablecoin liquidity. The protocol operates across multiple networks including Ethereum, Arbitrum, Optimism, Polygon, and Base, providing users with options to optimize for different fee structures and transaction speeds depending on their preferences[8]. This multi-chain presence reflects the reality that stablecoin liquidity is now inherently fragmented across blockchain networks, and DEXs must adapt by offering the same protocols across these venues to capture liquidity wherever users trade. The challenge is that capital efficiency depends partly on achieving critical mass on each chain; if Uniswap V3 on an obscure L2 has insufficient liquidity, concentrated liquidity positions will sit inactive, earning no fees.

**Curve Finance** occupies a specialized but critically important role in stablecoin infrastructure as a low-slippage trading platform for pegged assets[32][84]. Curve's bonding curve algorithm is optimized specifically for trading between assets with similar prices—stablecoins paired with stablecoins, or wrapped versions of the same asset—enabling efficient trades with minimal price impact. This specialization makes Curve the dominant venue for stablecoin-to-stablecoin trades and essential infrastructure for arbitrage operations that maintain peg stability across different stablecoin variants[5][8]. Curve's TVL has historically peaked at $19 billion and has remained substantial even during crypto winter periods, reflecting the stability of revenues from stablecoin trading fees[32].

Curve's competitive position in stablecoin trading reflects a fundamental economic principle: **the platform that minimizes slippage for a particular use case tends to capture disproportionate volume and liquidity** for that use case. Because Curve specializes in low-slippage stablecoin swaps, traders and arbitrageurs have strong incentives to route their orders through Curve rather than general-purpose DEXs. This specialization has evolved into a governance dynamic where stablecoin issuers and associated entities have competed to influence Curve governance through accumulation of its governance token (CRV) in order to direct liquidity incentives toward their preferred stablecoins—a phenomenon known as the "Curve Wars"[32]. Major protocols including Convex Finance, Yearn Finance, and StakeDAO have raced to accumulate voting power, recognizing that controlling Curve's incentive distribution translates directly to controlling where liquidity flows.

**PancakeSwap** and other chain-specific DEXs provide regional liquidity infrastructure, with PancakeSwap dominating BNB Chain trading and offering competitive yields for liquidity providers through its token incentive system[21][24]. The existence of these regional DEXs reflects the reality that each blockchain network has distinct user bases, fee structures, and community preferences, making a one-size-fits-all approach to DEX infrastructure impossible. Liquidity providers can earn different returns for providing the same stablecoin pair on different DEXs and chains, creating arbitrage opportunities in the liquidity provision itself.

For stablecoins specifically, DEX infrastructure serves multiple critical functions beyond simple token swaps. DEXs act as price discovery mechanisms, continuously determining the market-clearing price for stablecoin pairs based on supply and demand dynamics. This price discovery function is particularly important during market stress, when DEX prices can diverge from primary market par values[15]. When this occurs, arbitrageurs can profit from the discrepancy by purchasing stablecoins in the secondary market (DEX) and redeeming them for dollars in the primary market (directly from the issuer), driving prices back toward parity. The efficiency of this arbitrage process depends on the depth of DEX liquidity, the speed of blockchain transactions, and the issuer's willingness and ability to process redemptions quickly[52].

DEXs also provide essential infrastructure for decentralized finance (DeFi) applications that compose stablecoins into lending protocols, yield-farming systems, and other applications. Without permissionless DEX trading, DeFi would be far less functional; users and applications would have limited ability to rebalance holdings or access specific stablecoin variants needed for particular applications. This composability—the ability of stablecoins to flow seamlessly into and through decentralized applications—has become a critical competitive advantage for the most liquid stablecoins and the DEXs that serve them.

## Primary and Secondary Market Structures: Access and Arbitrage Efficiency

The architecture of stablecoin markets involves a critical distinction between primary and secondary markets. The primary market is the venue where stablecoins are originally issued and redeemed directly with the issuer at par value; the secondary market encompasses all trading venues (CEXs and DEXs) where stablecoins trade at market-determined prices[15][52]. Understanding the structure and constraints of each market is essential to understanding how price stability is maintained and where fragility emerges.

Most fiat-backed stablecoin issuers restrict direct access to the primary market to a small set of institutional participants who meet strict KYC/AML requirements, agree to minimum transaction thresholds (often $100,000 or more), and operate in approved jurisdictions[15][52][62]. Tether is particularly restrictive, requiring a reported minimum of $100,000 USDT per mint transaction on-chain, while Circle allows institutional customers to mint and redeem USDC but restricts the process to banking hours during the U.S. business day[15]. This operational constraint proved problematic during the March 2023 crisis when Circle's primary market operations were suspended over a weekend due to SVB exposure, leaving retail users unable to redeem stablecoins for dollars and forcing secondary market prices to trade at a discount[15][62].

The restriction of primary market access creates a two-tiered system where institutional users with direct redemption rights effectively face a hard ceiling of $1 on the price they would pay (why pay more than $1 when you can redeem directly for $1?) and a hard floor of $1 on the price they would accept (why sell for less than $1 when you can mint directly for $1?). This creates automatic arbitrage opportunities for these privileged participants but offers no such protections for retail users who can only trade on secondary markets. Retail users face the full risk of market prices diverging from par value and have no contractual right to redemption at $1[62]. During stress events when selling pressure exceeds available liquidity, retail users may be forced to accept prices materially below $1 to exit their positions.

Empirical research has documented how primary market access concentration affects stablecoin stability. The study analyzing USDT and USDC found that **USDT has approximately 6 active arbitrageurs in an average month, while USDC has approximately 521 active arbitrageurs**[52]. This dramatic difference reflects both issuers' deliberate choices about who to admit to primary market redemptions and the different business models: Tether's approach prioritizes creating a "squeezed" liquidity situation where major arbitrageurs capture outsized spreads, while Circle's approach emphasizes distributed arbitrage that keeps secondary market prices tightly pegged to par value. The empirical evidence supports this interpretation; USDT secondary market prices show a median discount of 11 basis points compared to 1 basis point for USDC, reflecting the less efficient arbitrage process[52].

Yet this relationship reveals a counterintuitive dynamic: **concentrated arbitrage is efficient for peg maintenance but fragile for price stability during stress**. When few arbitrageurs control primary market access, they can quickly move large volumes to capitalize on small deviations, keeping prices in secondary markets tightly pegged. However, this same concentration means that if the reserve assets become questioned or difficult to liquidate, the arbitrageurs lose their ability to execute redemptions, and the peg breaks suddenly. Distributed arbitrage like USDC's model provides less pressure for peg maintenance under normal conditions but proves more resilient during stress because many independent arbitrageurs can respond to discourage runs[52].

This tension between efficiency and stability during normal periods versus fragility during stress appears throughout stablecoin infrastructure. The system that most tightly controls secondary market prices is not necessarily the system most likely to maintain price stability under the most extreme conditions. Rather, the most stable systems appear to be those that balance multiple mechanisms: sufficient arbitrageur access to maintain peg discipline under normal conditions, adequate reserve asset liquidity and diversification to support redemptions under stress, and regulatory oversight that ensures transparency and accountability.

## Regulatory Requirements and Their Impact on Liquidity Infrastructure

The passage of the GENIUS Act in the United States in July 2025 and the prior implementation of the EU's Markets in Crypto-Assets Regulation (MiCA) in 2024 have substantially altered the regulatory environment for stablecoin issuers and, by extension, the liquidity infrastructure required to support them[19][72]. These frameworks have moved stablecoin regulation from a patchwork of interpretations and informal guidance to explicit statutory requirements, with direct implications for market-making, exchange listing, and cross-chain deployment strategies.

The GENIUS Act establishes a comprehensive framework creating the designation "payment stablecoin" for tokens redeemable at fixed value and usable for payments or settlements[19][72]. The Act permits issuance only by regulated entities—OCC-chartered non-bank issuers, subsidiaries of insured depository institutions, or approved state entities—and mandates one-to-one reserve backing with high-quality, liquid assets[19][72]. Critically, the Act prohibits stablecoin issuers from offering yield or interest on holdings, removing potential competition with bank deposits while also simplifying the regulatory framework[19][72]. The Act requires monthly public attestations and annual independent audits of reserve assets, establishing transparency standards substantially more rigorous than previously existed for any stablecoin[19][72].

These requirements have immediate implications for liquidity infrastructure. Market makers must now contend with a regulated stablecoin market that explicitly prohibits yield-bearing stablecoins, eliminating one potential competitive vector. Institutions cannot offer rewards, interest, or other incentives to attract stablecoin holdings; all competitive differentiation must come through network effects, exchange access, or integration with payment systems. This regulatory constraint has likely affected the competitive positioning of newer stablecoins like Ethena's USDe, which derives value partly from providing yield to stablecoins holders through its delta-neutral hedging strategy—a model that may fall outside traditional regulation and may face restrictions if regulators expand the definition of "payment stablecoin"[39][42][72].

The GENIUS Act also creates a pathpath for dual supervision: stablecoin issuers with circulation exceeding $10 billion must face dual federal oversight by the Federal Reserve, OCC, or National Credit Union Administration in addition to any state-level supervision[19]. This dual supervision requirement has implications for reserve management and liquidity provisioning; the regulatory attention faced by very large issuers like Tether and Circle means their treasury decisions face scrutiny from banking regulators concerned about systemic risk[19].

MiCA, the EU's Markets in Crypto-Assets Regulation, takes a different but complementary approach, focusing heavily on e-money tokens (EMTs) and asset-reference tokens (ARTs) while establishing a licensing requirement for crypto-asset service providers[22][44]. MiCA requires stablecoin issuers to maintain minimum capital buffers, ensures adequate reserve liquidity, and mandates governance structures with clear accountability[22][44]. Notably, MiCA grants to regulators the authority to impose limits on stablecoin issuance if they determine that financial stability is at risk[22][44]. This provision creates a legal framework within which regulators can intervene more directly than in the U.S. framework, though as of late 2025 such intervention has not occurred.

The regulatory fragmentation created by GENIUS and MiCA has already begun reshaping market structure. Major exchanges have begun delisting non-compliant stablecoins from European users, with many stablecoins including USDT initially facing potential delisting until compliance pathways became clearer[44][51]. USDC benefited substantially from MiCA's 2024 implementation because Circle proactively pursued MiCA licensing and compliance, positioning USDC as the regulatory standard for euros and dollars in European markets[44]. This regulatory positioning has translated into market advantage; Circle's market cap for USDC grew from approximately $42.4 billion in 2021 to $77 billion by December 2025, partly driven by regulatory clarity[25][72].

Tether, despite long-standing concerns about reserve transparency and regulatory compliance, maintained its market dominance partly because USDT's extreme liquidity and prevalence across venues created switching costs that prevented wholesale migration to alternatives[5][13][16][26]. However, the GENIUS Act and regulatory developments globally have created pressure on Tether to improve reserve transparency and comply with new standards. Tether's Q2 2025 attestation report showed assets exceeding liabilities and demonstrated holdings of over $127 billion in U.S. Treasury securities, positioning the firm as one of the largest holders of U.S. government debt globally[26]. This transparency improvement, while potentially driven partly by regulatory pressure, has enhanced market confidence in USDT's backing.

## Empirical Analysis of Market Structure and Liquidity Metrics

Understanding stablecoin liquidity infrastructure requires examination of concrete metrics including trading volumes, bid-ask spreads, market depth, and slippage across different venues and blockchain networks. These metrics reveal how liquidity is distributed, where market power is concentrated, and how effectively infrastructure provisioning maintains price stability.

**Trading Volume and Market Concentration**

Visa's on-chain analytics dashboard, which adjusts for high-frequency trading and inorganic bot activity, reveals the true scale of stablecoin settlement infrastructure. After adjusting for artificial volume inflation from bots and internal smart contract interactions, Visa's research estimates that USDT on TRON alone generated $3.5 trillion in volume on 549.2 million transactions during an observed period, with USDC on Ethereum generating $3.1 trillion across 38.1 million transactions[81]. This data demonstrates that while transaction counts are often cited to show DEX activity, the actual organic volume reflects more concentrated patterns. USDT's dominance by volume is evident, with USDT on Tron representing the single largest stablecoin volume corridor, followed by USDC on Ethereum[81].

This volume concentration reflects both technical characteristics of the chains and market-driven selection. Tron's extreme cost efficiency and mobile-wallet accessibility make it the obvious choice for retail cross-border payments in developing markets[45][48]. Ethereum's programmability and DeFi ecosystem make it the obvious choice for institutional treasury management and sophisticated financial applications[1][4]. Neither dominance is surprising given these technical and economic realities.

**Bid-Ask Spreads and Peg Stability**

S&P Global's analysis of bid-ask spreads for crypto assets compared to traditional finance provides important context for assessing stablecoin market efficiency[14]. For the stablecoins analyzed, bid-ask spreads on major venues like Binance are comparatively low relative to the size of the assets, suggesting reasonably efficient market-making provision[14]. Notably, BTC and ETH on Binance typically display higher bid-ask spreads (as a percentage of price) than comparable large-cap stocks in the S&P 500 like Apple, reflecting lower underlying liquidity in crypto relative to traditional equity markets[14]. For stablecoins specifically, the spreads are tighter because of the specialized DEX infrastructure (particularly Curve) optimized for stablecoin trading, and because the number of arbitrageurs maintaining peg discipline exceeds that for volatile cryptocurrencies[14].

Empirical research on peg stability across stablecoins found that USDC maintains median secondary market price deviations of less than 1 basis point while USDT's median discount is 11 basis points[52]. These differences are economically significant; on $1 million in transactions, 10 basis points amounts to $1,000, a material cost that retail users or smaller institutions must bear. The data strongly suggests that USDC's more distributed arbitrage model provides superior price efficiency under normal conditions, though as noted above this efficiency comes at the cost of less concentrated arbitrage pressure.

**Market Depth and Slippage**

Market depth—the volume of orders available at different distances from the current price—determines how much a large trade can move the market price (slippage). Kaiko Research's analysis of liquidity on Uniswap V3 and Binance for stablecoin pairs revealed that even for pairs involving the most liquid stablecoins, slippage can become material for very large orders[17]. A $10 million trade in USDT on Uniswap V3 might experience measurable slippage depending on the time of day and current liquidity provision levels[17].

These slippage metrics are particularly important for institutional users making large cross-border transfers or treasury management decisions. An institution moving $100 million in stablecoins across venues or chains may incur slippage costs that total millions of dollars. For this reason, institutional users have strong incentives to employ sophisticated order routing algorithms that split large orders across multiple venues, time their execution to avoid high-volatility periods, and utilize off-exchange settlement where available.

## Case Study: The USDC-USDT Competition for Market Dominance

The competitive dynamics between Circle's USDC and Tether's USDT provide a revealing case study in how stablecoin liquidity infrastructure determines market success. While Tether USDT launched earlier and established dominant network effects, Circle's USDC has steadily gained market share through superior transparency, regulatory alignment, and strategic partnerships with institutional infrastructure providers.

As of Q3 2025, the market share gap had narrowed considerably but remained substantial. USDT maintained approximately $175 billion in market capitalization (representing roughly 65% of the stablecoin market) while USDC reached $77 billion (representing roughly 28%)[5][25][26]. However, this comparison understates USDC's competitive position because the growth trajectories diverge sharply. USDT grew from approximately $140 billion at the end of January 2025 to $175 billion by September—a 25% increase over nine months. USDC grew from approximately $44 billion on January 1, 2025 to $77 billion by December 23, 2025—a 75% increase over the year[25]. If these growth rates persist, USDC could achieve parity with USDT within 18-24 months.

The competitive dynamics reveal how liquidity infrastructure advantages can be overcome through regulatory alignment and institutional partnerships. USDT's initial dominance reflected first-mover advantage, extreme liquidity, and early adoption by the world's largest crypto exchange, Binance. However, USDT's historical opacity around reserve backing and regulatory concerns about Tether's business practices created an opening for USDC[16][26]. Circle exploited this opening through an aggressive strategy of pursuing institutional integration:

**Regulatory Compliance**: Circle pursued MiCA compliance from the beginning, positioning USDC as the regulatory standard for compliant stablecoins in the EU[44]. This positioning proved valuable when MiCA's June 2024 implementation forced exchanges to delist non-compliant stablecoins[44].

**Banking Integration**: Circle secured partnerships with major banks and institutional custody providers, making USDC available through traditional banking channels rather than only through crypto-native exchanges[25][28]. The announcement that major banks would integrate USDC settlement signaled institutional confidence.

**Payment Network Integration**: Rather than attempting to compete with Visa and Mastercard, Circle partnered with them, enabling USDC settlement on their networks[25][28]. This integration path proved far more effective than trying to displace existing payment networks.

**Multi-Chain Deployment**: Circle deployed USDC across 28 blockchain networks as of late 2025, ensuring developers could access USDC on virtually any chain they built on[1][25].

**Transparency**: Circle published monthly reserve attestations from the beginning, establishing a transparency standard that forced other issuers to follow[25][26].

These strategies proved highly effective. By December 2025, Circle had not only achieved rapid market share gains but had also solidified USDC's position as the stablecoin of choice for institutional applications, regulatory-focused jurisdictions, and developers building consumer payment applications[25][84]. The fact that Circle maintained higher average prices per USDC in many venues (reflecting lower arbitrage activity and more efficient market-making) suggests institutional users viewed USDC as a superior long-term bet[5].

However, USDT maintained advantages that should not be underestimated. The sheer volume of existing USDT holdings and the deep liquidity across all major venues create network effects that are difficult to overcome. Tether's dominance on TRON (which processes over 75% of all USDT transfers globally) gave it unmatched access to the fastest-growing user base for stablecoins in developing markets[48]. No amount of regulatory compliance or institutional partnerships would have quickly converted billions of retail stablecoins users in Asia, Africa, and Latin America to switch from USDT on Tron.

The USDC-USDT competition reveals that stablecoin market dominance is not determined by a single factor but rather by a combination of liquidity, regulatory positioning, institutional partnerships, and user ecosystem match. There is room in the market for multiple major stablecoins serving different segments: USDT for retail users in developing markets and those seeking maximum liquidity, USDC for institutional applications and regulated financial integration, and Ethena's USDe for sophisticated DeFi participants seeking yield[39][42].

## The Infrastructure Maturation and Future Challenges

By late 2025, stablecoin liquidity infrastructure has achieved a degree of sophistication and institutional integration that would have been unimaginable even five years prior. Market makers operate with millisecond-level precision, managing positions across hundreds of venues and multiple blockchain networks. Exchanges maintain formal listing processes with rigorous compliance requirements. Decentralized protocols provision liquidity at scale through automated algorithms. Regulatory frameworks now explicitly govern stablecoin issuance and reserve management. Institutional financial firms have integrated stablecoins into their core treasury and payment operations.

Yet challenges remain. The fragmentation of liquidity across chains creates inefficiencies that harm users and may ultimately prove unsustainable. The concentration of market-making activity among a small number of firms, while enabling efficient arbitrage under normal conditions, creates fragility if major market makers experience operational disruption. The tiered access to primary markets—where institutions enjoy par-value redemptions while retail users face market prices—creates a two-speed system that advantages sophisticated participants. Regulatory requirements, while improving transparency, also impose compliance costs that smaller projects cannot bear, potentially entrenching dominant players.

The most immediate challenge facing stablecoin liquidity infrastructure is the maintenance of sufficiently deep liquidity across multiple chains and venues to support continued growth in transaction volumes. Current infrastructure has handled the scaling from roughly $500 billion in Q1 2025 to over $1 trillion by mid-year quite well, but further multiples of growth would require continued innovation in market-making, DEX design, and cross-chain protocols. If stablecoin volumes grow as projections suggest—potentially reaching $2-4 trillion annually by 2028[1][4]—the infrastructure must scale proportionally while maintaining price stability and operational resilience.

Second, the resolution of regulatory fragmentation around the world will likely reshape market structure further. The current situation where USDT faces restrictions in the EU while MiCA-compliant stablecoins dominate European markets will likely become more pronounced as other jurisdictions implement stablecoin regulation. The announcement in September 2025 that nine major European banks formed a consortium to launch a euro-denominated stablecoin reflects recognition that regulatory frameworks are creating opportunities for new entrants to challenge U.S. dollar stablecoin dominance[77]. The long-term market structure may include multiple dominant stablecoins pegged to different currencies (USD, EUR, likely JPY and GBP), each serving regional markets and institutional customer bases.

Third, the maturation of yield-bearing stablecoins and tokenized money market funds creates new competitive dynamics that existing liquidity infrastructure must accommodate[58][69]. When stablecoins are combined with yield-bearing protocols, they compete directly with money market funds and short-term deposits, potentially attracting substantial retail and institutional capital. This shift would increase the importance of deep liquidity and price stability; if a yield-bearing stablecoin deppegs, holders lose not only the stability but also the yield rationale for holding it.

## Conclusion: Stablecoin Liquidity Infrastructure as Financial Backbone

Stablecoin liquidity infrastructure has evolved from experimental protocols to a critical component of global financial infrastructure. The combination of institutional market makers providing continuous quotes, centralized exchanges maintaining rigorous listing standards, decentralized protocols offering permissionless trading, and cross-chain bridges connecting fragmented liquidity pools creates a system capable of processing over $15 trillion in quarterly transactions while maintaining price stability measured in basis points.

This infrastructure did not emerge accidentally or through uncoordinated market forces alone. Rather, it reflects deliberate choices by stablecoin issuers, exchange operators, market-making firms, and developers to provision liquidity across multiple layers and venues. Circle's aggressive pursuit of institutional partnerships and regulatory compliance created competitive pressure on Tether to improve reserve transparency and accept tighter operational standards. Market makers competing for volume drove innovations in arbitrage efficiency and automated liquidity provisioning. DEX operators specializing in stablecoin trading (notably Curve) created infrastructure delivering superior user economics. The result is a system characterized by continuous innovation, intense competition, and increasingly sophisticated coordination among participants.

The regulatory environment has shifted from ambiguity and informality to explicit statutory frameworks establishing minimum standards for reserve backing, transparency, and operational resilience. These frameworks, while imposing compliance costs that may favor large, well-capitalized issuers, have also enhanced consumer protection and stability by making explicit what stablecoins must deliver to earn and retain user trust. The GENIUS Act and MiCA represent milestones in the transition of stablecoins from cryptocurrency curiosities to regulated financial instruments integrated into the formal financial system.

Looking forward, stablecoin liquidity infrastructure faces challenges around maintaining efficiency as volumes continue to scale, resolving regulatory fragmentation across jurisdictions, and accommodating new forms of yield-bearing and tokenized financial instruments. However, the fundamental drivers of stablecoin adoption—the need for stable payment instruments, the advantages of 24/7 settlement, and the programmability enabled by blockchain infrastructure—appear structural rather than cyclical. The infrastructure that has been built to provision stablecoin liquidity will likely remain essential infrastructure for global finance for decades to come.
