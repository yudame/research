# Stablecoin Reserve Management and Custody Infrastructure: A Comprehensive Analysis of Regulatory Frameworks, Asset Requirements, and Attestation Standards

The emergence of stablecoins as a significant component of the global financial system has catalyzed unprecedented regulatory activity and operational infrastructure development. With stablecoin transaction volumes reaching approximately $46 trillion annually and market capitalization projections exceeding $4 trillion by 2030, the integrity of reserve management and custody arrangements has become a critical determinant of financial stability and consumer protection[1][62]. This report synthesizes current regulatory requirements, custody infrastructure standards, and attestation methodologies across major jurisdictions, examining how these frameworks address the fundamental challenge of maintaining token stability while ensuring accessibility of backing assets during periods of market stress. The analysis incorporates lessons from recent crisis events, including the March 2023 Silicon Valley Bank failure and its contagion effects on major stablecoins, to evaluate the sufficiency of existing safeguards and identify emerging vulnerabilities in the stablecoin ecosystem.

## The Regulatory Landscape: Convergence Around Reserve-Backed Frameworks

The establishment of comprehensive stablecoin regulation represents a watershed moment in cryptocurrency oversight. The United States, European Union, Singapore, and other major jurisdictions have moved from regulatory ambiguity to explicit frameworks establishing reserve requirements, custody standards, and transparency obligations. These parallel regulatory efforts suggest a global consensus that stablecoins fulfilling payment functions require prudential oversight commensurate with the risks they pose to the financial system[1][8][21].

### The GENIUS Act: America's Federal Framework

The Guiding and Establishing National Innovation for U.S. Stablecoins (GENIUS) Act, signed into law on July 18, 2025, establishes the first comprehensive federal framework for stablecoin issuers in the United States[1][4]. This legislation fundamentally reshapes the regulatory landscape by creating three pathways for entities to become permitted payment stablecoin issuers: subsidiaries of insured depository institutions approved by their primary federal regulator, federal qualified issuers approved by the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency (OCC), and state-qualified issuers approved by state regulators meeting federally established standards[8]. The Act's passage followed bipartisan support in both chambers of Congress, with the Senate approving the measure 68-30 and the House 308-122, reflecting broad recognition of the need for regulatory clarity in this rapidly growing sector[1].

The GENIUS Act establishes a mandatory one-to-one reserve backing requirement, positioning this standard as the cornerstone of consumer protection and financial stability[1][8]. This requirement mandates that permitted payment stablecoin issuers maintain identifiable reserves backing outstanding stablecoins on at least a one-to-one basis, creating what essentially amounts to a "hard promise" of redemption at par value[57]. The framework explicitly defines six categories of permitted reserve assets: physical U.S. coins and Federal Reserve notes; demand deposits at insured depository institutions, including regulated foreign banks; Treasury bills, notes, or bonds with remaining maturities of 93 days or less; repurchase and reverse repurchase agreements backed by such Treasury securities; money market funds invested solely in those same underlying assets; and central bank reserve deposits[1]. This enumerated approach contrasts with some alternative regulatory regimes that permit broader asset classes, reflecting the Act's prioritization of liquidity and credit quality over yield generation[8].

The operational requirements established by the GENIUS Act extend beyond reserve composition to encompass comprehensive disclosure, audit, and operational standards. Permitted payment stablecoin issuers must publish monthly reports on reserve asset composition that are examined by registered public accounting firms, establishing a frequent attestation cadence intended to provide continuous transparency to market participants[8]. The Act additionally imposes rigorous anti-money laundering and sanctions compliance obligations by explicitly bringing stablecoin issuers under the Bank Secrecy Act, subjecting them to Know Your Customer (KYC), Anti-Money Laundering (AML), and suspicious activity reporting (SAR) requirements equivalent to those applicable to traditional financial institutions[4][56].

The Act's treatment of stablecoin holders in bankruptcy proceedings represents a significant departure from traditional creditor hierarchies. Section 11 of the GENIUS Act grants stablecoin holders priority claims to reserve assets, placing their redemption rights above administrative claims, priority claims under Section 507 of the Bankruptcy Code, and other unsecured claims[57][60]. This priority framework recognizes the fundamental nature of the redemption obligation while creating potential challenges for estate administration and reorganization efforts. The Act additionally provides that stablecoin reserves are excluded from a debtor issuer's bankruptcy estate, attempting to create a bankruptcy-remote structure that ensures reserve assets remain unavailable to satisfy other creditor claims[57].

### MiCA: The European Union's Comprehensive Regulatory Framework

The European Union's Markets in Crypto-Assets Regulation (MiCA) represents the world's most comprehensive cryptocurrency regulatory framework, having entered into force in June 2023 with core provisions applicable as of December 30, 2024[29][68]. MiCA establishes distinct regulatory pathways for asset-referenced tokens (ARTs), which reference multiple assets or commodities, and electronic money tokens (EMTs), which reference a single fiat currency and serve payment functions[2][26]. Stablecoins typically fall within both categories, depending on their specific design and intended use cases.

For issuers of ARTs and EMTs, MiCA imposes stringent requirements regarding reserve composition and custody. Reserve assets must comprise high-quality liquid assets with minimal market and credit risk, with at least thirty percent of funds received in exchange for EMTs required to be deposited in segregated accounts at credit institutions[2]. The remaining balance must be invested in assets qualifying as a defined class of highly liquid financial instruments with minimal market risk, credit risk, and concentration risk, denominated in the same official currency as the token[2]. This requirement creates a reserve structure emphasizing liquidity and credit quality while permitting some flexibility in composition compared to jurisdictions mandating comprehensive Treasury backing.

Custody requirements under MiCA reflect a detailed approach to asset segregation and protection. Reserve assets for both ARTs and EMTs must be held in custody by an eligible custodian that is a different legal entity from the issuer[2]. For crypto assets held as reserve components, either a crypto asset service provider (CASP) or a credit institution may serve as custodian[2]. The regulation establishes detailed custody contract requirements, mandating that agreements document the roles, responsibilities, rights, and obligations of issuer and custodian, regulate information flows enabling custodians to perform their functions, and specify mechanisms for handling lost or damaged assets[2]. Importantly, MiCA imposes liability standards on custodians borrowed from UCITS V and AIFMD depository rules, requiring custodians to compensate or provide restitution for lost assets unless the loss occurred as a result of an external event beyond reasonable control[2].

MiCA additionally requires monthly independent attestations of reserve assets for all EMT issuers and for ARTs classified as "significant" based on holder count, market value, or transaction volume thresholds[26]. Annual audits by external auditors must verify reserve adequacy, creating a dual-layer attestation structure combining frequent point-in-time assessments with comprehensive annual reviews[26]. These requirements establish baseline transparency standards while permitting jurisdictional variations in implementation details, reflecting MiCA's role as a framework guideline for member state regulators rather than self-executing technical regulation[29][71].

### Singapore's MAS Framework: Targeted Approach to Single-Currency Stablecoins

The Monetary Authority of Singapore (MAS) finalized its stablecoin regulatory framework on August 15, 2023, establishing a specialized regime applicable to single-currency stablecoins (SCS) pegged to the Singapore dollar or G10 currencies[3][6][21]. This framework, expected to become effective in mid-2026, reflects a more targeted approach than broader jurisdictions, applying only to single-currency instruments issued from Singapore rather than the full spectrum of crypto-collateralized or algorithmic stablecoins. The MAS framework requires prospective issuers to demonstrate that their stablecoins will be recognized as "MAS-regulated stablecoins," creating a labeling mechanism intended to distinguish compliant instruments from unregulated alternatives[3][6].

Reserve requirements under the MAS framework mandate that SCS issuers ensure valuation of reserve assets is maintained at a level at least equal to 100 percent of outstanding SCS in circulation at all times[3][6]. Critically, reserve assets must be denominated in the currency of stablecoin peg and held in cash, cash equivalents, or government or central bank debt securities with maturities up to three months[3][6]. This requirement prioritizes liquidity over yield generation, explicitly limiting issuers' ability to extend maturity dates or invest in higher-yielding instruments that might compromise redemption capacity during stress periods. Annual audits and monthly independent attestations, to be published on issuers' websites, verify reserve adequacy and provide continuous transparency regarding reserve composition[3][6].

Segregation and custody of reserve assets represent focal points of the MAS framework. Reserve assets must be held in segregated accounts separate from issuer's own assets, with custodians either licensed for custodial services in Singapore or overseas-based custodians holding minimum credit ratings of A- and maintaining Singapore branches regulated by MAS[3][6][21]. This dual custody pathway attempts to balance access to international custodians with domestic regulatory oversight, establishing minimum creditworthiness thresholds for non-Singapore custodians. Additionally, MAS imposes minimum base capital requirements of either S$1 million or 50 percent of annual operating expenses (whichever is greater), alongside liquidity requirements mandating that issuers hold liquid assets exceeding half of annual operating expenses or amounts needed for orderly wind-down[3][6].

## Reserve Composition Requirements: Balancing Liquidity, Safety, and Yield

The design of reserve asset frameworks represents a critical policy lever determining how stablecoins interact with traditional financial markets and the extent to which reserve management creates concentration risks or fire-sale dynamics. Jurisdictional approaches vary substantially in their treatment of reserve composition, with policy choices reflecting differing assessments of risk and different assumptions about issuer incentives.

### The GENIUS Act's Enumerated Approach

Under the GENIUS Act framework, the six enumerated categories of permitted reserves provide different balances of safety, liquidity, and profitability, creating opportunities for issuers to optimize compositions based on operational needs and market conditions[1]. Physical U.S. coins and Federal Reserve notes offer absolute safety and immediate liquidity but generate no yield and create operational challenges given the physical storage requirements[1]. Demand deposits at insured depository institutions combine safety, achieved through federal deposit insurance, with reasonable liquidity and minimal yield generation[1]. The enumerated treasury security categories—bills, notes, and bonds with 93-day or shorter remaining maturities—create a maturity constraint intended to prevent issuers from extending duration excessively in pursuit of yield while maintaining reasonable liquidity[1].

The repurchase agreement category permits issuers to participate in overnight repo markets backed by Treasury collateral, creating a mechanism for collateralized lending that maintains high liquidity while potentially generating modest yield advantages over direct Treasury holdings[1]. Money market funds invested solely in the enumerated assets create a diversification mechanism while maintaining reserve quality, though adding an intermediary layer that introduces operational complexity[1]. Central bank reserve deposits represent the most recently clarified category, potentially enabling direct Federal Reserve account access for certain qualified issuers, creating a mechanism for direct central bank money access comparable to bank depositor privileges[1].

The practical significance of this enumerated approach lies in how it structures issuer incentives. By explicitly limiting permitted assets, the GENIUS Act prevents the reserve slippage that characterized earlier unregulated stablecoin developments, where issuers gradually expanded reserve compositions to include commercial paper, high-yield securities, and other instruments introducing credit risk, liquidity risk, and systemic interconnectedness[1][15]. Historical precedent from Tether illustrates these dangers; documents obtained by CoinDesk reveal that Tether's reserves as of March 2021 included substantial commercial paper holdings from international banks, creating exposure to corporate credit risk and demonstrating how gradual reserve composition drift can accumulate material exposures[15]. The GENIUS Act's enumerated list attempts to prevent such drift by establishing explicit boundaries beyond which issuers cannot venture.

### Treasury Backing and Macroeconomic Implications

The convergence around Treasury-based reserve backing in both the GENIUS Act and MiCA frameworks creates important implications for Treasury markets and monetary policy transmission. If stablecoin adoption reaches substantial scale, the reserve demand for short-duration Treasury instruments could significantly influence yield curve dynamics and Treasury market functioning[19][40]. Treasury analysis suggests that stablecoin reserve demand could increase demand for Treasury instruments, particularly at the short end of the curve where most reserve assets concentrate, exerting downward pressure on short-term yields[19][40]. This asset reallocation would shift the composition of the overall monetary base, with implications for interest rate risk distribution across financial system participants.

The magnitude of these macroeconomic effects depends fundamentally on adoption trajectories and reserve asset allocation decisions. Current projections suggest stablecoin issuance could reach $1.9 trillion in base case scenarios by 2030, with bull case estimates reaching $4.0 trillion[62]. If reserve backing concentrates in short-duration Treasuries as required by major regulatory frameworks, this growth could generate meaningful shifts in Treasury demand patterns. One Treasury Department analysis notes that stablecoin issuance growth creates two primary impacts on Treasury markets: overall increase in demand for Treasuries and shift to the front-end of the curve as legislation requires sub-93-day holdings[40]. However, these effects depend critically on whether stablecoin growth represents net new demand for dollar assets or merely reallocation from existing dollar holdings, a distinction with substantial implications for domestic credit provision and cross-border capital flows.

### Commercial Paper, Yield Management, and Risk Dynamics

The prohibition or strict limitation of commercial paper holdings in regulated stablecoin frameworks represents a deliberate policy choice rejecting earlier models where issuers accepted credit risk in pursuit of yield. Tether's historical reliance on commercial paper holdings—reaching nearly 50 percent of reserves at certain periods—illustrates how yield management pressures create gradual reserve composition drift that can accumulate substantial credit risk[15]. The revealed preference for Tether to hold commercial paper issued by international banks despite availability of comparable Treasury yields suggests that even modest basis point advantages create behavioral incentives for issuers to extend beyond the safest asset categories[15].

The GENIUS Act's prohibition on direct commercial paper holdings does not prevent issuers from accessing commercial paper exposure through money market fund intermediaries, potentially creating a loophole through which indirect exposure could accumulate[1]. This structure reflects a policy judgment that intermediation through regulated money market funds, which themselves face regulatory constraints on portfolio composition and diversification, provides sufficient protection compared to direct commercial paper holdings. However, the recent evolution of yield-bearing stablecoin products illustrates how alternative mechanisms can recreate yield-chasing dynamics even when direct holdings are prohibited. Crypto asset service providers now offer yields on stablecoin balances through DeFi lending platforms, creating incentives for issuers to invest in higher-yielding instruments to support such offerings, potentially circumventing regulatory intent even when direct mechanism restrictions prevent overt reserve composition expansion[37][52].

## Qualified Custodian Requirements and Infrastructure Standards

The custody infrastructure supporting stablecoin reserves represents a critical component of the overall risk management framework, with the technical security, operational resilience, and regulatory standing of custodians determining whether reserves remain accessible during periods of market stress. The design of qualified custodian standards thus represents a crucial policy lever determining the actual versus theoretical reserve quality available to support redemptions.

### Regulatory Definitions of Qualified Custodians

The GENIUS Act establishes that custody and safekeeping services for payment stablecoins or their reserve assets can only be provided by persons subject to federal or state supervision as outlined in the Act[8]. This requirement effectively restricts qualified custodian roles to entities holding banking charters, trust company licenses, or equivalent regulatory authorization. The Monetary Authority of Singapore similarly restricts reserve custodians to financial institutions licensed for custodial services in Singapore or overseas-based custodians meeting minimum creditworthiness thresholds (A- ratings minimum) with MAS-regulated Singapore branches[3][6]. MiCA establishes that credit institutions and certain investment firms authorized to provide safekeeping and administration services under MiFID II standards may serve as custodians, creating a regulatory gate linked to existing financial services authorization[2].

These definitions reflect a deliberate policy choice to restrict custody roles to entities already subject to prudential supervision, capital requirements, and operational resilience standards developed for traditional banking. This approach creates continuity with established banking regulations while avoiding creation of new regulatory categories requiring parallel supervisory infrastructure. However, this approach also inherently limits custody capacity to existing banking infrastructure, potentially creating bottlenecks as stablecoin adoption scales and custody demand exceeds available capacity among qualified custodians willing to provide specialized stablecoin custody services[53].

### Segregation and Bankruptcy-Remote Structures

The segregation of customer assets from custodian's own balance sheet and from other customer assets represents a fundamental design feature protecting against both custodian misappropriation and custodian insolvency. The GENIUS Act requires that reserve assets be held in segregated accounts separate from issuer's own assets, creating a legal structure where stablecoin reserves have restricted availability to satisfy custodian's creditors or claims against the custodian[8][57]. This segregation requirement builds on established money market fund regulatory standards, which similarly require segregation to protect fund assets from commingling with custodian or sponsor assets[73].

Trust company structures offering "bankruptcy-remote" trust arrangements provide enhanced protection compared to standard segregation. In bankruptcy-remote trust models, reserve assets are held in trust with the stablecoin issuer as grantor and stablecoin holders as beneficiaries, creating a legal relationship where reserves exist entirely outside the issuer's bankruptcy estate[13]. This structure prevents a stablecoin issuer's bankruptcy from triggering an involuntary liquidation of reserves to satisfy other creditor claims, as the reserves never become property of the issuer's bankruptcy estate. Trust company issuers like Paxos extensively employ this model, citing bankruptcy remoteness as a critical differentiator from non-trust-company issuers who may hold reserves as corporate assets subject to creditor claims in insolvency proceedings[13].

The practical significance of bankruptcy-remote structures became evident during the Silicon Valley Bank crisis. Circle's USDC held approximately $3.3 billion in reserves at Silicon Valley Bank at the time of that institution's failure, creating acute redemption risk as depositors questioned whether Circle could access those reserves[43][67]. While the FDIC ultimately protected all SVB depositors, including Circle, the episode demonstrated that even high-quality liquid assets can become inaccessible during systemic banking stress. Trust company structures do not eliminate this risk—trust company reserves remain subject to banking system stress—but they do create stronger legal claims to reserve assets even if custodian institutions fail[13].

### Multi-Signature Controls and Operational Resilience

Institutional-grade custody increasingly incorporates multi-signature controls requiring multiple independent approvals for critical operations including minting, redemption, and reserve transfers[50][53]. Multi-signature architectures distribute control across multiple parties or systems so that no single key can authorize reserve movements, eliminating the single points of failure characteristic of simpler custody models[50]. Implementation of multi-signature controls typically involves threshold configurations such as 2-of-3 (requiring any two of three designated signers) or 3-of-5 (requiring three of five), with configuration choices reflecting institutional risk tolerance and operational requirements[50].

These control structures create both security benefits and operational challenges. The security benefits derive from eliminating single points of compromise—even if an attacker obtains one signing key, they cannot move reserves without obtaining additional keys held by separate parties or systems. The operational challenges arise from increased complexity in managing multiple keys, coordinating approvals among multiple parties, and maintaining redundancy without introducing new single points of failure. Geographically distributed multi-signature controls, where signing keys are held in different physical locations by different institutions or individuals, provide additional security against coordinated attacks but complicate the approval process for time-sensitive redemptions[50].

Custody infrastructure increasingly incorporates Systems and Organization Controls (SOC) 2 compliance certifications, which provide independent attestation that custody providers maintain appropriate security controls and operational resilience standards[32][42]. SOC 2 audits evaluate controls across five Trust Service Criteria: security, availability, processing integrity, confidentiality, and privacy[32]. Stablecoin custody providers undergo SOC 2 Type II examinations, which evaluate controls across extended periods (typically 6-12 months) to verify consistent operation rather than point-in-time compliance. This certification provides independent verification that custody infrastructure meets institutional-grade security and operational standards, though it does not guarantee absolute protection against all potential threats[32][42].

## Attestation Requirements and Proof of Reserves Frameworks

The frequency and scope of attestation services has emerged as a critical transparency mechanism for stablecoin users, with attestation standards establishing baseline expectations regarding reserve verification. The distinction between attestations and full financial audits, and the appropriate cadence for each verification type, represents an ongoing policy debate reflecting tensions between transparency, operational efficiency, and cost considerations.

### AICPA 2025 Criteria for Stablecoin Reporting

The American Institute of Certified Public Accountants (AICPA) released the 2025 Criteria for Stablecoin Reporting in March 2025, establishing the first comprehensive framework for stablecoin issuers to present and disclose information on outstanding stablecoins and backing assets[10]. These criteria represent an important step toward consistent reporting standards across the stablecoin industry, addressing earlier criticism of divergent and sometimes opaque reserve disclosures. The AICPA criteria establish benchmarks for presenting information related to tokens issued and assets backing them, promoting consistent reporting among issuers and boosting trust in the stablecoin space[10].

The 2025 AICPA criteria comprise two distinct components: Part I establishes a common framework for presenting and disclosing information on outstanding stablecoins and reserve assets, while Part II introduces criteria focused on risk and controls supporting token operations[7][10]. This two-part structure recognizes that reserve adequacy (the focus of Part I) represents necessary but insufficient assurance regarding stablecoin stability—understanding of controls supporting token operations, custody arrangements, and operational security becomes equally critical[10].

The AICPA criteria establish detailed requirements regarding information presentation. Management assertions regarding outstanding stablecoins must disclose tokens outstanding by blockchain or chain, including circulating and non-circulating pre-minted tokens, along with smart contract issuer addresses enabling direct verification against on-chain token supply[9][33]. For reserve assets, criteria require disclosure of composition, maturity dates, CUSIP numbers, and fair market values, enabling independent verification of reserve adequacy and liquidity characteristics[9][33]. The criteria additionally permit management to make assertions regarding additional reserve characteristics, such as segregation in trust accounts, insurance coverage, or regulatory compliance, creating flexibility to address issuer-specific circumstances while maintaining comparable bases for attestation[9][33].

Importantly, the AICPA criteria permit and in fact encourage management to embed regulatory compliance assertions within attestation scope. For example, if an issuer operates under the GENIUS Act, management can assert that reserves comply with GENIUS Act requirements regarding asset categories and duration limits, enabling attestation providers to verify regulatory compliance as part of the reserve verification engagement[9][33]. This approach integrates regulatory compliance verification with financial attestation, creating a unified assurance structure rather than parallel independent assessments.

### Attestation Versus Audit Frameworks

A critical distinction between attestation services and full financial audits determines the appropriate frequency and cost-benefit tradeoff for stablecoin verification. Attestations represent limited-scope engagements focused exclusively on reserve assets and outstanding stablecoin liabilities, typically performed on a point-in-time basis to verify reserve adequacy at specified dates[9][12][36]. Full financial audits, by contrast, evaluate an issuer's entire financial system including income statement, cash flow statement, internal controls, and broader balance sheet items, requiring substantially greater audit effort and typically conducted annually[9][12][36].

The operational advantages of attestations derive from their limited scope. Because attestations focus only on tokens and reserves, they can be completed in substantially shorter timeframes and at lower cost than comprehensive audits[9][12][36]. This efficiency permits more frequent attestations—monthly or weekly attestations have become industry standard—providing continuous transparency regarding reserve adequacy without the operational burden that would accompany monthly comprehensive audits[9][12][36]. The GENIUS Act mandates monthly public attestations examined by registered public accounting firms, establishing this cadence as a baseline transparency requirement[8][9].

However, attestations provide narrower assurance than comprehensive audits. An attestation that reserves equal outstanding token liabilities does not evaluate whether the issuer can sustain operations or meet other obligations, whether internal controls over reserve movements function appropriately, or whether the issuer faces litigation or regulatory issues affecting the stablecoin's viability[9][12][36]. Recent regulatory frameworks increasingly call for hybrid approaches combining frequent attestations with less frequent but more comprehensive audits. The GENIUS Act requires monthly attestations for all permitted issuers but adds annual audit requirements for larger issuers, creating a tiered structure where smaller issuers rely primarily on frequent attestations while larger systemically important issuers undergo deeper annual examination[36][73].

The attestation standards themselves follow either ISAE 3000 (Assurance Engagements) standards commonly used internationally or AT-C 205 (Examination Engagements) standards prevalent in the U.S.[9][33]. Both standards require management to make assertions regarding stablecoin characteristics that auditors then test and opine upon. This structure grants management flexibility to include additional assertions beyond outstanding tokens and reserves—such as assertions regarding segregated custody, regulatory compliance, or particular risk characteristics—within the attestation scope[9][33].

### Real-Time and On-Chain Proof of Reserves

An emerging frontier in stablecoin verification involves real-time proof of reserves mechanisms utilizing Application Programming Interfaces (APIs) and on-chain verification to provide continuous or near-continuous assurance regarding reserve adequacy. Some custodians and reserve managers now expose secure data feeds updating token-backing balances every few seconds, enabling automated reconciliation and near-real-time verification of reserve coverage[9][33]. Firms like The Network Firm and Moore Hong Kong have begun offering real-time, independent attestations utilizing real-time bank and on-chain data to perform proof-of-reserves reconciliation as frequently as every thirty seconds[9][33].

This evolution toward real-time mechanisms addresses a fundamental limitation of point-in-time attestations: they capture reserve status at a single moment, providing no assurance regarding asset movements occurring after the attestation date. Real-time proof-of-reserves mechanisms eliminate this timing gap, creating continuous verification that reserves remain sufficient without relying on periodic snapshots[9][33]. The technical implementation involves secure APIs from custodial banks and blockchain explorers feeding reserve and token balance data to independent verification systems that continuously reconcile these datasets[9][33].

Additionally, some projects explore on-chain proof-of-reserves mechanisms utilizing oracle providers to publish validated reserve information directly to blockchains, enabling smart contracts to programmatically verify reserve adequacy and restrict minting if coverage falls below required thresholds[9][33]. This "compliance by design" approach embeds reserve verification into the smart contract logic itself, creating automated enforcement of reserve requirements rather than relying on external verification followed by manual remediation. While these mechanisms remain nascent and raise questions regarding oracle manipulation and technical implementation details, they represent the direction toward more continuous, automated verification compared to periodic attestation engagement conducted months apart.

## Case Studies: Systemic Stress and Reserve Accessibility

The March 2023 Silicon Valley Bank failure and its contagion to the stablecoin sector provides the most recent and comprehensive illustration of how reserve quality that appears adequate during normal times can become inaccessible during systemic stress. Additionally, the 2022 collapse of TerraUSD and the subsequent DeFi contagion through mechanisms including Peg Stability Modules demonstrates how reserve structures that function in normal market conditions can fail under coordinated redemption pressure combined with interconnected protocol designs.

### The Silicon Valley Bank Crisis and Circle's USDC Depeg

Silicon Valley Bank's failure on March 10, 2023, created acute stress in the USDC stablecoin market because Circle held approximately $3.3 billion of USDC reserves at SVB at the time of the bank's failure[14][43][67]. Circle's initial cryptic communication regarding the funds' inaccessibility triggered panic redemptions and a USDC price decline to $0.87 per coin, representing an 13 percent depeg despite USDC reserves exceeding outstanding tokens[14][43][67]. This episode demonstrated that even fully backed, high-quality asset reserves can lose their stabilizing function if access becomes questionable.

The Federal Reserve, Treasury Department, and FDIC's announcement that SVB's resolution would protect all depositors, including uninsured ones, eventually restored confidence in USDC and permitted the stablecoin to repeg[14][43][67]. However, the episode exposed important vulnerabilities in the stablecoin reserve architecture. First, concentration of reserves at a single custodian or financial institution creates systemic vulnerability, as deposit flows that might appear diversified at the stablecoin issuer level become concentrated at the deposit-taking level[14][67]. Second, the episode illustrated that consumer confidence in stablecoin redemption rights depends not only on reserve adequacy but also on perceived issuer and custodian stability[14][67]. Third, the speed with which confidence can evaporate—USDC experienced substantial redemptions within hours of SVB's failure announcement—illustrated the run risk inherent in non-interest-bearing payment instruments facing confidence shocks[14][67].

The USDC depeg's secondary effects in DeFi protocols further illustrated contagion channels connecting stablecoin stress to broader financial system risk. MakerDAO's Dai stablecoin operated Peg Stability Modules (PSMs) allowing one-to-one exchange between Dai and USDC, intended to support Dai's dollar peg by providing access to a more credible stablecoin[43][67]. However, during the USDC depeg, the PSM's one-to-one exchange rate became a liability rather than support. As USDC holders fled to preserve value, they purchased Dai on secondary markets at a discount (reflecting Dai's perceived lower trustworthiness), redeemed Dai at par through the PSM in exchange for USDC, and then sold that USDC at depressed prices to recover fiat[43][67]. This mechanism channeled USDC selling pressure to Dai and created simultaneous depeg pressure on both stablecoins despite Dai remaining solvent with excess collateral[43][67].

The cascade effect extended to other stablecoins holding components at SVB or other failed institutions. GUSD (Gemini dollar) and USDP (Paxos dollar) similarly experienced depegs as confidence that redemptions could be honored declined[43][67]. TUSD, which held portions of reserves at Signature Bank (which entered receivership during the same weekend), also experienced depeg pressure[43][67]. Collectively, the episode illustrated how stablecoin stress can cascade through interconnected DeFi protocols creating contagion beyond direct reserve exposure, as implied collateral paths become escape routes as confidence evaporates[43][67].

### Tether's Historical Reserve Evolution and Commercial Paper Exposure

Tether's historical opacity regarding reserve composition and subsequent gradual transparency reveals how stablecoin reserve practices evolved absent comprehensive regulatory constraints. Documents obtained by CoinDesk under Freedom of Information Act requests show that as of March 2021, Tether held reserves across four banks, two investment management firms, two gold depositories, and on its sister company Bitfinex[15]. Critically, Tether held substantial commercial paper assets, with nearly 85 percent of holdings at Ansbacher (Bahamas) taking the form of commercial paper issued by entities including Qatar National Bank, Barclays Bank, Deutsche Bank, Emirates NBD Bank, and Natwest Group[15].

The commercial paper holdings represented approximately half of Tether's total reserves at that time, creating exposure to corporate credit risk across international financial institutions[15]. This reserve composition differed substantially from the high-quality liquid assets (Treasuries and cash) that characterize compliant issuers under contemporary regulatory frameworks. The concentration of reserves at Deltec Bank (Bahamas), which held more than $26 billion as of March 2021, created additional concentration risk. Tether's reliance on international banking relationships outside traditional financial centers created regulatory and operational challenges, including the bank signature scandal when Deltec Bank's verification letter bore only a squiggle rather than a legible signature[15].

The historical Tether case illustrates the reserve composition drift that regulatory frameworks attempt to prevent. Even though Tether acknowledged putting funds in commercial paper, the extent of reliance—approaching 50 percent of reserves—was not publicly known until documents emerged years later. This pattern suggests that absent explicit regulatory constraints, issuers face incentives to extend reserve compositions toward higher-yielding assets, with each incremental extension appearing justified by modest yield advantages. By the time comprehensive transparency requirements emerged, Tether had already accumulated substantial commitments to non-Treasury assets.

Tether's subsequent evolution toward greater Treasury-weighted reserves reflects both regulatory pressure and market recognition that commercial paper concentration created unjustifiable credit risk. By 2025, Tether maintained near-zero bank deposit ratios and had substantially increased Treasury holdings, though complete transparency regarding reserve composition remains limited compared to regulated competitors like Circle[22][31]. The Tether case demonstrates both the effectiveness of regulatory constraints in preventing excessive risk-taking and the reality that achieving full compliance required regulatory intervention rather than voluntary issuer choice.

### DeFi Contagion: The Terra Luna Collapse and Systemic Vulnerabilities

While not exclusively a stablecoin reserve management issue, the May 2022 collapse of TerraUSD (UST) and its native governance token LUNA illustrates systemic risks arising from algorithmic stablecoin structures and the contagion channels connecting DeFi protocols[44][47]. UST employed a dual-token mechanism attempting to maintain peg through algorithmic minting and burning of LUNA, without maintaining traditional collateral reserves. The Anchor Protocol, operated by Terraform Labs (UST's sponsor), offered unsustainable 20 percent annual yields on UST deposits, creating liquidity imbalances and concentrating risk within a single platform[44][47].

When UST briefly depegged from $1 in May 2022, it triggered a death spiral where redemptions required algorithmic minting of LUNA tokens sold to restore peg, depressing LUNA prices and eroding confidence in the arbitrage mechanism supporting UST stability[44][47]. The cascade effect erased over $60 billion in value and demonstrated that algorithmic stablecoins lacking traditional collateral cannot maintain stability under stress conditions[44][47]. Regulators globally have subsequently moved to restrict or prohibit algorithmic stablecoins entirely, recognizing that the fundamental design—reliance on confidence and arbitrage mechanisms without collateral buffers—creates unacceptable systemic risk[44][47].

The Terra Luna collapse's significance for reserve management frameworks lies in illustrating the importance of conservative, liquid reserve backing as a stability mechanism. Fiat-backed stablecoins that maintained proper collateral—like Circle's USDC—experienced price deviations during the SVB crisis but recovered upon credible assurances regarding reserve accessibility. UST lacked any such recovery mechanism because it had no actual reserves to point to. This distinction illustrates why contemporary regulatory frameworks universally mandate collateral-backed models while excluding algorithmic mechanisms entirely[44][47].

## Global Regulatory Harmonization and International Standards

Parallel development of stablecoin regulatory frameworks across major jurisdictions suggests emerging convergence around core principles even as implementation details vary. International standard-setting bodies, including the Committee on Payments and Market Infrastructures (CPMI) and International Organization of Securities Commissions (IOSCO), have published guidance applying existing financial market infrastructure standards to systemically important stablecoin arrangements[51][54]. This approach avoids creating entirely new regulatory standards while recognizing that stablecoins performing payment functions should observe the same prudential principles applied to traditional payment systems.

The CPMI-IOSCO guidance confirms that Principles for Financial Market Infrastructures (PFMI) apply to global stablecoin arrangements performing systemically important payment system functions[51][54]. These principles cover governance, risk management, settlement finality, and money settlements—domains critical to stablecoin stability and market participant protection. Application of these existing standards rather than creating new crypto-specific frameworks enables regulators to build on established supervisory expertise and market participant familiarity with requirements, while still accommodating the novel technical characteristics of blockchain-based systems[51][54].

The European Systemic Risk Board's October 2025 analysis of crypto-asset systemic risks identified stablecoins as a critical concern, highlighting interconnection risks between traditional finance (through reserve holdings) and DeFi (through lending and collateral usage)[46]. The report emphasized potential for two-way contagion where stress in the banking system (like the SVB episode) triggers stablecoin depegs, which in turn create fire-sale pressure on Treasury and other reserve assets, potentially spreading stress back into traditional financial markets[46]. This analysis informed European regulatory approaches emphasizing rigorous reserve quality requirements and custody segregation as mechanisms to limit contagion pathways.

The Federal Reserve's research exploring banks' competitive responses to stablecoin adoption further illustrates emerging consensus regarding systemic risk channels[18][22][27][31][66][67]. Analysis suggests that if substantial deposit outflows occur toward stablecoins, banks would face increased liquidity risk, higher funding costs, and constrained lending capacity, with differential impacts across bank sizes and funding structures[18][22][27][31][66][67]. This risk assessment informs regulatory approaches emphasizing non-interest-bearing stablecoin structures (preventing direct competition with higher-yielding bank deposits) and reserve requirements encouraging Treasury demand rather than competing with bank funding[30][62].

## Liquidity Management and Reserve Composition Trade-offs

The design of reserve frameworks necessarily involves trade-offs between competing objectives: maximizing liquidity to support redemptions during stress, earning yields to support issuer operations, concentrating in highest-quality assets to minimize credit risk, and diversifying across multiple custodians and asset types to reduce concentration risk. These tensions have generated considerable debate among regulators and market participants regarding optimal reserve composition design.

### Liquidity Requirements and Stress Testing

Regulatory frameworks increasingly incorporate stress-testing requirements to verify that reserve compositions can support redemptions under various market disruption scenarios. The GENIUS Act requires that stablecoin issuers maintain sufficient reserves to support timely redemption under stressed scenarios, though the specific stress tests required remain subject to ongoing rulemaking by federal and state regulators[8]. MiCA requires that issuers maintain sufficient reserves to meet all claims by token holders, with "sufficient" interpreted to account for stressed redemption scenarios[2].

Practical implementation of stress testing involves modeling scenarios where redemption requests spike beyond normal daily volumes and custodian institutions face operational disruptions or liquidity constraints. A typical stress scenario might envision simultaneous redemption requests reaching 25-50 percent of outstanding token supply within a single business day, requiring verification that reserves remain accessible and liquid even if primary custodians experience disruptions. Advanced stress testing incorporates second-order effects, such as fire-sale pressure reducing prices of reserve assets if substantial quantities must be liquidated rapidly, potentially creating cascading impacts on reserve adequacy[20][25].

Some jurisdictions have begun exploring dynamic reserve requirements that increase required reserve percentages in response to elevated market stress. Singapore's framework envisions that MAS could increase minimum reserve requirements above 100 percent if stablecoin usage patterns create risks, though this authority remains undetermined pending full framework implementation[6]. This approach acknowledges that static requirements may be insufficient during periods when customer behavior becomes more volatile or when correlated redemption pressure emerges across multiple stablecoin issuers.

### Maturity Constraints and Deposit Banking Parallels

The 93-day maturity constraint embedded in the GENIUS Act reflects a deliberate policy choice limiting issuers' ability to extend duration in pursuit of higher yields. This requirement draws parallels to money market fund regulations established following the 2008 financial crisis, which similarly imposed strict maturity limits (39 days average, 60 days maximum) to prevent maturity extension for yield and ensure rapid asset liquidity[73]. The policy rationale underlying both frameworks recognizes that maturity extension creates illiquidity risk—if substantial redemptions occur, the issuer faces forced asset sales at potentially depressed prices[73].

The maturity constraint trade-off involves accepting lower yields in exchange for greater liquidity certainty. A Treasury three-month bill yields approximately 4.5-5.5 percent depending on market conditions, whereas 30-year Treasury bonds yield 4.0-4.5 percent. However, this modest yield advantage comes with substantially greater liquidity and reinvestment frequency. By constraining reserves to 93-day duration, regulatory frameworks prevent issuers from extending substantially into longer-duration instruments where yield advantages would become more attractive but liquidity would diminish materially.

The constraint also addresses what regulators view as a critical vulnerability in earlier stablecoin designs. By limiting duration, frameworks prevent scenarios where issuers hold substantial quantities of longer-duration instruments and face forced sales at fire-sale prices during stress periods. The historical USDC depeg episode illustrated this risk in a different form: even though Circle held extremely high-quality assets (deposits at a major U.S. bank), the assets became inaccessible due to banking system stress. Duration constraints don't solve asset inaccessibility risks entirely, but they ensure that assets can be rapidly liquidated at reasonable prices even if they must be sold immediately.

### Reserve Yields and Interest-Bearing Wrappers

An emerging tension involves how stablecoin reserve yields should be allocated and whether issuers should be permitted to offer interest to stablecoin holders. The GENIUS Act explicitly prohibits permitted payment stablecoin issuers from paying interest or yields to holders based on holding the stablecoin[37][52]. This prohibition reflects a policy judgment that non-interest-bearing stablecoins better preserve their payment function—if stablecoins offered attractive yields, they would begin competing with bank deposits and money market funds for savings balances rather than functioning primarily as payment instruments[37][52].

However, interest-bearing wrappers constructed by crypto asset service providers (CASPs) can circumvent this prohibition. CASPs now offer yield products where users deposit compliant stablecoins and receive returns from DeFi lending platforms[37][52]. Some offerings have reached 4.25 percent annual percentage yields on USDC holdings, exceeding typical bank deposit rates by approximately four times[37][52]. This structure creates an incentive for issuers to invest reserves in higher-yielding assets to support third-party yield offerings, potentially recreating the yield-chasing dynamics that direct interest prohibition was intended to prevent[37][52].

Regulators are beginning to address this tension. The Bank for International Settlements analysis of stablecoin-related yields recommends that authorities implement clear restrictions on indirect yield offerings through CASPs when such offerings create incentives for issuers to extend reserve compositions beyond specified safe asset categories[37]. Some regulatory approaches distinguish between issuers directly paying interest (prohibited) versus third-party CASPs offering yield products backed by issuer reserves (permitted but subject to CASP regulation), attempting to allow yield innovation while maintaining clear restrictions on issuer behavior.

## Bankruptcy and Priority Treatment of Stablecoin Holders

The GENIUS Act's novel treatment of stablecoin holders in insolvency proceedings reflects a judgment that stablecoin redemption rights merit priority status comparable to deposits in regulated depository institutions. However, this priority framework creates potential tensions with traditional bankruptcy principles and could affect issuer reorganization feasibility.

### Priority of Claims Framework

Under Section 11 of the GENIUS Act, claims of stablecoin holders to reserves have priority over all other claims, including administrative claims such as trustee fees and professional fees[57][60]. This priority is unusual because administrative claims typically receive highest priority under the Bankruptcy Code to encourage parties to continue doing business with troubled debtors. By subordinating administrative claims to stablecoin redemption claims, the Act signals that redemption obligations deserve paramount status comparable to secured creditor claims[57][60].

The priority structure includes two tiers. First, stablecoin holders receive priority claims to required reserves, which are specifically segregated and identified for redemption purposes[57][60]. If required reserves prove insufficient to satisfy all redemption claims, stablecoin holders then receive "super-priority" claims to debtor's unencumbered assets, superior to administrative claims, Section 507 priority claims, and unsecured creditor claims[57][60]. This two-tier structure attempts to ensure redemption at par to the maximum extent possible while preserving orderly resolution when reserves prove insufficient.

The novel treatment creates potential tensions with fundamental bankruptcy principles. Historically, the Bankruptcy Code provides administrative claims with highest priority (after secured creditors) to encourage practitioners, suppliers, and service providers to continue supporting debtors through reorganization. By subordinating administrative claims to stablecoin redemption claims, the Act could reduce incentives for service providers to support troubled issuers through reorganization proceedings, potentially making successful reorganizations less feasible[57].

### Reserve Exclusion from Bankruptcy Estate

The GENIUS Act provides that required stablecoin reserves are excluded from a debtor-issuer's bankruptcy estate, meaning they are not property available to satisfy creditor claims generally[57][60]. This exclusion creates a bankruptcy-remote structure comparable to trust arrangements, where reserve assets exist outside the issuer's legal control and creditor reach. The practical effect is that a stablecoin issuer cannot pledge reserves as collateral, borrow against reserves, or grant liens on reserves, regardless of bankruptcy circumstances[57][60].

However, the Act's language creates potential inconsistencies regarding the automatic stay. The automatic stay—the Bankruptcy Code provision freezing most actions against a bankrupt debtor—typically applies to the debtor and its property. If reserves are excluded from the estate, questions arise regarding whether the automatic stay applies to redemptions from reserves. The Act addresses this question by providing that the automatic stay applies to "the redemption of payment stablecoins issued by the permitted payment stablecoin issuer, from payment stablecoin reserves required to be maintained." However, the Act simultaneously establishes a process for lifting the stay to permit redemptions if reserves are available, creating a 14-day window before redemptions must commence[57][60].

This redemption timeline creates a tension with the broader objective of immediate stablecoin redemption. During normal circumstances, stablecoin issuers redeem on demand or within very brief windows. The bankruptcy framework permits up to 14 days before redemptions must commence, potentially creating meaningful delays and undermining the payment function that justifies stablecoin stability requirements[57][60].

## Future Directions and Emerging Challenges

As stablecoin markets mature and integration into traditional finance deepens, several emerging challenges and policy questions remain unresolved. These include the appropriate treatment of yield-bearing reserve products, integration with central bank digital currencies, cross-border stablecoin issuance, and the macroeconomic implications of substantial stablecoin adoption.

### Central Bank Digital Currencies and Stablecoin Coexistence

The Federal Reserve has undertaken extensive research on central bank digital currency (CBDC) design without making decisions regarding implementation[45]. Simultaneously, stablecoin adoption continues accelerating, raising questions regarding whether CBDCs and stablecoins will coexist or whether CBDC implementation would supersede private stablecoin development[30][45][48]. Federal Reserve Governor Miran has argued that stablecoins primarily address demand in emerging market economies where dollar access is limited rather than competing with bank deposits in developed economies, suggesting potential for complementary rather than competitive coexistence[30].

However, if CBDCs become available offering zero-risk central bank liabilities compared to private stablecoin credit risk, regulatory arbitrage might favor CBDCs for payment purposes while stablecoins maintain roles in DeFi and international settlement[30][45]. The policy question remains unresolved regarding whether regulators should actively promote CBDC adoption to constrain stablecoin growth or permit both mechanisms to develop through market competition.

### Cross-Border Issuance and Regulatory Equivalence

MiCA currently restricts MAS-regulated stablecoin issuance to Singapore-based issuers, citing difficulty in establishing regulatory equivalence with non-Singapore jurisdictions[3][6]. However, stablecoins inherently have cross-border properties—once issued on public blockchains, they can transfer across national boundaries immediately. The tension between regulatory intention (to control issuance within jurisdictions) and technical reality (cross-border asset transferability) remains largely unresolved[46].

The European Systemic Risk Board has identified risk in fungible stablecoin schemes where EU and non-EU entities partner to issue technically identical stablecoins with distributed reserves, potentially creating transmission channels for non-EU stress to affect EU market participants[46]. This pattern illustrates how cross-border issuance creates supervision challenges that single-jurisdiction frameworks cannot address through unilateral action alone[46].

### Integration with Real-World Asset Tokenization

An emerging frontier involves stablecoin integration with tokenized real-world assets (RWA)—securities, real estate, commodities, and other off-chain assets represented as blockchain tokens[81][84]. As RWA tokenization scales, stablecoins become the critical settlement mechanism for transactions in tokenized assets, creating dependencies where stablecoin reliability becomes foundational to RWA market functioning[69][84]. This integration raises questions regarding whether reserve requirements for RWA settlement stablecoins should differ from payment stablecoins and whether integration with tokenized assets creates concentration or contagion risks.

Current projections suggest RWA tokenization could reach $1-10 trillion by 2030, with stablecoins serving as settlement assets for majority of transactions[81][84]. If this materialization occurs, stablecoin infrastructure becomes critical financial market infrastructure supporting trillions in daily settlement volume. This scale suggests that regulatory frameworks must anticipate stablecoin role in institutional finance beyond current payment-focused design[81][84].

Stablecoin reserve management and custody infrastructure have evolved dramatically from the unregulated, opaque structures characterizing early issuers to the comprehensive regulatory frameworks and institutional-grade controls emerging in 2025. The convergence across major jurisdictions around reserve-backed models with explicit asset composition limits, frequent attestation requirements, and bankruptcy-remote custody structures reflects global recognition that stablecoins performing payment functions require prudential oversight. The case studies examined—particularly the SVB-USDC depeg and Terra Luna collapse—illustrate both the importance of conservative reserve design and the reality that even best-designed frameworks face vulnerabilities during systemic stress.

The GENIUS Act, MiCA, and Singapore MAS frameworks establish baseline standards for reserve quality, custodian qualification, and attestation frequency that collectively create more robust stablecoin infrastructure than existed previously. However, significant challenges remain, particularly regarding the evolution of yield-bearing products that create incentives for reserve composition expansion, the integration of stablecoins into tokenized asset markets, and the ultimate trajectory of stablecoin adoption relative to emerging central bank digital currencies. The regulatory frameworks examined in this report represent a significant step forward in formalizing stablecoin oversight, but they remain provisional frameworks subject to evolution as markets mature and new vulnerabilities emerge.

The coming years will test whether contemporary reserve frameworks prove adequate for stablecoin ecosystem scaled to trillions in transaction volume and whether custody infrastructure can reliably deliver the asset accessibility guarantees that stablecoin stability requires. The success of these arrangements will determine whether stablecoins mature into core financial market infrastructure or remain specialized instruments for narrow use cases.
