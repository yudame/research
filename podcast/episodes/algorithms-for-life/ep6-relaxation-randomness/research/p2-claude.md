## Research Output

# When breaking the rules beats following them

**The most powerful problem-solving strategies in computer science — relaxation and randomness — have direct, evidence-backed parallels in human life.** Relaxation (loosening constraints to find solutions) has driven breakthroughs from Apollo 13's survival to Slack's $27.7 billion pivot. Randomness (introducing chance into deliberate processes) accounts for an estimated 17–33% of major scientific discoveries and, according to a landmark LinkedIn experiment with 20 million users, moderately weak social ties generate the most career mobility. Perhaps most striking: a large-scale coin-flip experiment by economist Steven Levitt found that people who made major life changes — quitting jobs, ending relationships — were **substantially happier six months later**, suggesting humans systematically err on the side of too much caution. But these strategies have lethal failure modes. Boeing's relaxation of safety constraints killed 346 people; financial deregulation cost the U.S. economy over $20 trillion. The critical skill is not choosing exploration over structure, but knowing which context demands which approach.

## "I don't care what anything was designed to do"

On April 13, 1970, an oxygen tank exploded aboard Apollo 13, stranding three astronauts 200,000 miles from Earth. Carbon dioxide was building to lethal levels in the Lunar Module, but the Command Module's CO₂ filters were square and the Lunar Module's receptacles were round. No replacement parts could be sent. Flight Director **Gene Kranz** issued a directive that captures the essence of constraint relaxation: "I don't care what anything was designed to do." Engineers dumped every available material — hoses, plastic bags, cardboard, duct tape, a flight manual cover — onto a table and built the "mailbox," a jury-rigged adapter. All three astronauts returned safely on April 17.

This pattern — abandoning an assumed constraint to unlock a solution — recurs across domains with remarkable consistency. In 1965, Caltech graduate student **Gary Flandro** at JPL relaxed the assumption that spacecraft must travel in direct paths to distant planets. By allowing indirect trajectories using planetary gravity assists during a once-in-175-years alignment, Voyager 2's flight time to Neptune dropped from **30 years to 12**, visiting all four giant outer planets on a fraction of the originally required fuel. Both Voyagers still transmit data more than 45 years after launch.

The James Webb Space Telescope faced a similar impossibility: its 6.5-meter mirror couldn't fit inside any rocket's 4.5-meter payload fairing. Engineers relaxed the monolithic mirror constraint, creating 18 hexagonal beryllium segments that fold for launch and unfold in space, aligning to within **50 nanometers** — one ten-thousandth the thickness of a human hair. Each segment was deliberately ground to the *wrong* shape at room temperature, designed to warp into perfection at its operating temperature of −233°C.

Military history offers equally dramatic examples. When the Soviet Union blockaded all land access to West Berlin in June 1948, conventional wisdom held that a city of two million people **could not be sustained entirely by air**. Major General **William Tunner** relaxed this constraint and engineered a continuous-flow aerial supply system: standardized aircraft, landings every 45 seconds at peak, and Tegel Airport built from scratch in 90 days. Over 15 months, **277,000 flights delivered 2.3 million tons** of supplies. The Soviets lifted the blockade. During Desert Storm in 1991, General Schwarzkopf's planners relaxed the constraint that Iraq's western desert was "impassable" for armored formations, secretly shifting 500,000 troops 150 miles west. The ground war lasted **100 hours**.

In medicine, Australian gastroenterologist **Barry Marshall** confronted a century-old dogma: the stomach is too acidic for bacteria to survive. When his 1982 findings linking *Helicobacter pylori* to peptic ulcers were rated in the bottom 10% by the Gastroenterological Society of Australia, Marshall — unable to obtain permission for human experiments — drank a broth of cultured bacteria himself. He developed gastritis within days, treated himself with antibiotics, and eventually won the **2005 Nobel Prize**. Peptic ulcers went from a chronic surgical condition to a disease curable with a short course of antibiotics, saving Australia alone an estimated $300 million per year.

The startup world has its own vocabulary for relaxation: the pivot. Stewart Butterfield spent 3.5 years building a browser-based game called Glitch. When it failed, he relaxed the assumption that his company was a gaming company and recognized that the internal communication tool his team had built was the real product. Slack launched in 2013 and was acquired by Salesforce in 2020 for **$27.7 billion**. Instagram's founders stripped their cluttered check-in app Burbn down to a single feature — photo sharing with filters — reaching one million users in two months and selling to Facebook for $1 billion just 551 days after launch. YouTube abandoned its original concept as a video dating site ("Tune In Hook Up") after failing to attract users even by offering women $20 to upload videos. Opening to all content categories led to Google's $1.65 billion acquisition within 18 months.

## Why temporarily accepting worse leads to permanently better

Simulated annealing — the optimization algorithm that escapes dead-end solutions by temporarily accepting worse ones — finds its strongest human parallel in Levitt's 2020 coin-flip experiment, published in *The Review of Economic Studies*. Over 22,500 participants who were genuinely undecided about major life changes agreed to let a virtual coin flip decide. Those told to make a change were 11 percentage points more likely to do so, and at six months, people who quit jobs or ended relationships reported being approximately **2.2 points happier on a 10-point scale**. Third-party verifiers (nominated friends) corroborated these self-reports. Levitt's conclusion was blunt: "A good rule of thumb in decision-making is, whenever you cannot decide what you should do, choose the action that represents a change."

Career change research consistently reveals a **J-curve pattern**: an initial pay cut followed by recovery and satisfaction gains. An Indeed survey of career changers (average age 39) found **88% reported being happier**, with 58% having willingly accepted a pay cut. PayScale data suggests career changers who leverage transferable skills surpass their previous earning potential within four to six years. A 15-year longitudinal study in the *Journal of Vocational Behavior* (2022) found that horizontal career transitions — lateral moves and field changes — had a particularly strong positive impact on younger workers' long-term salary progression. Pew Research Center's 2022 analysis of Census data confirmed that job switchers during 2021–2022 were more likely to see real wage gains than stayers, whose median real wages actually declined 1.6%.

The evidence on later-life entrepreneurship is particularly compelling. A landmark NBER study by Azoulay, Jones, Kim, and Miranda (2020), drawing on U.S. Census data covering **2.7 million company founders**, found that the mean age of founders of the top 0.1% fastest-growing ventures was **45 years old** — not 25. A 50-year-old founder was **1.8 times more likely** to build a top-growth firm than a 30-year-old. This held even in high-tech sectors, entrepreneurial hubs, and among venture-capital-backed firms. The Kauffman Foundation reported that by 2019, over 25% of new entrepreneurs were aged 55–64, up from roughly 15% in 1996.

Divorce research mirrors the simulated annealing curve with striking precision. Gardner and Oswald's longitudinal analysis of the British Household Panel Survey (11 waves, 10,000+ individuals) found that divorce causes significant short-term psychological distress, but two years post-divorce, individuals of both genders showed measurable improvement in mental wellbeing compared to two years before. A 2024 study in the *Journal of Happiness Studies* using nine waves of Australian longitudinal data confirmed the pattern: **stable life satisfaction before dissolution, sudden decline, then long-term increases**. The critical caveat: these benefits emerged from *voluntary* disruption. The OECD's 2024 report on displaced workers found that involuntary job loss produces persistent earnings losses — **40% lower earnings** five years later — underscoring that strategic choice, not disruption itself, drives positive outcomes.

Geographic mobility research tells a similar story with important qualifiers. A study of 345 women in academic medicine found that those who relocated had **168% higher odds of promotion** than those who stayed. But an Italian longitudinal study found that migration benefits accrued primarily to men; women, especially "tied movers" following partners, experienced occupational disadvantages. The research on sabbaticals is more equivocal — Davidson et al. (2010) in the *Journal of Applied Psychology* found reduced stress, increased self-efficacy, and burnout relief, but also a "fade-out effect" where gains diminished upon return to routine. Sabbaticals spent abroad showed the strongest and most durable benefits.

## Chance favors the networked mind

Alexander Fleming's 1928 discovery of penicillin is the canonical serendipity story, but its details reveal something deeper than luck. Fleming noticed that a *Penicillium* mold contaminating an uncovered Petri dish had killed surrounding *Staphylococcus* bacteria. Yet the discovery languished for a decade — it took Howard Florey and Ernst Boris Chain at Oxford to purify and mass-produce penicillin, earning all three the 1945 Nobel Prize. The pattern repeats: Spencer Silver at 3M accidentally created a "low-tack" adhesive in 1968 while trying to make a super-strong one. He promoted his "solution without a problem" for six years before Art Fry — attending one of Silver's seminars — realized it could anchor bookmarks in his church hymnal. Post-it Notes launched nationwide on **April 6, 1980**, after 3M's "Boise Blitz" sampling campaign showed 90% purchase intent. The canary yellow color was accidental — it was simply the color of scrap paper available in the adjacent lab.

Pfizer's experience with sildenafil illustrates serendipity's cascading potential. Originally developed for angina at Pfizer's Sandwich, UK laboratories, the compound showed disappointing cardiac results in 1991 clinical trials. But male participants reported an unmistakable side effect. Pfizer redirected the entire research program, and Viagra received FDA approval in March 1998 — generating over one million prescriptions within weeks. The drug was subsequently approved for pulmonary arterial hypertension under the brand name Revatio, turning a single failed experiment into two major therapeutic applications.

Academic research suggests these stories are not outliers. Surveys estimate that **17% to 33%** of major scientific discoveries involve serendipitous elements. Christian Busch's 2024 systematic review in the *Journal of Management Studies* identified three conditions distinguishing serendipity from mere luck: agency (active human involvement), surprise (an unexpected element), and value (a beneficial outcome). A 2025 *Scientometrics* analysis of Nobel Prize discoveries found a "soft role of serendipity powered by hard tools" — virtually no major discoveries occurred without applying new methods or instruments, suggesting that **methodological innovation creates the conditions for productive accidents**.

The strongest experimental evidence for engineering serendipity comes from a Harvard Business School field experiment by Lane, Lakhani, and colleagues, published in *Strategic Management Journal* (2021). At a medical research symposium, researchers tracked **15,817 scientist-pairs** using sociometric badges and followed publication records for six years. Scientists who shared some overlapping interests acquired more knowledge and coauthored **1.2 additional papers**. But scientists from the same field cited each other three to seven times less, suggesting that too much similarity breeds competition rather than collaboration.

Mark Granovetter's "Strength of Weak Ties" (1973) — now the **most cited work in social science** with over 78,000 Google Scholar citations — provides the theoretical foundation. Weak ties (casual acquaintances) bridge otherwise separate social clusters, carrying novel information that strong ties (close friends) cannot. A massive 2022 LinkedIn experiment published in *Science*, involving over 20 million users across five years of randomized algorithm changes, found an **inverted U-shaped relationship**: moderately weak ties maximized job mobility, with diminishing returns at the weakest extreme. Weak ties proved most valuable in digital industries; strong ties mattered more in traditional sectors.

Organizations have tried to architect these collisions. Steve Jobs redesigned Pixar's headquarters around a single massive atrium containing the only restrooms, mailboxes, café, and screening rooms — forcing cross-disciplinary encounters. John Lasseter reported: "I kept running into people I hadn't seen for months." The MIT Senseable City Lab's 2017 analysis of 40,358 papers confirmed the underlying physics: researchers in the same building are **33% more likely to collaborate** than those in different buildings; on the same floor, **57% more likely**. Bell Labs' legendary Murray Hill corridor — longer than two football fields, connecting all laboratory spaces — was deliberately designed by physicist Mervin Kelly to create exactly these conditions, producing the transistor, laser, information theory, and multiple Nobel Prizes.

Creative practitioners have developed practical randomness tools. Edward de Bono's random word technique forces connections between a problem and an arbitrarily chosen noun, breaking cognitive fixation. Brian Eno and Peter Schmidt's **Oblique Strategies** cards (1975) — containing directives like "Honor thy error as a hidden intention" and "Work at a different speed" — were used during David Bowie's Berlin Trilogy and have remained in continuous use for 50 years. Their effectiveness is supported more by sustained professional adoption than by controlled studies, though neuroscience research using fMRI confirms that exposure to novel stimuli activates frontal brain regions associated with creative ideation.

## When the rules exist for a reason

The same principle that saved Apollo 13 killed 346 people aboard two Boeing 737 MAX aircraft. Boeing relaxed engineering safety constraints in pursuit of speed and cost savings after its 1997 merger with McDonnell Douglas. The MCAS software system was designed to mask aerodynamic problems caused by relocating larger engines, rather than redesigning the aircraft properly. In 2016, Boeing successfully lobbied the FAA to **remove references to MCAS from the flight manual**, hiding the system's existence from pilots. A 2012 simulation showed a test pilot taking 10 seconds to respond to an uncommanded MCAS activation — classified as "catastrophic" — but Boeing never reported this to regulators. The crashes cost Boeing over **$20 billion** and a criminal fraud settlement of $2.5 billion.

Financial deregulation tells the same story at systemic scale. The Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act (1999) repealed Glass-Steagall provisions separating commercial and investment banking. The Commodity Futures Modernization Act (2000) exempted credit default swaps from regulation. A system of voluntary regulation effectively "outsourced the job of monitoring risk to the firms themselves." Home mortgage debt rose from 46% of GDP to 73%. The Financial Crisis Inquiry Commission identified "dramatic failures of corporate governance and risk management." The estimated cost to the U.S. economy exceeded **$20 trillion in lost GDP**.

Facebook's original motto, "Move fast and break things," encapsulates the philosophy of constraint removal in technology. The consequences included the Cambridge Analytica scandal, facilitation of election interference, and AI systems producing racially biased bail decisions. As one LSE analysis noted: "To move fast and break things also means that someone else, somewhere, will be left to clean up the mess."

The research on High Reliability Organizations (HROs) — nuclear aircraft carriers, air traffic control, nuclear power plants — identifies the opposite principle. Karl Weick and Kathleen Sutcliffe's five HRO principles emphasize preoccupation with failure, reluctance to simplify, and sensitivity to operations. Atul Gawande's *Checklist Manifesto* demonstrated that the WHO Surgical Safety Checklist reduced surgical deaths by **47%** and major complications by 36% across eight global centers. In these domains, randomness and improvisation are not creative forces but existential threats.

The **Cynefin framework**, developed by Dave Snowden and published in *Harvard Business Review* (2007), offers the clearest boundary conditions. It distinguishes five decision contexts: Clear (follow best practices), Complicated (analyze with experts), Complex (probe with safe-to-fail experiments), Chaotic (act immediately to stabilize), and Disorder (clarify which domain applies). **Relaxation and randomness belong in the Complex domain** — where cause-and-effect relationships are discoverable only in retrospect and instructive patterns emerge through experimentation. In Clear and Complicated domains, they are dangerous. The critical boundary: Clear and Chaotic domains sit adjacent in the framework, emphasizing that removing too many constraints from a well-understood system doesn't produce creativity — it produces chaos.

The explore-exploit tradeoff from multi-armed bandit theory provides a complementary temporal framework. As Christian and Griffiths articulate in *Algorithms to Live By*: "Explore when you will have time to use the resulting knowledge; exploit when you are ready to cash in." The formal **37% Rule** (from optimal stopping theory) suggests spending the first 37% of your available time or options gathering information, then committing to the next option that exceeds your best-so-far. The implication is age-dependent: young people should lean heavily toward exploration; established professionals toward exploitation.

## A toolkit for tomorrow morning

Five practical frameworks emerge from this research, each applicable at different scales:

**Constraint inventory.** Eliyahu Goldratt's Theory of Constraints, introduced in his 1984 novel *The Goal* (seven million copies sold), provides a five-step cycle: identify the constraint limiting overall throughput, exploit it (maximize capacity with existing resources), subordinate everything else to it, elevate it (invest in expanding capacity), then repeat. For personal use, this translates to listing every assumption constraining a decision, categorizing each as physical (genuinely immovable), legal (imposed by regulation), or self-imposed (assumed but untested), and then systematically questioning the self-imposed ones. Most people discover that **the majority of their constraints are self-imposed**.

**Randomized connection programs.** Random coffee meetings — where an algorithm pairs people for casual conversations with no agenda — have demonstrated measurable impact. MIT studied call center workers at Bank of America and found that synchronized coffee breaks, which increased random interaction, produced productivity gains the company valued at **$15 million per year**. Tools like Donut (Slack integration), CoffeePals (Microsoft Teams), and the open-source Beans app (built by Yelp, available on GitHub) automate the matching process. The Lane et al. Harvard experiment suggests optimizing for *moderate* knowledge overlap — pairing people who share some interests but work in different fields.

**Strategic quitting criteria.** Annie Duke's *Quit* (2022) offers the most actionable framework: before beginning any venture, write explicit "kill criteria" — specific, measurable conditions under which you will quit. This pre-commitment sidesteps the sunk cost fallacy and escalation of commitment that cause systematic over-persistence. Duke advocates designating a "quitting coach" with explicit permission to deliver hard truths, maintaining an opportunity-cost ledger (tracking what you're *not* doing), and holding dedicated quit reviews separate from progress reviews.

**Calibrated coin flips.** Levitt's research suggests a specific protocol for decisions where you're genuinely stuck: frame the decision as "change vs. status quo," flip a coin, and commit to following it for a defined trial period. The evidence indicates that for major life decisions, the change option produces better outcomes on average — not because randomness is inherently superior, but because **humans exhibit a systematic status quo bias** that the coin flip corrects. The key qualification: this applies specifically to decisions where you've deliberated extensively and remain on the fence, not to decisions where one option is clearly better.

**Structured sabbatical planning.** The research supports a phased approach: 11 months of advance planning (the average for successful career changers), financial runway covering the break plus three to six months afterward, explicit goals that leave room for serendipity, and a re-entry buffer period. Davidson et al.'s finding that sabbaticals spent abroad produce the strongest benefits suggests that geographic displacement amplifies the "annealing" effect — physically removing yourself from your local optimum.

## The asymmetry humans get wrong

The deepest insight from this research is an asymmetry in human error. In personal decisions — career changes, relationship exits, geographic moves — the evidence consistently shows that people are **too cautious**, clinging to local optima that simulated annealing would escape. Levitt's study, the career change literature, and the entrepreneur age data all point the same direction: the marginal person considering a major life change should probably make it. But in organizational and systemic decisions — engineering safety, financial regulation, technology deployment — the evidence shows the opposite error: institutions are **too eager** to relax constraints, with catastrophic consequences when safety margins are treated as inefficiencies to be optimized away.

This asymmetry maps neatly onto loss aversion at the individual level (where losses loom larger than gains, producing excessive caution) and moral hazard at the institutional level (where decision-makers don't bear the costs of failure, producing excessive risk-taking). The practical implication: individuals should generally push themselves toward more exploration and disruption than feels comfortable, while institutions should maintain more constraints and redundancy than feels efficient. The Apollo 13 team succeeded not by abandoning all constraints but by relaxing exactly the right ones — the constraint on equipment purpose — while maintaining the ones that mattered most: the laws of physics, the timeline for CO₂ buildup, and the imperative to bring three humans home alive.
