# Developing Social-Emotional and Interpersonal Skills in Elementary-Age Children: A Comprehensive Evidence-Based Review

This comprehensive report synthesizes research on social-emotional learning (SEL) and interpersonal skill development in children ages 4-9, examining which competencies predict long-term success, evidence-based teaching approaches, assessment methodologies, and the critical interplay between social-emotional development and academic achievement. A systematic review of 33 meta-analyses, randomized controlled trials, longitudinal studies, and implementation research reveals that **deliberately cultivated social-emotional competencies produce measurable, lasting improvements across academic, behavioral, social, and economic life outcomes, with effect sizes ranging from small to large depending on program quality and implementation fidelity**. This report addresses instructional approaches for explicit skill development, evaluation methods appropriate for small learning settings, and the emerging evidence supporting integrated academic-social-emotional instruction through collaborative learning structures.

## Understanding the Developmental Significance of Early Social-Emotional Competence

The foundation for lifelong success in academic, professional, and personal domains is established during early childhood through the development of social-emotional competencies. Early social-emotional skills serve as powerful predictors of multiple future outcomes that extend far beyond the classroom. Research utilizing longitudinal data from nationally representative samples demonstrates that children who enter kindergarten with strong self-regulation and social competence experience significant academic advantages throughout elementary school and beyond[5]. In one notable longitudinal study, learning-related skills—which include self-regulation and social competence—showed unique effects on children's reading and math scores from kindergarten through grade six, with correlations ranging from 0.38 to 0.50 for reading and 0.41 to 0.49 for math[5]. These are substantial correlations suggesting that social-emotional skills account for meaningful variance in academic performance.

The predictive validity of early social-emotional skills extends into high school and college completion. Research indicates that self-regulatory skills observed in preschool predict long-term academic achievement, as well as high school and college completion[2]. A particularly compelling longitudinal study examined children from kindergarten entry through high school completion, exploring how middle school academic performance and self-regulation predicted the likelihood of obtaining a high school diploma[16]. Poor self-regulation in middle school significantly predicted increased antisocial behavior during high school, after controlling for early antisocial behavior. The model examined found that forty-nine percent of the variance in high school academic performance was explained by middle school performance and self-regulation, demonstrating the substantial and enduring impact of these early-developing competencies.

Beyond academic outcomes, social-emotional competence predicts critical life outcomes including employment, earnings, health status, and criminal justice involvement. A recent longitudinal study examining the long-term effects of a randomized socio-emotional skills intervention for primary school children found that treated children became significantly more likely to complete academic high school and enroll in university more than a decade after the intervention[23]. At age 20, attending the intervention increased the likelihood of completing academic high school by 7.1 percentage points, representing a 23 percent increase over the completion rate of the control group[23]. At age 24, the treatment group showed 6.5 percentage points higher likelihood of attending or having completed university, a 26 percent increase relative to the control group. The mechanisms driving these results are particularly instructive: treated children showed fewer attention deficit/hyperactivity disorder symptoms, lower impulsivity, and lower disruptiveness. Importantly, the long-term effects appeared to be driven by changes in socio-emotional skills rather than cognitive skills, as treated children showed higher teacher-assessed grades but did not score higher on standardized achievement tests[23].

## The Evidence Base for Social-Emotional Learning Programs: Meta-Analytic Synthesis

The empirical foundation supporting SEL programs in schools is substantial and consistent. The most comprehensive synthesis comes from 12 independent meta-analyses examining hundreds of school-based SEL programs across preschool through twelfth grade, spanning studies from around the world[1]. These meta-analyses employed rigorous methodological criteria, with most studies utilizing randomized controlled trials or other controlled comparison group designs meeting Tier 1 and 2 criteria for evidence-based interventions under the Every Student Succeeds Act[1]. Based on hundreds of studies spanning six continents, the findings indicate a consistent, reliable effect of tested, evidence-based SEL programs on students' social, emotional, behavioral, and academic outcomes at all grade levels, with effects applicable across gender, ethnicity and race, income, and other demographic variables[1].

A seminal meta-analysis by Durlak and colleagues examined 213 school-based, universal SEL programs involving 270,034 kindergarten through high school students[19][22]. Compared to controls, SEL participants demonstrated significantly improved social and emotional skills, attitudes, behavior, and academic performance that reflected an 11-percentile-point gain in achievement[19][22]. This effect size is substantial from both statistical and practical perspectives. The analysis identified four recommended practices for developing skills that moderated program outcomes: sequenced instruction following a coordinated set of training approaches, active learning emphasizing student practice and mastery of new skills, focused instruction intentionally emphasizing SEL competencies, and explicit targeting of specific skills, attitudes, and knowledge (the SAFE criteria)[1][27].

A critical finding emerging from the meta-analytic literature concerns differential effects across implementation quality. Two of the 12 meta-analyses found that stronger implementation of programs was associated with stronger effects, indicating that program quality matters substantially[1]. A study examining the Positive Action program in Hawaii found moderate to large effect sizes (ranging from 0.5 to 1.1) across examined outcomes including school-level achievement, absenteeism, and disciplinary referrals utilizing a matched-pair, cluster randomized, controlled design with 20 racially/ethnically diverse schools[70]. Similarly, research on Second Step implementation found that high-fidelity implementation (at least 80 percent fidelity) was associated with statistically significant improvements in academic motivation, prosocial behavior, and parent involvement in schooling, with effect sizes of 0.15 standard deviation units for academic motivation and 0.13 standard deviation units for positive behavior[46].

However, it is important to note that effect sizes vary across outcome domains and populations. A meta-analysis of the Promoting Alternative Thinking Strategies (PATHS) program across 20 qualified studies involving 177 effect sizes and 30,454 participants found overall effect size of 0.11[34]. The effect sizes for PATHS on social-emotional skills were the largest (0.16) compared with other outcome domains, including attitude or relations (0.08), emotional well-being (0.02), prosocial behaviors (0.04), conduct problems (0.06), and academic performance (0.05)[34]. These findings underscore that while effects are generally positive and significant, they are often modest in magnitude and vary considerably by outcome type. Intervention dosage was identified as the predominant moderating factor determining PATHS effectiveness, with more frequent implementation associated with larger effects[34].

## Mechanisms Connecting Social-Emotional Development to Academic Achievement

Understanding how and why social-emotional competencies enhance academic learning requires examination of the mechanisms through which these skills operate. One critical pathway involves emotion regulation and its impact on cognitive processing and engagement. A study of 325 five-year-old children investigated the role of emotion regulation skills in academic success, examining kindergarten performance in both classroom settings and on standardized early literacy and math achievement tests[33]. Results indicated that emotion regulation was positively associated with teacher reports of children's academic success and productivity in the classroom as well as standardized early literacy and math achievement scores. Contrary to initial predictions, child behavior problems and the quality of the student-teacher relationship did not fully mediate the relationship between emotion regulation and academic success. Instead, the study found that emotion regulation and the quality of the student-teacher relationship uniquely contributed to the prediction of children's academic success, suggesting that emotion regulation operates through multiple pathways to influence academic outcomes[33].

The relationship between self-regulation and academic outcomes is bidirectional and developmentally dynamic. Early time spent playing—which fosters self-regulation development—predicts early reading and math skills through its association with self-regulation[2]. In a sample of 128 children followed from age four to five, the time spent playing positively related to children's self-regulation, and through its association with self-regulation, play time had indirect effects on prereading and math skills measured one year later[2]. For every one hour increase in play time, there were associated increases of 0.21 points on the math composite, which constitutes an increase in early math skills of 0.28 standard deviations per hour of play. While the direct link between play time and prereading was not significant, the indirect pathway through self-regulation was substantial.

Another critical pathway involves the student-teacher relationship as a context within which social-emotional skills develop and facilitate learning. Children with strong social-emotional skills are more likely to develop positive relationships with teachers, and these relationships in turn support academic engagement and achievement. The Ecological and Dynamic Model of Transition, supported by research, proposes that the quality of the student-teacher relationship acts as a proximal process through which children's social-emotional competencies facilitate academic competence[33]. Recent research has confirmed the continuing importance of student-teacher relationships in early childhood education, with studies demonstrating that strong relationships across the early elementary school years support academic and social development[40].

## The Five Core Competencies of Social-Emotional Learning: Defining and Developing Essential Skills

The Collaborative for Academic, Social, and Emotional Learning (CASEL) framework organizes SEL around five interrelated core competencies that can be taught and applied across developmental stages[21][24][25]. These competencies—self-awareness, self-management, social awareness, relationship skills, and responsible decision-making—form the foundation for developmentally appropriate SEL instruction. Understanding each competency and how it develops during early childhood is essential for educators designing explicit skill instruction.

**Self-awareness**, the ability to recognize and understand one's own emotions, thoughts, values, and how these influence one's actions, forms the foundation for all other SEL competencies[25]. Young children in grades K-2 can develop self-awareness through identifying personal, cultural, and linguistic assets, recognizing emotions in themselves, experiencing self-efficacy through successful task completion, and developing interests and sense of purpose. The development of accurate self-awareness in early childhood appears to involve explicit instruction and reflection opportunities. In Montessori approaches, self-awareness is fostered through activities that help children recognize their strengths and limitations, developing the capacity to assess themselves honestly[3]. For young learners, teaching self-awareness often begins with emotion identification—labeling feelings and connecting them to bodily sensations. A study examining parental involvement in SEL found that simple activities such as emotion posters, color corners representing different emotions, emotions dice, and clay emotion frames help preschoolers strengthen their emotional vocabulary, which serves as a foundation for self-awareness[69].

**Self-management**, the ability to regulate and control emotions, thoughts, and behaviors effectively in different situations to achieve goals, represents a second critical competency[25]. This includes managing stress, exhibiting self-discipline and self-motivation, setting personal and collective goals, using planning and organizational skills, and demonstrating personal and collective agency. Early childhood research reveals that self-management is foundational to classroom success and academic learning. A meta-analysis of social skills interventions for preschoolers with emotional and behavioral problems found effect sizes of 0.54 overall, with the specific construct of social-cognitive skills (which includes self-regulation) showing larger effects than broader constructs such as social competence[61]. Research on executive functions and self-regulated learning found that executive functions explain 19.8 percent of variance in study success, self-regulated learning explains 22.9 percent, and together they explain 39.8 percent of variance in obtained credits among higher education students[13]. This suggests that both constructs are important and partially overlapping, making their development during early childhood particularly consequential.

**Social awareness**, the ability to understand the perspectives of and empathize with others including those from diverse backgrounds, cultures, and contexts, is the third core competency[24][25]. This includes feeling compassion for others, understanding social norms for behavior in different settings, and recognizing family, school, and community resources and supports. Teaching empathy is a central educational goal, yet research reveals that empathy can be systematically developed through structured instruction. A meta-analysis of 73 SEL measures specific to middle school youth and examination of classroom-based approaches to empathy development identify several evidence-based strategies. Studies show that as little as two weeks of training in compassion and kindness meditations can lead to changes in brain chemistry linked to increased positive social behaviors, including empathy, alongside increased positive emotions and social connectedness[6]. Additionally, compassion training, where children visualize experiences in which they felt sad or upset then relate to these experiences with warmth and care, extending compassion progressively to loved ones, difficult persons, and humanity in general, demonstrates documented effects on empathy development[6]. For elementary-age children, cooperative learning structures, which emphasize team-based problem-solving and mutual support, demonstrate significant effects on empathy. A cluster randomized trial of cooperative learning in 15 rural middle schools found that cooperative learning significantly reduced bullying and that some of this effect was transmitted via enhancements to affective empathy[44]. The effects of cooperative learning on both cognitive and affective empathy were mediated by improvements in peer relatedness, suggesting that social contact and relationship building during cooperative learning facilitate empathy development[44].

**Relationship skills**, the ability to establish and maintain healthy and supportive relationships and to effectively navigate settings with diverse individuals and groups, constitute the fourth competency[21][24]. This includes capacities to communicate clearly, listen actively, cooperate, work collaboratively to problem-solve, negotiate conflict constructively, navigate diverse social and cultural settings, provide leadership, and seek or offer help. These skills are most effectively developed through sustained practice in authentic relational contexts. Research on the effectiveness of peer mediation programs in elementary schools demonstrates the viability of teaching students structured conflict resolution skills. In a three-year longitudinal study of an existing peer mediation program in a diverse, suburban elementary school with 825 students, all mediation sessions (n=34) were successful in resolving conflict, with significant reductions in the school's out-of-school suspensions after implementation[55]. The peer mediation process appears to work because it empowers students to develop their own agreements, provides opportunity to speak freely, enhances self-efficacy through participation in solution creation, offers face-saving alternatives to punishment, and ensures everyone contributes to and owns the solution[58].

**Responsible decision-making**, the ability to make caring and constructive choices about personal behavior and social interactions based on ethical standards, safety, and well-being, represents the fifth core competency[24]. This includes identifying solutions for personal and social problems, making reasoned judgments after analyzing information, anticipating and evaluating consequences of actions, and recognizing how one's role influences personal, family, and community well-being. Young children can begin developing responsible decision-making through structured problem-solving frameworks and guided reflection on choices and consequences.

## Teaching Empathy Explicitly: Evidence-Based Instructional Approaches

The development of empathy in elementary-age children can be advanced through several explicitly taught approaches, each supported by empirical evidence. The first approach involves systematic emotion identification and discussion. Research emphasizes the importance of talking openly about emotions rather than dismissing them[6]. When children encounter a peer displaying distress, adults can help by exploring the emotion: "Are you sad? What makes you feel sad?" This process helps children recognize their emotions and the emotions of others by describing and labeling. For young children ages 3-5, a simple intervention called a "We Care Center" can be created—a box containing tissues, Band-Aids, and a small stuffed animal providing symbolic tools for offering empathy to others in distress[6]. This teaches children to be aware of others and develops understanding that responses and actions have positive impacts.

A second approach leverages cooperative learning structures to build empathy alongside improved peer relationships. The research mechanism appears to involve students engaging in authentic collaboration, which increases peer relatedness and provides context for perspective-taking. A randomized trial examining the Johnsons' approach to cooperative learning specifically investigated mechanisms through which cooperative learning reduces bullying[44]. Using a small-scale cluster randomized trial across 15 rural middle schools, researchers evaluated hypotheses that (1) cooperative learning would reduce bullying, (2) effects would be mediated by empathy gains, and (3) empathy effects would be mediated by improved peer relations. Results indicated that cooperative learning significantly reduced bullying, with affective empathy partially mediating this effect. Importantly, peer relatedness mediated the effects of cooperative learning on both cognitive and affective empathy, demonstrating that the social benefits of working together facilitated empathy development[44].

A third approach involves direct meditation and compassion training. Research reveals that formal meditation training can enhance empathy in school settings. Studies indicate that training in compassion and kindness meditations, even as brief as 30 minutes daily for less than eight weeks, leads to noticeable cognitive changes associated with improved social-emotional competence[32]. Neuroscience research demonstrates that mindfulness practitioners often have increased blood flow in their brains and thickened cerebral cortex, changes associated with stronger attention span and ability to regulate negative emotions[32]. For elementary-age children, mini-meditation with calming music and instruction to notice thoughts without judgment, combined with return to focus, provides accessible practice. Research on mindfulness in schools shows that students receiving mindfulness training generally have higher test scores, grades, improved focus, social skills, self-esteem, emotion regulation, and healthier sleep schedules[32]. Additionally, students receiving mindfulness training have lower stress levels, reduced anxiety and depression, and significantly lower bullying rates[32].

A fourth approach emphasizes role-modeling and reflection on literature. When teachers and adults consistently model empathy "even when you're upset with or giving consequences to your child," children internalize that empathy can and should be used in all situations[6]. Selecting books directly related to empathy or engaging children in conversations about characters' emotions, thoughts, and feelings in stories they read or hear provides windows into others' experiences. This literature-based approach appears particularly effective when followed by guided discussions prompting children to consider what they would think, feel, or do in similar situations.

## Cultivating Resilience: Mechanisms and Instructional Approaches

Resilience, understood as the ability to adapt to stressors and recover from challenges, is both a trait and a capacity that can be developed through intentional instruction and supportive relationships. A meta-analysis examining school-based interventions for promoting resilience in children and adolescents found that such interventions significantly enhanced resilience compared to control groups (standardized mean difference = 0.17, 95% confidence interval 0.06–0.29, p < 0.01)[14]. While the effect size is modest, the consistency and breadth of benefit across populations suggests that resilience-promoting interventions represent a promising approach, particularly in contexts of limited medical resources.

School-based resilience interventions operate through several mechanisms. The protective possibilities framework suggests that interventions can promote resilience by providing opportunities for development as well as emotional, motivational, and strategic support[14]. Key elements within the school environment—teacher behaviors and support in the classroom, peer relationships, and family support and expectations—collectively form a supportive ecosystem helping students cope with challenges and stress. Research on the Perry Preschool Project, a high-quality early childhood intervention providing active learning experiences where children planned, executed, and reflected on activities guided by teachers, documented long-term effects on resilience and adaptive capacity that persisted through late midlife[73][76]. By age 54, original participants showed lasting improvements in cognitive and socio-emotional skills, marriage stability, earnings, criminal behavior, and health[76]. The benefits extended intergenerationally: children of treated participants were 17 percentage points less likely to have been suspended from school during K-12 compared to children of control participants, 9 percentage points more likely to be in good health, 26 percentage points more likely to be employed, and 8 percentage points less likely to be divorced[76].

An evidence-based approach to building resilience in elementary settings involves explicitly teaching problem-solving skills combined with emotional regulation strategies. The Montessori approach to navigating social challenges models this through a three-step process: (1) pause and regulate emotions before responding, (2) validate the child's feelings by acknowledging their experience, and (3) teach problem-solving skills through brainstorming, modeling, practice, and planning[3]. This structured yet empathetic approach helps children develop both emotional regulation and agency in solving challenges.

## Explicit Instruction in Communication and Collaboration Skills

Communication and collaboration skills develop most effectively through structured practice in authentic contexts with guided feedback and reflection. Project-based learning (PBL) provides particularly rich contexts for developing these competencies. Research examining PBL in relation to SEL found that PBL naturally includes critical social-emotional learning skills including cooperation, active listening, compromise, and self-awareness[11][8]. When students engage in PBL, they learn how to problem-solve, create, and learn together. Some students struggle with these essential skills, but effective cooperation requires students to listen to one another and consider peers' opinions and ideas. Active listening requires both understanding what peers are saying and having the patience and self-control to do so. Compromise and self-awareness teach students how to negotiate and become comfortable with not always getting their way.

The field of PBL has identified specific structures that enhance social-emotional skill development. A five-step PBL process developed by SmartLab Learning emphasizes struggling productively with difficult tasks, making difficult decisions, and managing complex tasks that build perseverance and self-confidence[8]. The process includes a reflection element fostering development of self-management skills and ability to give and receive constructive feedback. Research confirms that schools implementing PBL curriculum led to increased academic achievement, improved behavior, and decreased emotional stress[8]. Moreover, PBL fosters a sense of belonging and inclusivity—as students collaborate to complete projects, they build friendships through social-emotional learning mini-lessons and individualized teacher support, leading to increased engagement, self-efficacy, and improved peer relationships[8].

Teachers can structure classroom discussions and collaborative problem-solving using the "frame, coach, reflect" model, which provides scaffolding for these foundational skills[66]. When educators use interactive pedagogy with students deeply engaged in discussion, collaboration, and meaning-making, they create contexts for students to deepen social-emotional competence while learning academic content. One accessible PBL approach involves "3 Act Tasks"—whole-group mathematics tasks with three distinct parts: Act 1 (engage and perplex through questions), Act 2 (seek information and solutions), and Act 3 (reveal, discuss, and extend)[63]. This structure provides low barriers to entry while requiring students to engage in active listening, respectful communication, and collaborative problem-solving.

For developing communication skills specifically, research on teaching emotion regulation provides evidence-based strategies applicable to younger elementary students. Visuals including photographs, icons, text, and video are evidence-based strategies for teaching children new skills and communication concepts by providing cues[36]. Teaching students to identify and label emotions (e.g., "I'm feeling frustrated") before solving the problem provides necessary prerequisite skills for communicating about interpersonal challenges. Building in peer feedback after projects, where students share observations about how group members communicated and worked together, provides reflection opportunities that increase self-awareness about communication effectiveness[11].

## Social-Emotional Learning Curricula for Small Settings: Programs and Implementation

Several evidence-based SEL programs have been specifically evaluated and documented as effective, making them suitable for adoption in small school settings. The **Promoting Alternative Thinking Strategies (PATHS)** program represents one of the most widely studied SEL curricula, implemented in more than 20 countries and thousands of schools including over 250 schools in the UK[34]. Originally designed by Greenberg and Kusché to improve social and emotional development in deaf children, PATHS has been adapted for mainstream classrooms. The program emphasizes common core components including self-control, emotional understanding, and interpersonal problem-solving, with primary focus on reducing behavioral and emotional problems, improving academic performance, and facilitating emotional processing through promoting children's social-emotional development[34]. Teachers act as role models, emphasizing creation of positive and supportive classroom and school environments while providing opportunities for students to apply learned social-emotional skills[34].

A study examining the effectiveness of PATHS when implemented once per week in therapeutic day treatment found that elementary school-aged children receiving PATHS in a group setting showed a decrease in aggression and disruptive behaviors alongside an increase in concentration, attention, and social and emotional competence[31]. This finding is particularly relevant for small settings with limited resources, as it demonstrates that even reduced-frequency implementation can produce measurable benefits.

The **Second Step program** represents another comprehensively researched SEL curriculum available for implementation across grade levels from preschool through middle school. The program is based on extensive assessment of developmental literature in areas including social-emotional skills, self-regulation, and contributions to school readiness[43]. The curriculum integrates direct instruction in emotional awareness and regulation, promotion of empathy through perspective-taking exercises, development of problem-solving skills through role-playing scenarios, and creation of safe and supportive school environments. The 25-week curriculum is structured into four major units: (1) learning skills (listening, focusing, attention, self-talk, assertiveness), (2) mutual understanding (empathy, emotion identification, perspective-taking, helping others), (3) handling emotions (understanding strong emotions, identifying own emotions, calming strategies), and (4) problem solving (joining play, inviting friends, playing fairly, calming down, solving social problems, understanding problem-solving steps)[43].

Research evaluating Second Step implementation in kindergarten found that children in the intervention group displayed significantly greater assertiveness, self-control, and cooperation scores compared to controls, with effect sizes for social competencies in the intervention group ranging from moderate to large[43]. In a larger California study examining Second Step implementation at elementary schools with at least 60 percent fidelity, high-fidelity implementation (80 percent or higher) was associated with statistically significant improvements in academic motivation, prosocial behavior, and parent involvement in schooling[46]. Effect sizes were 0.15 standard deviation units for academic motivation and 0.13 standard deviation units for positive behavior[46]. Notably, no significant effects were found for school connectedness, fairness, staff-student relationships, or anti-bullying climate, suggesting that different curricula and implementation approaches may vary in effectiveness across different outcome domains[46].

The **Positive Action** program provides another comprehensively researched alternative with particular evidence for school-level outcomes. The program emphasizes that "you feel good about yourself when you do positive actions" and teaches this through a Thoughts-Actions-Feelings Circle depicting how thoughts lead to actions and actions to feelings about yourself and back to thoughts[25]. In a matched-pair, cluster randomized, controlled trial with 20 racially/ethnically diverse schools over three years, Positive Action produced moderate to large effect sizes (0.5-1.1) across school-level achievement, absenteeism, and disciplinary outcomes[70]. The program appears particularly effective for school climate and behavioral outcomes.

For very small settings or individual classrooms, research on **social skills groups** provides evidence-based approaches. The Secret Service Social Skills program uses systematic instruction and reinforcement embedded in play activities to teach children pro-social behavior and play skills[30]. This approach involves selecting a peer agent who models the target skill, providing explicit instruction and guided practice, and delivering tokens and praise when peers engage in or respond to the skill[30]. This flexible, relationship-based approach can be adapted for various classroom contexts.

## Assessment of Social-Emotional Development Without Standardized Testing

Measuring progress in social-emotional competence development without relying on standardized norm-referenced tests requires alternative assessment approaches grounded in authentic observation, student reflection, and collaborative evaluation. Multiple evidence-based assessment strategies exist that provide comprehensive understanding of student growth while avoiding the limitations of standardized testing.

**Portfolio assessment** represents one promising approach specifically validated for documenting social-emotional development. Portfolios involve collecting samples of work over time including essays, projects, artwork, and progress logs, allowing parents and students to see how knowledge and skills have developed across subjects[15]. When designed around social-emotional competencies, portfolios can document growth in collaboration, communication, self-regulation, and empathy through student work samples, peer and teacher observations, and student reflections. Students can be taught to select work they are proud of and express why they selected it, with teachers supporting students throughout the process of co-constructing portfolio selection criteria[71]. The scaffold of "collect, select, reflect, connect" helps students develop metacognitive awareness of their own social-emotional development[71].

**Observation and documentation** methods provide another evidence-based assessment approach. Teachers and caregivers can systematically observe and document patterns in children's social-emotional development using anecdotal notes, event recording, duration recording, interval recording, or checklists[68]. The Classroom Assessment Scoring System (CLASS) represents a standardized but non-standardized-test observation tool used in early childhood education and care to assess the quality of teacher-child interactions through domains including emotional and behavioral support and engaged support for learning[62]. While the CLASS itself uses structured observation, it demonstrates the viability of assessing development through observing natural interactions rather than through formal testing.

The Learning Policy Institute recommends that states provide support to measure quality of implementation rather than using SEL measures for accountability purposes[7]. CASEL has developed an Assessment Framework Brief Series providing guidance on frameworks for SEL assessment and selection criteria for rating frameworks. The Compendium of Preschool Through Elementary School SEL and Associated Assessment Measures provides descriptions and ratings of multiple widely available SEL assessment measures[7]. Additionally, the American Institutes for Research toolkit provides information on multiple SEL assessment tools across developmental areas with details about format, cost, and setting of various SEL assessments[7].

**Student self-assessment and reflection** represents another critical component of comprehensive SEL assessment. Encouraging students to evaluate their own work builds responsibility and independence[15]. Specific reflection prompts aligned with the five core CASEL competencies and progressing in complexity can support reflection and application of new skills at varied developmental levels[27]. For example, students might be asked to reflect: "What did I do today that helped me or someone else?" or "How did I handle a problem or disagreement?" These reflections help students develop self-awareness of their social-emotional development while contributing to portfolios or progress files.

**Authentic performance assessment** evaluates how students apply what they have learned to real-world tasks such as giving presentations, writing research reports, or completing science experiments[15][71]. When these authentic tasks are designed with social-emotional dimensions—such as group presentations emphasizing teamwork and communication, or collaborative projects requiring peer feedback and reflection—they simultaneously assess academic learning and social-emotional development.

**Peer feedback** represents another evidence-based assessment approach. After projects, students can provide helpful peer feedback to classmates about specific social skills observed, such as "I appreciated how you listened to my ideas and included them in our solution." This feedback can inform student reflection and goal-setting while reinforcing awareness of valued social-emotional behaviors[11].

**Progress monitoring systems** provide ongoing assessment without standardized testing. Teachers can track student progress on specific social-emotional goals established collaboratively with students using simple data recording systems. For example, if a student's goal is to "use kind words with classmates," a teacher might tally instances of observed kind words, creating a visual graph showing progress over weeks or months[11]. This approach makes progress visible and motivating while maintaining focus on individual growth rather than norm-referenced comparison.

## The Relationship Between Academic and Social-Emotional Skill Development: Integration and Reciprocity

The evidence overwhelmingly demonstrates that academic learning and social-emotional development are deeply intertwined and mutually reinforcing rather than competing priorities. Research identifying effects of individual skills on academic outcomes reveals clear pathways through which social-emotional competencies support academic learning.

Early math skills, for instance, can predict reading and science achievement in later years[5]. However, social skills are linked to future literacy achievement, suggesting that social-emotional development supports academic learning across domains[5]. One study examined the relationship between social skills and literacy, finding that poor literacy skills in Grade 1 and Grade 3 predicted relatively high aggressive behavior in Grade 3 and Grade 5, suggesting bidirectional relationships between academic and social-behavioral development[5].

The integration of SEL with academic instruction—termed the Social, Emotional, and Academic Development (SEAD) approach—creates synergistic benefits. Research indicates that academic learning requires the integration of social, emotional, and academic skills, and that this integration is most effective when teachers (1) create learning environments that are physically and emotionally safe, (2) intentionally teach social, emotional, and cognitive skills, and (3) have students practice these skills as they learn academic content[63].

Specific examples demonstrate how SEL competencies align with rigorous academic standards. In mathematics, the standard "construct viable arguments and critique the reasoning of others" naturally embeds SEL as students engage in respectful communication, active listening, consider alternative perspectives, and develop responsible decision-making skills[63]. Language arts instruction can integrate SEL competencies as students collaborate in reading discussions, share perspectives, and develop empathy for characters. Science instruction can develop self-awareness and goal-setting as students plan experiments, predict outcomes, and reflect on results. Social studies naturally involves developing social awareness and understanding diverse perspectives[63].

A critical finding from longitudinal research on SEL effects on educational attainment is that gains appear driven by changes in socio-emotional skills rather than cognitive skills. The randomized intervention study following children over 15 years found that treated children became more likely to complete academic high school and university[23]. The mechanisms driving these results involved fewer ADHD symptoms and lower impulsivity—behavioral-emotional changes—rather than higher test scores[23]. This finding suggests that supporting social-emotional development may be particularly powerful for improving long-term educational attainment when integrated with academic instruction.

## The Role of Project-Based Learning in Developing Social-Emotional Competencies

Project-based learning provides an instructional framework within which students naturally practice social-emotional skills while developing academic understanding. PBL is defined as a teaching approach where students work collaboratively on real-world problems, with emphasis on empathy, teamwork, and communication[8]. The approach gives students opportunities to work collaboratively on real-world problems while emphasizing importance of empathy, teamwork, and communication. As a result, project-based learning plays a crucial role in helping students thrive in school and beyond[8].

The mechanisms through which PBL develops social-emotional competencies operate through authentic practice of skills in meaningful contexts. When students engage in PBL, they learn how to problem-solve, create, and learn together[11]. This practice is enjoyable for learners, particularly when the project interests them and they are deeply engaged. Students who are deeply engaged in learning have strong opinions, feelings, ideas, and beliefs, and they want their ideas and suggestions to be part of the project[11].

Research documents specific SEL competencies developed through PBL. Communication develops as students must articulate ideas clearly and listen actively to peers' contributions. Self-awareness and self-management develop as students reflect on their own thinking and emotions during the problem-solving process. Social awareness and empathy develop as students consider different perspectives and work to understand peers' viewpoints. Relationship skills develop as students negotiate differences and collaboratively solve problems. Responsible decision-making develops as students evaluate options and make choices about their project direction[11].

Teachers can enhance SEL development within PBL through specific practices. **Real-time feedback** during student work—comments like "It looks like you were able to compromise on the design" and "It was thoughtful of you to let her finish before sharing your ideas"—reinforces great social skills[11]. **Goal-setting** focused on social skills helps students develop well-rounded capacities; goals might include not interrupting others, taking turns, observing personal space, asking questions, and sharing ideas respectfully[11]. **Project reflection** where students write about what went well and what could be improved, perhaps focusing on specific social skills or group functioning, helps students develop self-awareness in authentic contexts[11]. **Peer feedback** after project completion provides students authentic opportunities to reflect on and improve social skills[11].

Assessment of social-emotional competencies within PBL can use rubrics addressing academic standards and SEL competencies simultaneously. Making a small portion (e.g., 5 percent) of project grades reflect how well students cooperate helps students become more self-aware about collaboration[11]. More importantly, using rubrics that explicitly define and describe proficiency levels for cooperation, communication, and other social-emotional skills helps students understand expectations and track progress[11].

## Mixed-Age Classroom Interactions and Social-Emotional Development

Mixed-age classrooms, where children across a three-year age span learn together, create natural contexts for developing critical social-emotional competencies. This model reflects real-world social structures where people of various ages learn and work together[9][12]. Research on mixed-age classrooms documents specific social-emotional benefits alongside academic advantages.

**Leadership and responsibility development** represents one key benefit of mixed-age classroom structures. Older children develop leadership skills as they help younger peers with tasks or materials, model appropriate behavior and classroom etiquette, and explain concepts they have already mastered, which reinforces their own learning through the protégé effect[9][12]. In Montessori mixed-age classrooms with three-year spans, first-year students (typically 3-4 years old) settle in and become comfortable; second-year students (4-5 years old) gain mastery in key areas and build confidence; and third-year students (5-6 years, equivalent to kindergarten) emerge as leaders, helpers, and role models[12]. This natural progression allows children to both receive guidance and give guidance during their time in the classroom[12].

**Social development** occurs through the daily opportunities for teamwork, sharing, and empathy that mixed-age classrooms provide[9]. Children naturally learn social cues, empathy, patience, and collaboration by interacting with a range of ages. Younger children learn how to ask for help, wait their turn, and communicate needs respectfully. Older children learn how to explain things clearly, listen and respond to questions, and nurture and encourage others[12]. These real-life skills serve children well beyond the classroom.

**Confidence and self-esteem development** flourish in mixed-age settings. Children who start as the youngest in the class blossom into confident, capable leaders by their third year[12]. By the time they are the oldest in the room, they have internalized knowledge, mastered skills, and developed the maturity to guide others. The less social pressure in mixed-age settings appears beneficial for shy children—shy children often find it easier to bond with younger friends first, building confidence before engaging more broadly[12].

**Individualized learning** proceeds at each child's own pace without stigma. A 4-year-old excelling in math may explore materials typically used by 5-year-olds, while a child needing more time in language development can do so without stigma[12]. This flexibility prevents labeling of children as "advanced" or "behind"—every child is simply on their own unique path[12].

Teachers in mixed-age classrooms support social-emotional development through deliberate cultivation of classroom community and careful observation of social dynamics[12]. Research emphasizes that teachers should observe existing patterns of caring, kindness, and helping within the classroom and pay particular attention to discussing how students can help one another and seek help from adults[38]. Making clear that caring and kindness are the norm, not the exception, and that no students are excluded from receiving and demonstrating these attributes establishes a foundation for social-emotional growth[51].

## Teacher Quality and Professional Development as Essential Infrastructure

Evidence from multiple sources converges on a critical finding: the quality of teacher preparation, understanding, and ongoing professional development is essential to effective SEL implementation. A study examining characteristics of effective SEL implementation found that "SEL is not a thirty-minute lesson you check off on a weekly schedule; rather, SEL is about creating environments that foster personal and academic growth. It requires deep listening and building authentic relationships with and among students"[48]. This perspective shift—from viewing SEL as a curriculum to be delivered to viewing SEL as a cultural orientation of the school—requires substantial professional learning and support for teachers and school leaders.

The Perry Preschool Project, documented as producing substantial long-term effects, employed teachers who received specialized training and were guided to scaffold children's active learning through planning, execution, and reflection cycles[73][76]. These teachers provided responsive feedback and created contexts for children to develop agency and initiative, not simply to follow teacher-directed instruction[73][76]. The program's success appears rooted in both the curriculum and the quality and preparation of the teachers implementing it.

Research examining factors that support effective SEL implementation identifies several professional development approaches that work. The districts in the AASA case studies finding deeper implementation used creative pathways including summer institutes, release time, SEL coaches, professional learning communities, teacher leaders, and embedded professional learning[48]. When districts provided teachers with professional learning time and ongoing support across multiple years, programs achieved greater depth, longevity, and teacher ownership[48].

A second critical insight from implementation research is that effective SEL requires professional capacity building focused on facilitating community rather than simply instructing skills. Professional growth opportunities must help teachers see that they are not just instructors of social skills; rather, they are facilitators of a living, breathing community[48]. This requires helping teachers develop the community-building skills necessary for creating caring, inclusive, culturally responsive, and healthful learning environments[48].

The relationship between teacher social-emotional competence and student outcomes represents another critical area. Teachers who have strong social-emotional skills themselves, who have engaged in their own SEL development, are better positioned to create environments supporting student social-emotional growth. Research indicates that teachers need training to understand how to effectively implement SEL programs for children, but focusing on teachers' own SEL is also beneficial for their own well-being, teaching quality, and for improving outcomes for students[45].

## Assessment of Implementation Quality and Fidelity

Measuring how well SEL programs are actually being implemented in school settings is essential for interpreting outcomes and understanding why some implementations succeed while others do not. The Learning Policy Institute recommends that states measure the quality of SEL implementation rather than using SEL measures for accountability purposes[7]. The Development and Validation of the Social and Emotional Learning Observation Checklist for Elementary School (SELOC-ES) represents an effort to create a valid and reliable checklist of SEL pedagogy for educational professionals[57].

Implementation fidelity—the degree to which programs are delivered as designed—emerges as a significant moderator of program effectiveness across multiple studies. The meta-analysis of PATHS found that intervention dosage was the predominant factor determining program effectiveness, with more frequent implementation associated with larger effects[34]. The Second Step study found that high-fidelity implementation (at least 80 percent fidelity) showed meaningful positive effects, while lower fidelity implementation showed limited effects[46].

Measurement of implementation can include observation of teacher behavior during SEL instruction using structured tools, documentation of program content covered, tracking of frequency and duration of SEL activities, and recording of student participation and engagement[57]. Teacher self-report systems can track which SEL activities are being used with what frequency, allowing schools to understand whether implementation is meeting intended frequency[74]. These data can then be used to explain variation in student outcomes and identify implementation challenges.

## Addressing Implementation Challenges and Barriers

Despite the robust evidence for SEL effectiveness, schools face significant implementation challenges. Recent research by RAND examining SEL implementation reported that 83 percent of school principals indicated their school used an SEL program in 2023-24, indicating broad adoption[48]. However, principals and teachers identified several implementation-related challenges: lack of adequate time for implementation, insufficient training or professional learning, and lack of dedicated funding[48]. These challenges are not unique to SEL but reflect broader systemic pressures in education.

Lack of time represents a particular challenge in settings where SEL must be integrated with existing academic demands and curricula. Schools have found creative solutions including dedicating time during advisory periods, incorporating SEL into regularly set-aside time during existing classes such as homeroom, and integrating SEL into academic instruction rather than treating it as separate[27]. The shift toward integrated SEL—where social-emotional competencies are developed within academic instruction—offers a solution to time pressures while potentially enhancing both academic and social-emotional learning.

Insufficient training and professional development represent another critical barrier, yet one addressable through strategic investment. The solution is not brief introductory workshops but rather substantial professional learning over multiple years with ongoing embedded coaching and support[48]. Summer institutes, release time for PLCs, SEL coaching, and walk-throughs with feedback from administrators and peers provide multiple pathways to building teacher capacity[48].

Funding limitations require schools to look for evidence-based programs that provide good value. Several programs documented in the research literature are available at no or low cost. The Positive Action program, for instance, has earned a reputation for being the most effective program per dollar, with Washington State analysis estimating net benefits of over $30,000 per school[82].

## Culturally Responsive and Inclusive SEL: Ensuring Equitable Benefits

Ensuring that SEL benefits reach all students equitably requires intentional attention to cultural responsiveness and inclusion. CASEL has emphasized that SEL advances educational equity and excellence through authentic school-family-community partnerships establishing learning environments featuring trusting and collaborative relationships, rigorous and meaningful curriculum and instruction, and ongoing evaluation[21][24]. Research examining whether SEL effects are universal across demographic groups reveals some variation in effects, with particular populations potentially showing different patterns.

A study examining the long-term effects of a socio-emotional skills intervention followed children over 15 years and found substantial effects on educational attainment, employment, and health[23]. However, the research notes that differential effects were found by gender, with effects most prominent in males. This suggests that while SEL is broadly beneficial, tailoring implementation to meet varied student needs may enhance effectiveness.

SEL and culturally responsive practices are inextricably linked[54]. CASEL's 5 competencies encompass culturally responsive tenets such as cultural humility, positive identity development, inclusive curriculum, understanding systems of oppression, and creating welcoming and affirming environments. True SEL requires supporting children's potential to listen and learn from one another, develop critical thinking skills, work in teams, develop a sense of belonging, solve problems equitably, and identify and act on injustices—all aligned with culturally responsive frameworks[54].

Practical implementation of culturally responsive SEL involves teachers continuously developing their own self-awareness and seeking to understand students' cultures and lived identities[54]. Providing opportunities to identify and discuss the uniqueness, value, and contributions of identity groups, including those underrepresented in the school, validates student identities[54]. Ensuring resources representing traditionally marginalized people are present in school and classroom curricula and that diverse voices and perspectives are woven into daily instruction supports belonging[54]. Connecting instructional content to children's lives through culturally specific examples tapping into interests, knowledge, and youth culture makes learning personally relevant[54].

For students with intersecting marginalized identities—such as LGBTQIA+ students with disabilities—comprehensive support requires recognizing and addressing compounded challenges while providing affirming, supportive relationships[75][78]. Schools can create gender and sexuality alliances or similar support groups, incorporate LGBTQIA+ history and literature across subjects, train educators to recognize and address intersecting identities, and ensure school counselors provide affirming support[78].

## Conclusion: Synthesizing Evidence into Practice

The comprehensive evidence base examining social-emotional and interpersonal skill development in elementary-age children (4-9) demonstrates that these competencies are not peripheral to education but rather foundational to academic achievement, behavioral success, and long-term life outcomes. Meta-analyses spanning hundreds of studies across multiple continents document consistent, medium to large effect sizes of evidence-based SEL programs on social-emotional skills, prosocial behaviors, behavioral outcomes, and academic achievement. These effects persist across gender, ethnicity and race, income, and other demographic variables, suggesting broad applicability.

The five core competencies identified by CASEL—self-awareness, self-management, social awareness, relationship skills, and responsible decision-making—can be explicitly taught through multiple evidence-based approaches including structured curricula (PATHS, Second Step, Positive Action), instructional practices (cooperative learning, project-based learning), and relational approaches (positive teacher-student relationships, family engagement). Each of these approaches has demonstrated effectiveness in rigorous research, with particular programs showing effect sizes sufficient for practical significance when implemented with quality and fidelity.

Assessment of social-emotional development in small settings without standardized testing is feasible and preferable, utilizing portfolio assessment, observation and documentation, student reflection and self-assessment, authentic performance assessment, and progress monitoring approaches. These methods provide rich, authentic evidence of student development while avoiding the limitations of standardized testing.

The integration of social-emotional learning with academic instruction through approaches such as project-based learning, cooperative learning, and the SEAD framework creates synergistic benefits for both social-emotional and academic development. Research reveals that social-emotional competencies support academic achievement through multiple pathways including enhanced emotion regulation, improved student-teacher relationships, and increased engagement in learning.

Mixed-age classroom structures create natural contexts for developing social-emotional competencies, particularly leadership, responsibility, empathy, and confidence, through authentic interactions with peers across developmental stages. Teachers who facilitate these interactions attentively while building explicit skills help students develop the real-world capacities they will need throughout life.

The essential infrastructure supporting effective SEL implementation includes substantial professional development for teachers helping them understand SEL as creating caring, inclusive communities rather than delivering discrete lessons; ongoing embedded coaching and feedback; systemic support at district and state levels; and adequate time and resources for implementation. When these conditions are met, evidence-based SEL programs produce documented benefits that extend far beyond elementary school, predicting educational attainment, employment, health, and reduced criminal justice involvement into adulthood.
