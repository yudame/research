## Self-Directed Learning as a Foundational Capability in Middle Childhood

### The Architecture of Self-Directed Learning

Self-directed learning represents a pedagogical orientation fundamentally distinct from traditional teacher-centered instruction, positioning students as active agents in their own educational journey rather than passive recipients of predetermined knowledge.[1] The foundational definition establishes that during self-directed learning, the learner sets goals, determines how progress will be assessed, defines the structure and sequence of activities and a timeline, identifies resources, and seeks out feedback.[1] This constellation of skills—which encompasses planning, monitoring, resource identification, and metacognitive reflection—develops progressively throughout childhood, with particular flourishing possible during the elementary years when cognitive capacities expand rapidly and students begin exhibiting greater autonomous motivation.[1]

The implementation of self-directed learning in classroom settings with students ages 6-9 requires deliberate scaffolding and structure. Research indicates that the primary underpinning of SDL is that students take responsibility for learning well beyond what is presented by an external body such as a faculty member or curriculum.[1] In contrast to traditional educational settings where an instructor provides learning objectives, assessments, and resources, in SDL the learner initiates the process by setting learning objectives, identifying assessments that allow for meaningful feedback, and identifying resources to help them reach their goals.[1] When implemented with appropriate developmental sensitivity, SDL instills a sense of autonomy and mastery in the learner, and a sense of purpose into the educational process.[1] Most importantly, it supports the unlimited learning potential and creativity of students, with research documenting that in well-designed SDL environments, students' motivation increases, as well as their feelings of control, confidence, and belief in themselves.[1]

A critical distinction exists between self-paced learning and self-directed learning that educators must navigate carefully. Giving students two weeks to read a chapter for class is self-paced, but because students do not determine what they learn, it is not self-directed.[1] The learning goals and any needed resources are supplied by the instructor, leaving the essential element of agency absent. For young learners in the 6-9 age range, the development of these agentic capacities requires a systematic, scaffolded approach that gradually shifts responsibility from teacher to student over time.

### Six-Step Process and Developmental Scaffolding

Self-directed learning can be described as a six-step process: developing goals for study; outlining assessment with respect to how the learner will know when they achieve those goals; identifying the structure and sequence of activities; laying out a timeline to complete activities; identifying resources to achieve each goal; and locating a mentor or faculty member to provide feedback on the plan.[1] Research with elementary-aged students demonstrates that when these steps are made explicit and taught systematically, students gradually internalize the capacity to engage in each phase with decreasing adult support.[1]

A particularly important finding from educational psychology research is that developing self-directed learners requires a scaffolded approach in which more self-paced or teacher-directed activities are introduced early on, during didactic instruction, to help students become more self-regulated in their self-directedness.[1] Over time, as the student moves from the classroom to the experiential setting, control of the learning environment can be shifted from the instructor to the student incrementally. This scaffolding may include starting with more self-paced activities and providing explicit guidance to the learner on how to be more self-regulated. Health professions programs and increasingly elementary school programs employ several educational components to help develop SDL, including flipped classrooms, learning contracts, and minimal-guidance instruction.[1]

Learning contracts represent one evidence-supported tool for developing self-directed learning capacity in elementary students. A learning contract is an agreement between the instructor and student that specifies the work the learner will complete in a given time period.[1] Learning contracts can be used to keep individuals organized, normalize expectations, and increase communication between the learner and instructor. These contracts consist of five components, similar to that of the SDL process: learning objectives, learning resources and strategies, target date for completion, evidence of accomplishment, and criteria for evaluating.[1] These can be used within courses, as independent studies, or even to help guide extra-curricular or co-curricular activities.

### School-Wide Systematic Approaches

Research from successful implementations demonstrates that developing self-directed learners requires prioritizing SDL as a fundamental goal of schooling at the school and district levels.[46] The skills required for self-directed learning are unlikely to develop on their own, nor will these skills fully blossom from the actions of one or two teachers working alone.[46] Helping learners achieve effective self-direction requires a comprehensive approach that systematically develops key skills across grades and disciplines. Schools that have successfully implemented such approaches have identified key self-directed learning skills and mapped them out developmentally across grade levels.[46]

The ability to direct one's learning involves a constellation of skills, including setting learning goals, developing a learning plan, making needed adjustments, and reflecting on the learning results.[46] Effective self-directed learning also requires perseverance in the face of obstacles, and these skills and habits of mind can, and should, be cultivated by design.[46] Once schools have identified the skills they want to target, they need to articulate what the development of each skill looks like by designing a set of rubrics across developmental levels, with clear and age-appropriate learning targets that support focused instruction and guide assessment, including self-assessment by learners.[46]

## Mastery-Based Learning and Progression Systems

### Theoretical Foundations and Empirical Effectiveness

Mastery learning represents a distinct pedagogical framework from traditional education systems, built on the premise that all students can achieve mastery of content with sufficient time and appropriate support.[2] The approach involves specifying learning goals or objectives, developing formative assessments, organizing corrective activities, planning enrichment activities, and developing summative assessments.[2] Evidence suggests that students within a mastery learning model perform better academically than those in nonmastery learning models, with moderate to large effect sizes reported across meta-analyses.[2]

A meta-analysis of 108 controlled evaluations of mastery learning programs found that mastery learning had positive effects on the examination performance of students from elementary through college.[18] Of the 108 studies, 72 used Fred S. Keller's Personalized System of Instruction (PSI) method for college-level teaching and 36 used Bloom's Learning for Mastery (LFM) method to teach first grade through college-level students.[18] Of the 108 studies, 103 reported results from a final test given at the end of instruction, and all but seven reported positive effects for the mastery learning programs.[18] Statistically significant results were reported in 67 of the 96 studies with an average of about 0.5 standard deviations—enough to move students from the 50th to the 70th percentile.[18]

More recent meta-analysis provides additional precision regarding effect sizes. A meta-analysis on group-based mastery learning programs found that over the 46 studies reviewed, mastery learning demonstrated consistent positive effects.[15] Whiting and colleagues showed an improvement in grade point average by almost one and a half grades (for example, from C to B+) in classes taught by mastery learning, with 7,179 student participants.[2] The average grade in classes taught by mastery learning was an A (with scores ranging from 97% to 99%), with only 1.5% of students achieving a grade of F.[2] A study by Marshall supported these findings with a 10% increase in average semester grade between the mastery learning course and the traditional course.[2] This study also showed a reduction in formative assessment attempts throughout the course, demonstrating that students become more efficient at learning.[2]

### Implementation Components and Assessment Practices

The mastery learning approach addresses all three principles of Universal Design for Learning in that it typically has multiple ways for students to acquire the required knowledge, skills, and attitudes.[2] Consistent with multiple means of engagement, if a student does not acquire mastery in the first attempt, the corrective activities will address a different method of instruction.[2] Similarly, the various approaches to learning, the corrective activities, the enrichment activities, and the multiple chances and formats to demonstrate mastery capture all three principles of UDL.[2]

Regular formative assessment lies at the heart of mastery-based systems. Students complete a formative assessment based on established learning outcomes following initial instruction.[2] For students who accomplish the predefined level of mastery, enrichment activities are offered to supplement the students' understanding of the material.[2] Conversely, students who do not accomplish the predefined level of mastery are given corrective exercises specific to the concepts missed by the student.[2] These students are then given a separate formative assessment pertaining to the same learning outcomes and must demonstrate mastery prior to moving to the next unit.[2]

The structure emphasizes several critical principles. First, students are graded solely based on their performance on the final summative assessment.[2] Second, the student is assessed on predetermined standards and not relative to peers.[2] Third, students who attain the standard will receive the appropriate grade reward.[2] Fourth, throughout learning the student will be given a series of ungraded diagnostic assessments to promote and pace learning.[2] Finally, each student will be given all the help they need to learn.[2]

### Benefits for Student Self-Efficacy and Motivation

Beyond academic achievement measures, mastery learning produces important affective and motivational outcomes. Research indicates that mastery learning also encourages the notion of ability not being fixed early but prevalent to effort, practice, and feedback.[2] Students get to know that failing is not the last point but is a part of the learning process, promoting perseverance, resilience, and overall liking of challenges.[2] Students interviewed after being taught using mastery learning methods reported a high sense of confidence or self-efficacy for mathematics, high self-evaluation with their progress, and high goal standards.[15] The additional benefits of mastery learning instruction include an improvement in self-efficacy and a change in the types of goals students set, which often carry over into other classrooms, even those not using the mastery method of instruction.[15]

## Mixed-Age Learning Environments: Structure, Benefits, and Implementation

### Theoretical Rationale and Continuity of Care

Mixed-age grouping represents a deliberate departure from traditional age-segregated classroom organization, positioning students of different ages to learn together with multiple benefits for both younger and older students.[6] The fundamental rationale centers on providing stability for a group of children with their caregivers over a longer time period—also known as continuity of care.[6] Continuity of care is a term used to describe programming and policies that ensure children and families are consistently engaged in high-quality early learning experiences through stable relationships with caregivers who are sensitive and responsive to a young child's signals and needs.[6]

Research demonstrates that consistency of relationships is critical in supporting young children's development and learning.[6] Mixed-age groupings create a frame for deepening relationships for staff with children and their families, with studies showing that the caregiver-child attachment relationship is often more secure the longer a caregiver stays with children.[6] Frequent transitions to new caregivers can take a toll on children and families, as each new caregiver must learn to read a child's cues and to gain the trust of the family.[6]

Montessori-specific research reveals that mixed-age classroom design allows children to interact with peers both younger and older than themselves, and therefore encourages widely supported practices such as imitative learning, collaboration, and peer tutoring.[19] In some studies, only the younger students in mixed-age classrooms showed benefits like more goal-direction, peer interaction, and longer attention-spans, and exhibited fewer off-task behaviors and negative interactions with peers and teachers than students in same-age settings.[19] However, in other studies, social-emotional benefits extended to all ages of students, with children in three-year mixed-age classrooms experiencing more extended peer interactions, more frequent positive (as well as fewer neutral to negative) interactions, and less loneliness, aggression, and behavioral problems than their same-age grouped peers.[19]

### Peer Tutoring and Modeling Effects

A particularly powerful mechanism through which mixed-age groupings support learning involves the naturally occurring peer tutoring and peer modeling that emerge in such environments. Younger students may observe, question, and imitate the work of older peers, while children may collaborate on work with a material, with older students even presenting some lessons to their younger classmates.[19] The effectiveness of peer tutoring is reliably supported by evidence demonstrating that peers make successful tutors, that benefits apply to a diverse range of contexts and students, across both academic and social domains for both learner and tutor, and that they persist through time.[19]

Research specific to Montessori contexts has reinforced the notion that mixed-age settings bolster language development for younger students, with researchers hypothesizing that the mixed-age grouping of Montessori classrooms may promote more frequent peer-to-peer talk on the part of younger students via modeling by older students.[19] Montessori students do, in fact, consistently outperform their peers on tests of language and literacy, and while many factors might contribute to this advantage, mixed-age settings are one plausible reason.[19] In addition, Montessori students have been found to exhibit advanced social understanding, a characteristic that researchers often attribute to the increased opportunities for perspective-taking and collaborative work in mixed-age environments.

### Teacher-Child Continuity and "Looping"

Mixed-age groups also provide students with multi-year consistency of teacher, often referred to as "looping" in the literature.[19] The practice of looping is supported by evidence highlighting academic benefits that appear strongest amongst minoritized students, as well as higher performance on measures of personal and social development, and lower rates of absenteeism and suspension.[19] Furthermore, benefits to individual students increase in correlation with the proportion of looping time maintained.[19] Importantly, most looping research examines a two-year match between teacher and student, leading some researchers to hypothesize that Montessori's three-year match could confer additional benefits.[19]

Research acknowledges that a host of variables, such as classroom quality, level of teacher education, level of peer skill, classroom management, individual student engagement, and the degree of teacher-led activity, need equal consideration as factors that shape whether and when mixed-age classrooms prove beneficial.[19] Therefore, while studies that examine mixed-age groupings in isolation from other variables draw mixed and inconclusive results, research into some of the mechanisms known to facilitate the success of mixed-age groups—such as peer tutoring, teacher looping, student-led learning, and specially prepared teachers—may prove more enlightening.[19]

### Implementation Challenges and Teacher Preparation

Teachers implementing mixed-age grouping face legitimate design challenges. Designing a curriculum that meets the needs of a diverse age group can be complex, requiring teachers to balance varying academic levels and ensure that all students are adequately challenged and supported.[22] The teachers are likely to need specialized training to manage a classroom that caters to such a wide range of needs.[22] Resource issues and finding ways to evaluate student progress in such a diverse age range are other factors to consider.[22]

Yet evidence from schools successfully implementing mixed-age approaches demonstrates feasibility when proper structures are in place. One example observed mixed-aged classrooms in action at Françoise Dorléac Elementary School in northern Paris, where the teacher—working solo—managed the room by splitting her lesson into two parts: instruction and problem solving.[22] She began by teaching a grammar lesson to the older age group on one board, then after five minutes, a timer chimed out, the teacher tasked the older kids with a problem, and navigated to the younger age group to focus on them, with older children enthusiastically solving grammar problems while younger children traced cursive letters in the air.[22]

## Scaffolding Independence: Theoretical Foundations and Practical Applications

### Zone of Proximal Development and Scaffolding Theory

Scaffolding emerges from Vygotsky's conceptualization of the zone of proximal development (ZPD), which refers to the range of tasks a learner cannot yet complete independently but can accomplish with appropriate support.[39] The term "proximal" means "near" and describes skills the learner is close to mastering.[39] The ZPD represents the gap between what a learner can do alone and what they can achieve through guidance and collaboration with a more knowledgeable other (MKO), such as a teacher, parent, or peer.[39] This is the space where effective instruction should occur, enabling learners to internalize new skills and eventually perform them independently.[39]

Scaffolding is defined as providing assistance to a student when needed and fading the assistance as the competence of the student increases.[7] Research indicates that scaffolding facilitates learning as it supports learners in tasks that they cannot accomplish successfully by themselves, as well as developing knowledge for future learning.[7] Unlike scaffolding in an individual setting, student interaction scaffolding in a collaborative setting modifies student involvement and supports additional metacognitive activities which can influence learning.[7]

Scaffolding is anchored in three critical principles: contingency, fading, and transfer of responsibility.[21] Contingency is targeted support based on the student's current learning ability and needs.[21] Fading refers to the intentional withdrawal of assistance as the student's proficiency increases.[21] Transfer of responsibility occurs when the learner can independently complete the task.[21] To the untrained eye, effective scaffolding looks like tactical alchemy, as the teacher determines when support is needed, what tools to use, and the precise moment to step back and let the child proceed independently.[21] For students, the process should feel automatic, inevitable, like a clock chiming the hour.[21]

### Complementarity Between Material and Teacher Scaffolds

An important finding from recent research emphasizes the critical interplay between scaffolds embedded in instructional materials and scaffolding provided dynamically by teachers. One study explored how teachers' scaffolding complemented fading material scaffolds in a paper-and-pencil tool and how this combination of support impacted students' learning of science practices and content.[24] Differences in teachers' responsive versus static scaffolding moves corresponded with differences in students' performance as material scaffolds faded in support.[24] One teacher complemented support provided by the material scaffolds by frequently monitoring students' understanding and providing additional support as needed, even when material scaffolds faded; her students maintained a high level of performance throughout the unit.[24] In contrast, the other teacher tended to extend the static kind of scaffolding found in the instructional materials rather than adapt support to his students' needs as material scaffolds faded; his students showed a significant decrease in performance over time.[24]

These findings show that the complementarity between responsive scaffolding moves from the teacher and scaffolding embedded in instructional materials is important for effectively supporting the wide range of students' needs in the classroom.[24] A key feature of scaffolding is that it is temporary and eventually fades as learners take more responsibility for their learning.[24] Additionally, the level of support is tailored or calibrated to the student's knowledge and understanding at any particular point in time.[24] Both fading and calibrated support are hard to achieve in paper-and-pencil tools alone.[24]

### Strategic Use of Questions, Prompts, Cues, and Direct Explanations

If the goal of small-group instruction is to move students toward becoming independent readers or learners, it is critical to consider how questions, prompts, cues, and direct explanations are framed.[10] Educators will want to offer the scaffold that maximizes student ownership and engagement of the task.[10] Most often, this will mean starting with a question and then offering a prompt, if needed, followed by a cue if still needed.[10]

The strategic progression of scaffolding support works as follows: a teacher poses a question to check for understanding, with the intent not to assess but to uncover misconceptions or errors to gauge if additional scaffolding is needed, or whether students are ready for less-supported applications of learning or more rigorous tasks.[10] If the student cannot answer the question successfully, the teacher provides a prompt—a follow-up question to prompt students to do their own cognitive or metacognitive work.[10] If prompting is insufficient, the teacher shifts to a cue, which can be visual, verbal, or involve a gesture that helps the student form a relationship between the words on paper and a physical object or process.[10] Short of direct explanations, cues provide the most scaffolding support.[10] Finally, if cues are insufficient, the teacher provides a direct explanation, which is the most supportive level of the hierarchy.[10]

This strategic approach must be employed with careful attention to timing and calibration. Being deliberate and prepared through plenty of rehearsal regarding precise, concise language provides students with the opportunity to consider the questions, prompts, and cues in their own brain.[10] Educators can be confident that their response in the moment is intentional and appropriate.[10] Highly effective scaffolding does not simply materialize in the moment; it requires deliberate planning and practice by educators before teaching a lesson.[10] When educators prepare in an intentional way, increasing their understanding of the elements and nuances of what they will be teaching, they gain skill and confidence in discerning when and how to employ the right scaffolding routine, at the right time.[10]

### Metacognitive, Cognitive, and Procedural Scaffolding

Scaffolding provides tailored support through three approaches with distinct purposes and mechanisms.[21] Cognitive scaffolding is the general process by which a teacher provides targeted guidance and resources to help learners gain new skills that are initially beyond their independent abilities.[21] Metacognitive scaffolding supports learners in planning, monitoring, and evaluating their own learning strategies.[21] Procedural scaffolding guides students through the steps required to complete tasks.[21]

Metacognitive scaffolding through structured reflection cultivates the skills necessary for self-regulation and independent learning.[21] Checklists help students plan, monitor, and evaluate their task progress, building organizational skills and reinforcing progress.[21] Concept clouds—tools like EdWordle or Mentimeter—improve their essays, allowing learners to plug their writing into a word cloud site and see whether their intended main ideas came across as important, or incorporate new words for those that are often repeated throughout their writing.[21] A learning contract outlines actions the learner promises to take in a course to achieve academic success, and reverse brainstorming encourages students to consider what might hinder their success before identifying solutions, promoting reflection and self-assessment.[21]

Procedural scaffolding techniques keep learners focused, reduce cognitive overload, and build independence by breaking tasks into manageable steps and offering guidance at each stage.[21] Task cards are a versatile teaching tool that allows for differentiation, featuring questions, challenges, or instructions that guide students through an activity.[21] Process prompts—a specific type of sentence stem—guide students through each stage of their work (for example, "After completing ____, I will____," "I completed ____ and my next step is ____," or "Based on the feedback I received, I will ____").[21] These prompts exemplify procedural scaffolding by providing structured guidance that helps students organize their thoughts, reflect on progress, and plan next steps, fostering independence over time.[21]

## Metacognition Development in Middle Childhood

### Definition, Components, and Importance

Metacognition is awareness and control of thinking for learning.[14] Strong metacognitive skills have the power to impact student learning and performance.[14] While metacognition can develop over time with practice, many students struggle to meaningfully engage in metacognitive processes.[14] Students with well-developed metacognition can identify concepts they do not understand and select appropriate strategies for learning those concepts.[14] They know how to implement strategies they have selected and carry out their overall study plans.[14] They can evaluate their strategies and adjust their plans based on outcomes.[14] Metacognition allows students to be more expert-like in their thinking and more effective and efficient in their learning.[14]

Metacognition comprises three main areas in which faculty and teachers can foster students' metacognition: supporting student learning strategies (that is, study skills), encouraging monitoring and control of learning, and promoting social metacognition during group work.[14] The developmental literature indicates a trajectory from task specificity in metacognitive abilities in young children, with a unitary, domain-general metacognitive system (one that is drawn upon irrespective of task) emerging by the age of around 15 years.[17] However, this does not mean metacognition is absent in younger children—rather, it suggests that the development and integration of metacognitive capacities progresses substantially during middle childhood and beyond.

### Learning Strategies and Study Skills

Research on learning strategies reveals that students can employ different types of strategies depending on their goals and the learning context. Surface strategies are used when students have not yet gained background knowledge in an area; they can use surface strategies to acquire the necessary background knowledge, and then incorporate deep strategies to extend, connect, and apply this knowledge.[14] Importantly, surface and deep strategies can be used simultaneously for effective learning, with the use depending on what students are expected to know and be able to do, and these expectations are set by instructors.[14] Openly discussing these expectations with students can enable them to more readily select effective strategies for learning.[14]

Goal setting represents a critical metacognitive process with substantial documented impact on achievement. When students set a specific goal to identify areas of confusion each week by answering end-of-chapter questions each weekend, they are expected to do better than a student who sets a more general goal of staying up-to-date on the material.[14] Although some studies include goal setting and planning as one of many metacognitive strategies introduced to students, the influence of task-specific goal setting on academic achievement has not been extensively studied on its own in the context of elementary science courses.[14] However, research with kindergarten students examining how goal setting impacts intrinsic motivation and academic success in letter identification demonstrates that elementary students involved in setting goals showed an increase in their academic performance.[31]

### Monitoring, Control, and Confidence Calibration

Monitoring and control of learning represents another critical metacognitive process. Students often rate their confidence in their learning based on their ability to recognize, rather than recall, concepts.[14] Instead of focusing on familiarity, students should rate their confidence based on how well they can retrieve relevant information to correctly answer questions.[14] Opportunities for practicing retrieval, such as self-testing, can improve monitoring accuracy.[14] Instructors can help students monitor their understanding more accurately by encouraging students to complete practice exams and giving students feedback on their answers, perhaps in the form of a key or a class discussion.[14]

In some studies, changing answers on an exam has been shown to increase student performance, in contrast to the common belief that a student's first answer is usually right.[14] Changing answers on an exam can be beneficial if students return to questions they had low confidence in answering and make a judgment on their answers based on the ability to retrieve the information from memory, rather than a sense of familiarity with the concepts.[14] Two important open questions remain: To what extent does the ability to evaluate affect student learning and performance? When students evaluate the outcome of their studying and believe their preparation was lacking, to what degree do they adopt more effective strategies for the next exam?[14]

### Social Metacognition and Group Work

Social metacognition happens when students share ideas with peers, invite peers to evaluate their ideas, and evaluate ideas shared by peers.[14] Students also use social metacognition when they assess, modify, and enact one another's strategies for solving problems.[14] While enacting problem-solving strategies, students can evaluate their peers' hypotheses, predictions, explanations, and interpretations.[14] Importantly, metacognition and social metacognition are expected to positively affect one another.[14] While collaborating in small groups, students can also stimulate metacognition in one another, leading to improved outcomes.[14]

Research examining how metacognitive activities mediate the relationships between different avatar scaffolds on students' learning found that metacognitive scaffolding in a computer-supported learning environment can influence students' metacognitive activities, metacognitive knowledge, and domain knowledge.[7] Students receiving structuring or problematizing metacognitive scaffolds displayed more metacognitive knowledge than students in the control group.[7] Metacognitive activities mediated the effects of scaffolding, and increased metacognitive activities supported students' metacognitive knowledge.[7] Moreover, students who were engaged in proportionately more cognitive activities or fewer off-task activities also outperformed other students on the metacognitive knowledge test.[7]

### Developmental Trajectories and Age Effects

Developmental research examining metacognitive processing across the lifespan reveals important patterns relevant to elementary-aged children. A steep cognitive decline was observed in older age, while metacognitive efficiency was preserved across the lifespan.[17] Exploratory factor analysis indicated that metacognition and executive function are distinct but related constructs.[17] The best metacognitive efficiency was observed in mid-adulthood, whereas the best executive function processing reached its peak in young adulthood.[17]

For young school children, there is evidence that bilingual university students have better insight into their reading comprehension abilities compared with their monolingual peers.[17] Moreover, children who learned a second language in a formal context display an increased awareness and use of communicational strategies, and proficient multilingualism is associated with the flexible use of grammatical as well as reading strategies.[17] These findings suggest that metacognitive development is responsive to environmental inputs and that certain experiences can enhance metacognitive sophistication even in younger learners.

## Productive Struggle: Conceptualization, Implementation, and Limitations

### Defining and Contextualizing Productive Struggle

The productive struggle is a relatively new educational construct, although its theoretical underpinning can be traced back to early research on challenge and learning.[8] Currently, there are several common definitions of the productive struggle applied within mathematics education. According to the Mind Research Institute, the productive struggle is the process of effortful learning that develops grit and creative problem solving.[8] While the National Council of Teachers of Mathematics defines productive struggle as students delving more deeply into understanding the mathematical structure of problems and relationships among mathematical ideas, instead of simply seeking correct solutions.[8] Yet another definition of the productive struggle is developing strong habits of mind, such as perseverance and thinking flexibly, instead of simply seeking the correct solution.[8]

Together these definitions characterize the productive struggle as essential for deepening understanding, fostering problem-solving skills, and promoting long-term learning.[8] The productive struggle helps learners move beyond surface-level memorization and encourages them to grapple with complex concepts, thus leading to more meaningful and retained knowledge.[8] However, the absence of a consensus on the most salient considerations and expectations guiding the implementation of the productive struggle can impede progress, reduce efficacy, and ultimately generate undue frustration for students and teachers.[8]

### Key Mechanisms: Attribution, Metacognition, and Controlled Frustration

Research examining productive struggle identifies three critical psychological mechanisms that mediate successful engagement in challenging learning: attribution theory influences how students interpret their struggles, metacognition enables them to reflect and regulate their thinking, and controlled frustration can lead to meaningful learning experiences.[8] Recognizing the contributions of these constructs to understanding the productive struggle and providing appropriate guidance can help learners develop strong problem-solving skills and a positive attitude towards challenging mathematics tasks.[8]

Attribution theory in mathematics education reveals that students who attribute their success to internal factors such as effort show higher mathematics achievement compared to students who attribute their success to external factors.[8] Because perceived task difficulty and effort are important considerations for the development of the productive struggle, the effects of attribution in mathematics cannot be underestimated.[8] A key element of the productive struggle is developing "habits of mind" or ways of thinking about mathematics that resemble the process employed by mathematicians.[8] Through the productive struggle, students are encouraged to think about thinking to promote problem-solving practices akin to those implemented by mathematicians.[8]

However, habits of mind are just one of many explicit and implicit metacognitive considerations related to the productive struggle.[8] Another element of the productive struggle is having students think deeply about the mathematics, connections to prior knowledge, and explore creative solutions.[8] Undoubtedly, these processes require metacognitive tasks that have been shown to promote mathematics achievement and higher levels of problem-solving effectiveness.[8] The teacher must understand how to foster student reflection, exploration, and reconsideration of prior knowledge—all of which are highly metacognitive tasks.[8]

Regarding frustration in mathematics, academic rigor has long been a cornerstone of mathematics teaching and learning, as students are expected to persist through challenging tasks.[8] In review of the literature on frustration in mathematical problem-solving, researchers have concluded that frustration is associated with different cognitive and affective features of mathematics problem-solving, the majority of which but not all are negative.[8] Yet there are emerging arguments for the importance of the productive struggle or what some have defined as productive failure.[8] Frustration can actually be beneficial when students persevere through frustration; they develop a greater sense of accomplishment, resilience, and a deeper understanding of the content.[8] The key is to provide support and guidance during this process to prevent frustration from turning into a barrier that hinders learning.[8]

### Implementing Productive Struggle in K-12 Classrooms

An important priority in actualizing the productive struggle in classrooms is the importance of challenging students and encouraging students to struggle.[8] The importance of challenge and frustration is evidenced by the many references to effort, persistence, and perseverance within the active definitions of the productive struggle.[8] Current definitions encourage students to move past frustration through the same levels of persistence and problem-solving that define the construct itself.[8] Students come to realize that they are capable of doing well in mathematics with effort and perseverance in reasoning, sense-making, and problem-solving.[8] Appropriately, perseverance in reasoning, sense-making, and problem-solving are often what mathematics educators use to characterize productive struggle.[8]

A strategic approach to implementing productive struggle involves assigning difficult, application-based homework that correlates directly with classroom learning.[55] Instead of grading these assignments for accuracy, they should be graded for effort or completion.[55] This removes the fear of failure while still encouraging students to engage deeply with the material.[55] Imagine a high school math class where, instead of assigning a dozen repetitive practice problems, the teacher includes a few complex multi-step problems that require students to think critically.[55] When students struggle with these problems, they should be encouraged to ask questions and work through their difficulties in class.[55] The goal is not to punish students for making mistakes but to help them recognize gaps in their understanding and develop the problem-solving skills needed to overcome them.[55]

For educators concerned about limited class time, even small changes can make a difference.[55] A simple way to introduce productive struggle is to incorporate a challenge problem into existing homework assignments.[55] This doesn't require a complete overhaul of the curriculum; it just means providing students with opportunities to test their limits.[55] These questions should be difficult enough to make students think, but not so overwhelming that they become discouraged.[55] Over time, as students encounter these challenges regularly, they will build confidence in their ability to tackle difficult problems independently.[55]

### Limitations and Concerns

Despite the theoretical appeal and emerging evidence for productive struggle, important limitations and concerns exist regarding its implementation. Many studies provide a direct implementation of the productive struggle, primarily involve teachers and not students, present primarily qualitative or non-empirical research approaches, and specify the NCTM content area focus less often across studies.[23] This suggests that quantitative research examining direct causal mechanisms is limited, and many claims regarding productive struggle's effectiveness rest on theoretical rather than empirical grounds.[23]

Furthermore, research on productive failure—a closely related construct—reveals mixed findings. Several studies support engaging in direct instruction prior to problem-solving rather than productive struggle approaches. Other studies support engaging in direct instruction prior to problem-solving, with research finding no clear benefits to either approach when learning about general inquiry skills and in non-STEM domains.[20] These contradictions suggest that the effectiveness of productive struggle may be highly contextual, dependent on subject matter, task characteristics, and student characteristics not yet fully specified in the literature.[20]

## Managing Heterogeneous Ability Ranges: Differentiation, Grouping, and Personalization

### Defining Differentiation and Its Theoretical Foundations

Differentiation is a method of instruction designed to meet the needs of all students by changing what students learn (content), how they accumulate information (process), how they demonstrate knowledge or skills (product), and with whom and where learning happens (learning environment).[12] Differentiation is not an assortment of instructional recipes; rather, it is a way of thinking about teaching and learning to ensure that children receive appropriate classroom experiences.[12] Matching a required curriculum with the learning styles, expression styles, interests, and abilities of students represents the core of differentiation, predicated on the simple belief that engaged and motivated students score higher, are easier to manage, and enjoy learning more.[9]

Research and current practice illustrate the importance of differentiated instruction for engaging all students, and both research and contemporary educational theory emphasize the centrality of assessment in supporting differentiation. Teachers can differentiate curriculum for a diverse group of students by adapting and implementing differentiation in a consistent and progressive manner to meet the needs of all learners.[9] Renzulli's framework identifies five dimensions of differentiation: educators can adapt and implement differentiation through content, process, product, learning profile, and readiness dimensions, providing a systematic approach to meeting diverse student needs.[9]

Differentiation by competency, grouping, and using compacting represents one approach to operationalizing differentiation. A recent emphasis on differentiated instruction calls for the use of assessment data to support modification of curriculum and instruction to respond to differences in students' readiness, interests, and learning profile.[9] Differentiated instruction emphasizes that learning is most effective when teachers are able to assess students' current levels of functioning and learning preferences, and then use this information to help students progress to more advanced levels of functioning and more advanced learning.[9]

### Flexible Grouping and Within-Class Ability Grouping

Differentiated instruction combines flexible grouping of students with adjustments to the learning tasks; in some instances, whole group instruction is the most appropriate delivery model, while in other instances, students work in small groups or individually to complete tasks that are targeted to their own levels of readiness, interests, and learning preferences.[9] Research examining different grouping approaches reveals important findings. One meta-analysis examined studies comparing heterogeneous grouping to homogenous ability grouping and found that grouping by achievement test performance compared to heterogeneous grouping worked better for certain outcomes.[9] Another study by Tieso found that significant student achievement gains resulted when teachers used flexible within-class ability grouping.[9]

In a year-long longitudinal study, Gentry and Owen found that flexible cluster grouping had positive effects on all ability levels of students in a small rural, Midwest elementary school when accompanied by professional development.[9] This finding highlights the important moderator of teacher preparation: grouping practices are only as effective as the instructional practices that accompany them.[9] A meta-analysis on the effects of ability grouping and acceleration on K–12 students' academic achievement found that across 100 years of research, ability grouping increased achievement by 0.2 to 0.3 standard deviations, with similar benefits for students of higher and lower ability.[47] However, more recent meta-analysis conducted after 1995 concluded that ability grouping had no effect on the achievement of average students, and reduced the achievement of low-ability students by 0.2 standard deviations.[47]

### Heterogeneous Grouping and Equity Considerations

Heterogeneous grouping—placing students with varying abilities in the same learning groups—has gained substantial research support as a mechanism for promoting equity while maintaining or enhancing achievement for all students. Heterogeneous grouping has been proven to improve learning outcomes, better prepare students for an increasingly diverse workforce and society, and better prepare students as professionals.[75] Heterogeneous grouping improves cognitive skills, critical thinking, and problem-solving because students' experience with individuals different from themselves, and the novel ideas and situations that such experience brings, challenges their thinking and leads to cognitive growth.[75]

Varied achievement within student groupings positively impacts student achievement, and heterogeneous classrooms have high expectations, a faster pace of instruction, peer models of effective learning, and challenging curricula.[75] Importantly, students having difficulty at school, including students experiencing poverty, learn more when they learn in heterogeneous rather than in homogenous ability groups.[75] For students with mild cognitive disabilities and learning disabilities, specifically in reading and math, there are no additional gains in segregated settings.[75] These findings suggest that segregation based on ability does not provide the purported instructional benefits and may, in fact, disadvantage lower-performing students.

Research on ability grouping placement in kindergarten reveals important biases in how students are placed into groups. In national data predicting kindergarten ability group placement using linear and ordinal logistic regression with classroom fixed effects, test scores were the best predictors of group placement in fall, but there was bias favoring girls, high-socioeconomic status children, and Asian Americans, who received higher placements than their scores alone would predict.[44] Net of socioeconomic status, there was no bias against placing Black children in higher groups.[44] By spring, one third of kindergartners moved groups, and high-SES children moved up more than their score gains alone would predict.[44] Teacher-reported behaviors such as attentiveness and approaches to learning helped explain girls' higher placements, but did little to explain the higher placements of Asian American and high-SES children.[44]

### Curriculum Compacting and Enrichment

Another proven strategy for differentiation is curriculum compacting, a service described by Joseph Renzulli and Sally Reis, which is another process that can be widely used in both gifted and regular education programs.[9] Curriculum compacting allows advanced learners to skip specific chapters or activities and instead engage in mini-courses on research topics or small group projects as alternatives.[9] The Schoolwide Enrichment Model and its Enrichment Triad Model suggest the need for a comprehensive approach to elementary enrichment to differentiate instruction.[9] The Enrichment Triad Model is an organizational and service delivery model with three components: general exploratory activities, group training activities, and individual and small group investigations of real problems.[9]

When starting implementation of differentiation with students, it is important to do so gradually. Experiencing comprehensive student-centered instruction for the first time makes some students uneasy, and gradually integrating a student-centered curriculum by asking learners to choose between two or three assignments that can be completed before the end of class proves more effective than immediately implementing full choice-based systems.[12] Additionally, providing choice within defined parameters helps students avoid feeling overwhelmed. Students can choose how they learn with others—individually, in pairs, in small groups, or with the whole class; the difficulty levels of assignments; what content they study; what quiz questions they answer; and what, where, when, and how they learn via individual learning contracts.[12]

## Supporting Learning Across the Ability Spectrum: Executive Function, Self-Regulation, and Cognitive Load

### Executive Function Development and Self-Regulation in Early Elementary Years

Executive functions (EF) and self-regulation (SR) are fundamental for children's learning, school functioning, and academic achievement.[30] EF/SR fail to develop to their full potential if contextual stimulation is not adequately presented.[30] This is evident in the training programmes directly and exclusively targeting EF/SR stimulation, which lack durable and transferable effects.[30] Core EF and SR skills rapidly develop in preschool and continue to develop throughout childhood.[30] Given the importance of EF/SR to children's academic outcomes, social and emotional development, and its predictive value of adjustment later in life, there have been numerous attempts to improve EF/SR functioning in preschool and primary school children.[30]

Research on executive attention—one component of executive function—reveals important developmental trajectories. Executive attention development translates into school readiness and general school achievements, with studies indicating the effectiveness of executive attention training in children in these age groups.[49] Results suggest that older children benefit more from practicing specific cognitive skills, while the far transfer mechanisms of training effects seem stronger in younger children.[49] This suggests that the timing of interventions targeting executive function may produce different types of effects depending on the age of children.[49]

Working memory specifically represents a critical component of learning capacity across the ability spectrum. Working memory capacity refers to how many items you can track at once—which is not very much, even for people with exceptional working memory abilities.[52] However, this individual variation is meaningful, as people with larger working memory capacities can juggle more information, which helps them process information more quickly, with well-documented benefits.[52] People with higher-than-average working memory capacity are more likely to excel in the classroom.[52] When researchers have tracked the development of primary school children, they have found that early gains in working memory—especially in visuospatial memory—predicted later achievement in mathematics.[52]

Working memory is also predictive of language skills, like the ability to keep track of the ideas presented in a long or complex sentence.[52] On the flip side, individuals with poor working memory skills are at a disadvantage, more likely to struggle with mathematics and reading, and may also struggle with following spoken directions.[52] There is too much to juggle, and they lose track of what they are supposed to do.[52] When researchers have administered the same working memory tests across different developmental stages, they have found evidence for steady improvement, with adults performing almost twice as well as young children.[52] Overall, working memory performance shows a slow-and-steady rate of improvement from the preschool years to the age of 15.[52]

### Cognitive Load Theory and Instructional Design

Cognitive load theory provides important guidance for designing instruction that respects the limitations of working memory while promoting learning. Cognitive load theory is a theory of instructional design based on an understanding of human cognition and working memory.[69] Working memory is the small amount of information that can be held and applied to tasks, whereas long-term memory is the cumulative stored knowledge from one's life.[69] According to cognitive load theory, learning is more effective when cognitive resources are properly managed in working and long-term memory.[69]

Cognitive load theory acknowledges the limitation of working memory in both capacity and duration.[69] Learning and performance are optimized when instruction is designed according to the human cognitive architecture.[69] New information must be processed in working memory before being transferred to be stored in long-term memory.[69] When working memory is exposed to too much information or unnecessary demands, the cognitive load on working memory increases, producing cognitive overload.[69] Learners can feel confused and frustrated when overloaded, and motivation and engagement decrease.[69] However, if working memory is properly managed, there is sufficient capacity for learning to be productive and enjoyable.[69]

Cognitive load theory conceptualizes cognitive load during learning into three elements: intrinsic cognitive load is the task or material's inherent complexity, the rate of information flow, and the learner's expertise and prior knowledge (this load is necessary for learning and should be optimized by responding to and adjusting the difficulty level of the learning content); extraneous cognitive load is the cognitive load that results from how the information is presented (this should be minimized); and germane load is the cognitive load that is beneficial and supports learning.[69] In practice, the more working memory the learner must devote to extraneous load because of poor instructional design, the fewer cognitive resources are available to deal with the intrinsic load of the material, reducing learning.[69]

## Fostering Intrinsic Motivation and Student Agency

### Self-Determination Theory and Psychological Needs

Self-determination theory assumes that inherent in human nature is the propensity to be curious about one's environment and interested in learning about it.[25] The theory posits that goal-directed behaviors are driven by three innate psychological needs: autonomy (the need to feel ownership of one's behavior), competence (the need to produce desired outcomes), and relatedness (the need to feel connected to others).[25] When these three psychological needs are satisfied, intrinsic motivation flourishes.[25] A large corpus of empirical evidence based on self-determination theory suggests that both intrinsic motivation and autonomous types of extrinsic motivation are conducive to engagement and optimal learning in educational contexts.[25]

Teachers' support of students' basic psychological needs for autonomy, competence, and relatedness facilitates students' autonomous self-regulation for learning, academic performance, and well-being.[25] The need for autonomy refers to the experience of behavior as volitional and reflectively self-endorsed, with students being autonomous when they willingly devote time and energy to their studies.[25] The need for competence refers to the experience of behavior as effectively enacted, with students being competent when they feel able to meet the challenges of their schoolwork.[25] Importantly, satisfaction of both autonomy and competence needs is essential to maintain intrinsic motivation, contrary to what is hypothesized by self-efficacy theory, which denies functional significance to autonomy.[25] Therefore, students who feel competent but not autonomous will not maintain intrinsic motivation for learning.[25]

Students' autonomy can be supported by teachers' minimizing the salience of evaluative pressure and any sense of coercion in the classroom, as well as providing relevant information on how to master the tasks at hand.[25] In addition to the needs for autonomy and competence, self-determination theory posits that satisfaction of the need for relatedness facilitates the process of internalization.[25] People tend to internalize and accept as their own the values and practices of others in contexts that satisfy the need for relatedness, meaning that they feel cared for and supported.[25] In classroom contexts that support satisfaction of autonomy, competence, and relatedness, students tend to be more intrinsically motivated and more willing to engage in less interesting tasks, and to value academic activities.[25]

### Student Agency and Choice

Student agency and choice refer to students' ability to make decisions about their learning experiences and take ownership of their educational journey.[51] Giving learners choice, even basic decisional choices, satisfies a basic psychological need for autonomy and self-endorsed decisions, leading to increased engagement and information retention.[51] Students who feel a strong sense of agency persist longer and are more likely to deliberately use the available scaffolds when encountering difficulty.[51] Effective student choice must be relevant to the student's personal interests, at an appropriate level of challenge, and offered in a manageable number to avoid overwhelming the student.[51] By considering the principles of self-determination theory, educators can create learning environments that nurture intrinsic motivation, autonomy, competence, and relatedness and foster students' holistic development and long-term success.[51]

A study investigating the effects of choice in a reading comprehension task hypothesized that choice would act as a trigger for situational interest, impacting engagement with the reading text, and would therefore improve children's performance in a reading comprehension task, and promote higher levels of enjoyment for that task.[54] Participants were 110 Grade 3 pupils (61 boys, 49 girls).[54] Reading comprehension performance and task enjoyment were measured in a cross-over, repeated measures design where children were either allocated a short story or offered a perceived choice of story to read.[54] In fact, all children read the same story in each condition.[54] Choice was found to significantly affect comprehension scores (Cohen's d = 0.52) and reported task enjoyment (Cohen's d = 0.23), indicating that choice impacts engagement with a reading text.[54]

### Classroom Climate and Emotional Safety

Classroom emotional climate refers to the global classroom atmosphere and the degree to which the classroom as a whole functions smoothly and harmoniously and is characterized by interactions with a positive tone or, conversely, by frequent disruption, conflict, and disorganization.[56] Thus, positive as well as negative interactions (for example, excitement and humor versus conflict) contribute to climate.[56] Because emotional climate is an evaluation of the classroom as a whole, the teacher's behavior, students' responses to the teacher, the teacher's response to students, and interactions among students all contribute to emotional climate.[56] Classroom emotional climate is more than the sum of evaluations of single actors and includes evaluations that refer explicitly to group functioning (for example, the classroom functions as a well-oiled machine).[56]

American elementary school classrooms have been shown to vary considerably in emotional climate.[56] Evidence suggests that negative emotional climate is detrimental to children's concurrent psychosocial adjustment.[56] Children in first-grade classrooms with negative versus more positive emotional climate demonstrated more concurrent maternally-rated internalizing symptoms.[56] For children with preexisting vulnerability to social anxiety, relative to other children, they may be more strongly affected by potentially anxiety-provoking classroom contexts.[56] The research supports a child-by-environment model of children's social and emotional adjustment, where individual characteristics interact with classroom contexts to shape outcomes.[56]

## Spaced Learning and Transfer of Knowledge

### The Spacing Effect and Generalization

The spacing effect describes the robust finding that long-term learning is promoted when learning events are spaced out in time, rather than presented in a massed format.[60] Research examining the spacing effect in children reveals that spacing lessons out in time resulted in higher generalization performance for both simple and complex concepts.[60] Spaced learning schedules promote several types of learning, strengthening the implications of the spacing effect for educational practices and curriculum.[60]

A particular strength of spaced learning lies in its promotion of complex generalization. By one account, spaced learning should promote generalization by supporting the abstraction of relevant and irrelevant features.[60] Spaced learning provides time in between learning presentations for learners to forget irrelevant information. However, relevant features are likely to be present on subsequent learning presentations, reactivated in memory, and thus be forgotten to a lesser degree.[60] Consequently, when the learner is required to make a generalization at a later point in time, the learner will remember relevant features and thus generalize based upon these characteristics.[60] In the case of complex generalization, perceptual features are likely to be forgotten, whereas the abstract structure is likely to be remembered to a greater degree.[60]

Results from studies examining the spacing effect revealed that spacing educational lessons apart in time promoted both simple and complex generalization, with benefits present one week after the last lesson.[60] This is the first study to demonstrate that spaced learning promotes complex generalization.[60] Thus, these results have several implications for theory and research: spaced learning is not constrained to memory for specific information, such as facts or lists of words, but instead spaced learning also promotes the acquisition and generalization of educational concepts.[60] This is important because a primary goal of education is to foster the generalization of knowledge outside of the context in which it is learned.[60]

### Teaching for Transfer

Understanding how students transfer learning from one context to another represents a critical educational goal. An ambitious national effort called Reading for Understanding supported six stellar project teams as they designed and tested interventions to improve students' reading comprehension.[57] In a recent summary of findings from the initiative, researchers concluded that, while elementary-grade students enjoyed gains on outcomes that were closely tied to the intervention activities, these gains did not translate into better performance on standardized reading comprehension tests.[57] In other words, there was no evidence that students were able to apply—or transfer—what they had learned from these interventions to other reading tasks.[57]

If a fundamental challenge of reading comprehension is that readers need background knowledge to understand and learn from text, then a fundamental aim of instruction must be to help students build knowledge.[57] Building background knowledge through schemas enables transfer. A robust schema serves as the foundation for transfer, as demonstrated by the example that having an easily accessible schema about team sports might help someone follow a cricket match that shares certain structural similarities.[57] For young children to become proficient readers, they must not only master the big five reading skills but also develop schemas and learn how to tap into relevant schemas as they read about new topics.[57]

One intervention called the Model of Reading Engagement (MORE) was designed to fit into a school's content (science/social studies) block, including thematic lessons that build background knowledge and reading engagement in grades 1-3 for the purpose of promoting and measuring transfer in reading comprehension.[57] Researchers developed thematic lessons meant to introduce students to a handful of schemas to help them read and understand texts on various topics that branch out from them.[57] After the grade 2 lessons, researchers found clear evidence that children were able to read about and understand near-transfer and mid-transfer texts on topics like ammonites and Pompeii.[57] Importantly, researchers found that both low- and high-performing children enjoyed similar gains on the transfer passages, and they have replicated their findings across many studies.[57]

## Building Resilience, Growth Mindset, and Attribution Patterns

### Attribution Theory and Effort-Based Success

Attribution theory examines how people interpret the causes of success and failure, with significant implications for academic motivation and persistence.[73] How a person describes her or his current successes and failures can predict future academic success.[73] Three key attributional dimensions include locus of control (whether the cause is internal or external), stability (whether the cause is stable or changeable), and controllability (whether the cause is controllable or uncontrollable).[73]

If you attribute a mark to your ability, then the source of success is relatively stable—by definition, ability is a relatively lasting quality.[73] If you attribute a top mark to the effort you put into studying, then the source of success is unstable—effort can vary and has to be renewed on each occasion, or else it disappears.[73] The controllability of attribution is the extent to which the individual can influence it.[73] If you attribute a top mark to your effort at studying, then the source of success is relatively controllable—you can influence effort simply by deciding how much to study.[73] But if you attribute the mark to simple luck, then the source of success is uncontrollable—there is nothing that can influence random chance.[73]

It usually helps both motivation and achievement if a student attributes academic successes and failures to factors that are internal and controllable, such as effort or a choice to use particular learning strategies.[76] The worst outcome for academic motivation is attributions, whether stable or not, related to external factors.[76] Believing that performance depends simply on luck or on the excessive difficulty of material removes the incentive for a student to invest in learning.[76] Teachers can influence students' attributions by framing their own explanations of success and failure around internal, controllable factors.[76] Instead of telling a student "Good work! You're smart!" try saying "Good work! Your effort really made a difference, didn't it?"[76] If a student fails, instead of saying "Too bad! This material is just too hard for you," try saying "Let's find a strategy for practicing this more, and then you can try again."[76]

### Growth Mindset and Incremental Beliefs About Intelligence

A growth mindset is the belief that intelligence and abilities are not fixed but can be developed through effort, practice, and learning from feedback.[79] In contrast, a fixed mindset assumes that abilities are innate and unchangeable.[79] Students with a growth mindset believe that intelligence is malleable and can be developed, believe in the power of effort, and see challenges as learning opportunities.[79] They stretch themselves instead of giving up and develop new strategies, while students with fixed mindsets view mistakes as failure and avoid challenges out of fear of appearing unintelligent.[79]

Research examining the effectiveness of growth mindset interventions found that growth mindset interventions on average can improve academic performance of students.[79] A growth mindset intervention has the potential to increase students' academic performance by shaping their belief in their ability and intelligence and influencing their motivation and willingness to persist when tasks increase in difficulty.[79] Students with a growth mindset have a greater likelihood of higher grades in mathematics and language disciplines, as well as in general GPA, compared to students with fixed mindsets.[79]

A key process through which growth mindset affects learning involves students' interpretation of challenges and setbacks. Researchers have found that compared to students with a fixed mindset, students with a growth mindset have higher grades in mathematics and language disciplines, as well as in general GPA.[79] Importantly, ability—usually considered a relatively stable factor—often actually changes incrementally over the long term.[76] Recognizing this fact is one of the best ways to bring about actual increases in students' abilities.[76] A middle-years student who plays the trumpet in the school band at a high level of ability may actually reflect a lot of previous effort and a gradual increase in ability.[76]

### Building Resilience Through Deliberate Practice

Resilience is the ability to spring back when one experiences failure, roadblocks, and hurdles that impede progress on the path to successful learning within the classroom.[58] Teachers can promote resilience on a regular basis so that students have inner resources when they become frustrated.[58] Think of resilience as a stress ball: a stress ball is resilient because it springs back to its original shape after being squeezed, and likewise when students experience stress or frustration, we can think of that as pressure on them that they need to spring back from.[58]

A strategic approach to relieving mental tension while building resilience is to infuse students with a productive mindset known as "Habits of Mind."[58] These strategies serve as a pathway to finding a solution when one is not immediately apparent.[58] They promote insightfulness, creativity, and perseverance—all dispositions schools should want resilient students to have.[58] Teachers can cultivate a resilient classroom where students are given a chance to work through difficult problems, as experiencing failures, challenges, and roadblocks within the learning environment is crucial to the process of recovery.[58]

Four specific ways to foster the Habits of Mind and Resilience include teaching persistence with brainteasers, which allow safe and friendly opportunities for building resilience and persisting; guiding students to develop metacognition by explicitly guiding them to develop a feel for their capacity to recover; fostering optimism in students' ability to take responsible risks by complimenting them when they take risks and don't get the best result; and using wait time to manage impulsivity, where implementing a five-second pause after asking a question and before selecting anyone to answer relieves pressure.[58]

## Teacher Professional Development and Implementation Quality

### Features of Effective Professional Development

A comprehensive review of 35 methodologically rigorous studies identified seven widely shared features of effective professional development.[71] Such professional development is content focused, with PD that focuses on teaching strategies associated with specific curriculum content supporting teacher learning within teachers' classroom contexts.[71] This element includes an intentional focus on discipline-specific curriculum development and pedagogies in areas such as mathematics, science, or literacy.[71]

Additionally, well-designed professional development includes active learning experiences, moving away from traditional learning models that are generic and lecture-based toward models that engage teachers directly in the practices they are learning and, preferably, are connected to teachers' classrooms and students.[71] Professional development that utilizes models of effective practice has proven successful at promoting teacher learning and supporting student achievement.[71] Curricular and instructional models and modeling of instruction help teachers to have a vision of practice on which to anchor their own learning and growth.[71] The various kinds of modeling can include video or written cases of teaching, live demonstrations by expert teachers, or simulations.[71]

Furthermore, effective professional development includes coaching and feedback from expert facilitators, sustained duration of teacher and leader participation (research suggests that at least two years of participation in professional development produces meaningful effects), and opportunities for teachers to collaborate with colleagues and reflect on their practice.[71] A fellowship model for professional development designed to provide teachers, coaches, and principals with ongoing, collaborative, job-embedded professional development significantly increased student proficiency rates in English language arts and math on state achievement exams, with positive impact concentrated in the first cohort.[68] The positive impact was concentrated in schools that maintained more sustained duration of teacher and leader participation in professional development.[68]

### The Role of Content Knowledge and Pedagogical Content Knowledge

An important finding from professional development research is that the knowledge teachers bring to professional development—and whether it is sufficient to support their learning of particular pedagogical strategies—significantly influences outcomes. In one interesting case where mathematics professional development was conducted in a district that had very large numbers of uncredentialed teachers, researchers found positive effects on student learning only when teachers attended both the content and pedagogical components of the professional development program.[71] Teachers who participated in comprehensive professional development had students who achieved greater learning gains than comparison students whose teachers received content training only.[71]

## Conclusion: Synthesizing Evidence and Identifying Priorities for Implementation

The research base examining how children ages 6-9 develop capacity for self-directed learning, engage in mastery-based progression systems, benefit from mixed-age learning environments, respond to scaffolding strategies, develop metacognitive capabilities, experience productive struggle, and navigate heterogeneous ability groupings reveals consistent evidence for the positive impacts of carefully designed and implemented educational approaches. The convergence of findings across multiple research traditions—educational psychology, cognitive science, and empirical studies of classroom practice—suggests that these developmental capacities are neither fixed nor immutable but rather responsive to thoughtfully designed educational experiences.

Self-directed learning capacity emerges through systematic scaffolding that gradually transfers responsibility from teacher to student, with particular attention to explicit instruction in goal-setting, resource identification, and reflective evaluation. Mastery-based learning systems, supported by meta-analyses documenting effect sizes averaging 0.5 to 0.59 standard deviations, demonstrate consistent benefits for academic achievement while also promoting enhanced self-efficacy, growth mindset orientation, and intrinsic motivation. Mixed-age learning environments, when paired with teacher preparation focused on responsive pedagogy and differentiated instruction, produce positive social-emotional and academic outcomes for students across the ability spectrum.

Metacognitive development in the 6-9 age range is responsive to instruction emphasizing planning, monitoring, control, and social metacognition, with evidence that this development supports self-regulated learning and academic achievement. Productive struggle, despite theoretical promise and emerging supportive evidence, requires careful calibration of task difficulty, provision of strategic scaffolding, and cultivation of growth-oriented attributions to prevent frustration from becoming counterproductive. The management of heterogeneous ability ranges is most effective when employing flexible within-class grouping paired with differentiated instruction, with evidence suggesting that heterogeneous grouping supports learning for all students while advancing equity goals.

Several cross-cutting themes emerge from this synthesis: the centrality of high-quality classroom emotional climate and positive student-teacher relationships as the foundation for all more specific interventions; the necessity of responsive, contingent teacher support that adjusts dynamically rather than following static protocols; the importance of student agency and choice within structured parameters in supporting intrinsic motivation; and the critical role of deliberate, sustained teacher professional development focused on content and pedagogy.

For schools and educators implementing these approaches, several priorities warrant emphasis. First, develop comprehensive, school-wide approaches to building self-directed learning capacity across grade levels rather than leaving these skills to chance or isolated teachers. Second, combine mastery-based assessment systems with quality formative feedback and responsive instruction to move beyond assessment-for-sorting toward assessment-for-learning. Third, when implementing mixed-age groupings or any heterogeneous grouping, ensure that teachers receive substantial professional development in differentiation, responsive scaffolding, and management of diverse learning needs.

Fourth, position metacognitive development and metacognitive scaffolding as explicit instructional priorities rather than assuming these capacities will develop incidentally. Fifth, implement productive struggle strategically with appropriate scaffolding, monitoring of frustra

tion levels, and cultivation of growth-oriented beliefs about intelligence and effort. Sixth, implement flexible within-class grouping that responds to formative assessment data and allows for student mobility rather than static, permanent tracks that perpetuate inequity.

Finally, recognize that implementation quality—particularly the quality of teacher professional development, the responsiveness of instructional supports, and the warmth and emotional safety of classroom environments—matters substantially in determining whether structural innovations translate into genuine learning benefits. These elements working in concert create conditions where children ages 6-9 can develop as capable, confident, self-directed learners with increasing agency over their educational journey and expanding capacity to engage with challenging content through productive struggle, metacognitive awareness, and growth-oriented resilience.
